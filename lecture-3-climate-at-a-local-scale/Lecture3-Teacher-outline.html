Climate at a local scale — lecture outline

A running order for the session, with the point to make at each step, the number to put on the board, which interactive page to open, and the question to throw back at the room. Built from Lecture 3 — Climate at a Local Scale and the four teaching pages that go with it.

The one sentence the whole session is built on

The same sunlight falls on every surface. What happens next — how warm it gets, how much water it evaporates, how deep the heat penetrates, how cold it goes at night — is decided by the properties of the surface, not by the radiation arriving.

Everything else is an elaboration of that. Each section takes one surface property (albedo, water availability, thermal admittance, roughness, vegetation structure) and shows which term of the budget it controls.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the session students should be able to:

  • Write down the radiative budget and the energy balance of a surface and say which quantity joins them.
  • State the sign convention for each term and predict how each one flips between day and night.
  • Use the bulk formulas for FSH and FLH, and say what each of the three factors does.
  • Interpret a Bowen ratio, including a negative one, and explain what it says about the surface.
  • Explain the oasis effect and why FLH can exceed FNR.
  • Use the damping depth to predict amplitude decay and phase lag with depth.
  • Explain why a desert surface reaches extremes that a moist soil never does.
  • Predict when the surface layer is convectively stable or unstable, and why.
  • Explain why albedo depends on solar altitude and on canopy structure.
  • Describe how radiation, temperature, humidity, wind and CO2 vary inside a canopy.
  • Given a land-cover change, reason qualitatively about the change in the local energy balance.

Running order

Timings assume the 12:15–16:00 slot with two short breaks. They are a shape, not a schedule — block 2 is the one to protect, and block 4 is the one to cut into if you run long.

Framing: what changes when you zoom in

12:15 · 10 min

Points

  • Last session was the planetary budget: one number for the whole Earth, 240 W m−2 in and out.
  • Today: stand on one square metre. Ask what the numbers look like there. They look nothing like the global average, and two patches a fence apart can differ enormously.
  • Set up the idealised site the whole first half assumes: horizontal, homogeneous, extensive, cloudless; a flat moist soil under grass. Say explicitly that we relax each of these later — the oasis breaks “extensive”, the forest breaks “homogeneous”.
  • Outline the six sections: radiative budget → energy balance → soil moisture → the oasis effect → snow and ice → vegetation.
Warm-up
Why is a car park hotter than the lawn beside it on the same afternoon? Take three or four answers and write them on the board without judging them. Nearly every mechanism in the session will appear in that list — colour, water, heat storage, roughness. Come back to the board at the end.
Block 1

The radiative budget

12:25 · 35 min

1 · The equation

  • FNR = Fsolar − αFsolar + FIR↓ − FIR↑  [W m−2]
  • Four terms, two shortwave and two longwave. Go through them one at a time on the Fig. 2.20 diagram.
  • Sign convention — spend a moment here, it pays for itself all afternoon. Radiative terms positive towards the surface; turbulent and conduction terms positive away from it.
  • FNR is not an output. It is the energy the surface has left to spend. That framing carries straight into the next block.

2 · Worked example — short grass, Matador, 30 July 1971

  • Put the five numbers on the board: Fsolar 27.3, reflected −4.6, FIR↓ 27.5, FIR↑ −36.8, giving FNR = 13.4 MJ m−2 day−1.
  • Net shortwave +22.7, net longwave −9.3. Half the day’s gain is given straight back in the infrared.
  • The point to land: the two longwave terms are individually enormous — both larger than the reflected sunlight — and they very nearly cancel. The budget is a small difference between two large numbers, which is exactly why small changes in either one matter so much.
  • Albedo here is only 0.16, so reflection is a minor term. Foreshadow: that will not be true of the next four surfaces.
Show
Page 1 · Where the energy goes at the surface — select Grass. Use the scaled-arrow diagram to make the “two big longwave arrows nearly cancel” point visually, then the waterfall to walk the running total down term by term.

3 · Change the surface, keep the Sun

  • Step through the sites without changing anything else. Suggested order: grass → moist soil → desert → snow.
  • Desert: the most sunlight of any site (30.0) and only 9.0 MJ of net radiation. Albedo 0.30 throws away a third; the scorching surface radiates 39 MJ back out.
  • Snow, Antarctica, 21 hours of daylight: FNR = −1.0. A whole sunlit day that ends in deficit. This is usually the moment the room sits up.
  • Draw the conclusion out loud: albedo is the single most powerful lever a surface has, and it acts before anything else in the chain can.
Show
The live explorer — set the site to 45°N and step through the six surface presets. Same solar geometry, six different budgets. This is the clearest single demonstration of the session’s core sentence. Must be opened from the web address, not from a file on disk — check it loads before the lecture.
Ask
The Antarctic snow gets more than 26 MJ of sunlight in a day. Why does it end the day colder? Because 80 % is reflected before it can be absorbed, while FIR↑ continues all 24 hours regardless. Follow up: what would happen if it were dusted with soot?

break · 13:00

Block 2

The energy balance and the turbulent fluxes

13:15 · 45 min  ·  the core of the session

1 · Spending the net radiation

  • FNR = FSH + FLH + Fcond + heat storage
  • Same FNR as the last equation — write them one above the other so the join is obvious.
  • Name the terms physically: heating the air, evaporating water, conducting into the ground, warming the surface layer itself.
  • Worked example, Agassiz, 30 May 1978, moist bare soil: FNR 18.0 = FSH 2.3 + FLH 13.4 + Fcond 2.3. Three quarters of it goes into evaporation.
Ask (from the slides)
Why does the surface record both the highest and the lowest temperature of the day — not the air at 1.2 m, not the soil at 0.2 m? Because the surface is the only place the radiation is actually absorbed and emitted. Everything else is heated or cooled second-hand. By day heat flows away from it in every direction; by night it flows towards it from every direction.

2 · The bulk formulas

  • FSH = ρ cp ch Ua (Ts − Ta)   and   FLH = ρ Lv cL Ua (qs − qa)
  • Identical structure — say this explicitly. A transfer efficiency, times the wind, times a difference between the surface and the air.
  • All three factors must be non-zero. No wind, no flux however hot the surface. No gradient, no flux however hard the wind blows.
  • ch is the surface’s aerodynamic grip: the roughness length in disguise. A forest moves several hundred W m−2 on a light breeze; a lake cannot.
Show
Page 2 · Sensible and latent heat — load the Desert, noon preset, then Calm, no wind, and let the class watch both fluxes collapse together while nothing else changed.

3 · The Bowen ratio

  • Bo = FSH / FLH — how the surface splits its turbulent loss.
  • Bo > 1: more energy than water. Desert ≈ 37, pine forest 1.67. Bo < 1: barley 0.02, moist soil 0.17, fir forest 0.48.
  • The result worth deriving on the board. Divide the two formulas. If ch = cL, then ρ, c and Ua all cancel:
    Bo = (cp/Lv) · ΔT/Δq ≈ 0.40 · ΔT [K] / Δq [g kg−1]
  • So the Bowen ratio is independent of wind speed and of roughness. Blow harder and both fluxes grow together. The split is set only by the ratio of the two gradients — i.e. by how much water the surface can supply.
  • Pine vs fir is the case that proves it: same latitude, same month, albedo 0.08 and 0.09, and opposite balances. Nothing radiative explains it.
Show
Page 2 — move the wind and transfer coefficient sliders and let the class watch Bo refuse to move. Then move Δq and watch it swing.
Ask
A surface has ΔT = 8 K. How large must Δq be before evaporation takes more energy than heating the air? Bo = 1 needs Δq ≈ 0.40 × 8 ≈ 3.2 g kg−1. Then: which surfaces can supply that, and which cannot?

4 · Heat storage, briefly

  • heat storage = ρ cground · hsu · δTs/δt
  • Usually the smallest term over vegetation, and the reason cities stay warm at night.
  • The teaching point is the product: a thin layer with a low heat capacity changes temperature violently for a small energy input. Park that thought — block 3 pays it off.
Block 3

Into the ground, and the effect of water

14:00 · 45 min

1 · Which way does Fcond point?

  • Draw the vertical profile through the soil–air interface, day and night, on the same axes.
  • Day: surface warmest, soil cooler below, air cooler above. Fcond downward, FSH upward, convectively unstable.
  • Night: surface coldest. Soil warmer below, air warmer above — an inversion. Fcond upward, FSH downward, convectively stable, turbulence suppressed.
  • Stress that the reversal happens because the surface leads and everything else follows.

2 · Temperature waves in the soil

  • T(z,t) = T̄ + A0 e−z/D sin(ωt − z/D) with D = √(2κ/ω)
  • The same D appears twice, and that is the whole story: the wave loses 63 % of its amplitude per damping depth and falls one radian (≈ 3.8 h) further behind in the same distance. Weaker and later, locked together.
  • Daily wave in dry sand: D ≈ 8 cm. Annual wave: √365 ≈ 19 times deeper, so metres rather than centimetres, and the lag runs to months.
  • Wet soil conducts far better than dry. Water displaces still air in the pores; k rises faster than C, so κ rises. Saturating sandy soil roughly doubles the damping depth — the heat spreads through twice as much material and the surface swing is correspondingly gentler.
Show
Page 3 · Heat in the ground — the depth–time heatmap makes the tilt (the phase lag) visible in one glance. Switch dry sand → saturated sand and let them watch the wave reach deeper.

3 · The Sahara, which ties it together

  • Surface swings from 27 °C to 64 °C in a day. Air at 2 m swings only about 11 K. Sand at 0.30 m: no diurnal signal at all.
  • Explain it with the damping depth: 0.30 m is nearly four D in dry sand, so 2 % of the amplitude survives. All the day’s energy is trapped in a layer a few centimetres thick — and a thin layer with a low heat capacity has nowhere to put it but temperature.
  • At night the same thinness works in reverse: almost no stored heat to draw on, so radiative cooling drops the surface 35 K before dawn.
  • Note the low emissivity too — desert sand is 0.84–0.91, among the lowest of any natural surface.
Ask (from the slides)
When is the air convectively stable, and when unstable? Read it off the crossing of the surface and 2 m curves: unstable roughly 07:00–19:00 when the surface is hotter; stable overnight when it is colder. Follow up: what does that do to the depth of the boundary layer?

4 · Soil moisture and the boundary layer

  • Wet soil: energy goes to FLH, the surface stays cool, the boundary layer stays shallow and humid.
  • Dry soil: energy goes to FSH, the surface runs hot, the boundary layer grows deep and dry.
  • Same FNR, completely different atmosphere above it. This is the land–atmosphere coupling that PC Lab 3 explores in the Sahara.

5 · The oasis effect

  • A small moist patch inside a dry landscape: an oasis, an irrigated field, a city park in August.
  • Evaporation keeps the patch cooler than the hot dry air arriving from upwind, so Ts − Ta goes negative, FSH reverses, and the air starts delivering heat to the surface.
  • The surface now has two supplies — radiation and advection — so FLH can exceed FNR, and Bo is negative. Measured irrigated field: FNR 16, FLH 21, FSH −6.
  • It only works because the patch is small. Irrigate the whole desert and the upwind air turns cool and humid, the advection stops, the effect vanishes. Genuinely local — it cannot appear in a global budget, because globally there is no upwind.
Show
Page 2 · the oasis panel, or load the Irrigated field preset and point at the negative Bo in the readout.

6 · Snow and ice

  • The balance now applies to a volume, not a surface: FNR + Frain = FLH + FSH + Fcond + ΔSSH + ΔSLH
  • ΔSLH carries melting and freezing — the term that has no counterpart over soil, and the one that matters most.
  • Cold pack in winter vs wet pack in spring: contrast the two.
  • Above the Arctic circle in winter the budget simplifies drastically — no sunlight, Frain zero, FLH and Fcond negligible.
  • Snow insulates. k ≈ 0.08 W m−1 K−1 for fresh snow, some thirty-five times lower than rock. The snow surface can sit at −30 °C while the soil beneath stays near 0 °C. Say plainly why that matters for plants, soil microbes and small mammals — a winter with little snow can be harder on an ecosystem than a colder one with deep snow.

Block 4

Vegetation and surface properties

15:00 · 45 min

1 · Albedo and emissivity across all surfaces

  • Walk Table 1.1: albedo spans 0.03 (calm water, high Sun) to 0.95 (fresh snow) — a factor of thirty. Emissivity spans only 0.71 to 0.99, and almost everything living sits above 0.95.
  • That asymmetry is the headline. In the longwave, nature is close to a blackbody and differences barely matter. In the shortwave the surface has enormous leverage. Which is why land-cover change is such a powerful climate forcing.
  • Emissivity = absorptivity (Kirchhoff, same wavelength). So ε does double duty: it sets FIR↑ through εσTs4, and it sets how much of FIR↓ the surface takes in.
  • The two natural exceptions worth remembering: desert sand and old snow, both around 0.84–0.89.
Show
Page 4 · What the surface does to the radiation — the range chart, then hit Sort by emissivity to make the “narrow range” point land.

2 · Albedo is not a constant

  • Albedo rises steeply at low solar altitude (large zenith angle): glancing incidence gives stronger Fresnel reflection off waxy leaves, and the beam penetrates less deeply so has fewer chances to be trapped.
  • Steepness is the structural signature. Short grass is nearly flat with angle; pine falls from 0.20 to 0.08 across the same range, because a tall open conifer canopy is full of gaps that swallow a high Sun and barely exist for a low one.
  • Consequence: the same forest has a measurably higher daily-mean albedo in winter at high latitude, before any snow is involved.
Ask
The bare oak canopy has a lower albedo than the same oak in leaf, at every solar altitude. Why does putting leaves on a tree make it brighter? Leads into multiple reflection: it is the structure that traps light, not the optical properties of the individual element.

3 · Why a canopy is darker than its own leaves

  • A single leaf reflects 10–15 % of visible light. A closed canopy of the same leaves reflects less, because a reflected photon usually hits another leaf rather than escaping, and each encounter takes another bite.
  • Hence the rule: taller vegetation generally has a lower albedo. Grass 0.16–0.26, crops 0.18–0.25, deciduous forest 0.15–0.20, conifers 0.05–0.15.
  • This is a real climate feedback, not a curiosity. Boreal conifer forest is among the darkest natural surfaces on Earth; the tundra it is advancing into is among the brightest in winter, because tundra is buried under snow while trees stand above it. Shrubs and trees moving north warm the local climate directly through albedo — separately from any carbon they store.

4 · Inside the canopy

  • Radiation is absorbed layer by layer: F(L) = F0 e−kL, controlled by cumulative leaf area, not by height.
  • Net radiation follows Fsolar closely inside the canopy, because longwave is nearly in balance between neighbouring leaves.
  • The diffuse share rises from about 15 % above the canopy to about 46 % at the forest floor — direct beam needs a clear line through every layer; diffuse light is scattered rather than blocked.
  • The active surface is not the ground. In a tall stand it sits several metres up in the crown, where the absorption happens.
  • The canopy microclimate follows from that: T and vapour pressure bulge at the foliage-density peak; wind collapses to near zero inside and is strong above; CO2 is highest at the ground where soil respiration releases it.
Show
Page 4 — set the canopy height slider to 0.2 m and read it as the grass stand from the slides, then back up to 12 m for a forest. Then the four spruce profiles.
Ask (from the slides)
What would those four profiles look like in a deciduous forest in winter? Take them one at a time: foliage density first, then ask what each of the others depends on. It is a good test of whether the mechanism landed or only the picture did.

5 · Two forests, side by side

  • Barley field: α 0.24; evaporative cooling keeps the canopy cool, suppressing FIR↑, while humid air radiates strongly down — so net longwave loss is unusually small. FLH takes 99 % of FNR.
  • Pine, Thetford 52°N: α 0.08, about 60 % of FNR to FSH, Bo = 1.67.
  • Fir, Haney 49°N: α 0.09, about 68 % to FLH, Bo = 0.48. FSH peaks before FNR; FLH peaks after.
  • Return to the slides’ own question: what causes the difference between the pine and the fir? Water availability, not radiation. This is the single best summary of the whole session.

Pulling it together

15:45 · 15 min

The summary that matters

  • Go back to the board from the warm-up and tick off the mechanisms the class guessed. Nearly all of them will now have a name and a term in the budget.
  • The local budget depends on surface characteristics, and each one owns a term:

Bridge to the next session. Every one of these is a property of the surface, not of the atmosphere. So changing land cover changes climate without changing anything about the incoming radiation — which is exactly the mechanism PC Lab 1 (Daisyworld) and PC Lab 3 (abrupt vegetation change in the Sahara) put into a model.
