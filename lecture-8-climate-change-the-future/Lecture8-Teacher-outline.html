Climate change: the future

A running order for the single slot the topic occupies — Thursday 17 September, 08:15–12:00, room 4-311B, followed by PC Lab 3 in the afternoon. Seven public pages, four hours, and one arithmetic result that everything converges on.

The one sentence

Say it in the first ten minutes and land it five times: the physics of the future is better understood than the future itself, because the largest uncertainty is a choice nobody has made yet — and the one number that survives every pathway is the total amount of carbon.

The spine. Seven pages, arranged from the assumption outwards: scenario → uncertainty → where → water → tails → budget → commitment. The first two are about what kind of statement a projection is; the middle three are the physics of the response; the last two are the two things that make this lecture different from all the others — a hard budget, and irreversibility.

The debt from yesterday. Lecture 7 ended with the attribution regression and σglobal = 0.11 K. Open by saying: yesterday we established that the warming is ours. Today we ask what happens next — and the very first thing we discover is that the question is not the same kind of question. Also owed from Lecture 6: ECS ≈ 3 K, ΔF = 5.35 ln(C/C0), and the slab-ocean transient. All three are used in the first demonstration.

The trap to head off in the first five minutes. “So what do the models predict for 2100?” Refuse the question once, deliberately and pleasantly, and explain why — then give them the conditional version. If you do not do this at the start you will spend the whole morning doing it piecemeal.

The second trap. Students arrive with a background belief that this material is either catastrophic or exaggerated, and both camps are listening for confirmation. The defence is the same in both directions: every number in this lecture is computed on screen and can be checked. Use page 5’s AR6 comparison table for this explicitly — a Gaussian shift reproducing all eight assessed multipliers is the single most persuasive thing in the morning, for both camps.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the session students should be able to:

  • State the difference between a projection and a prediction, and say which is an initial-value and which a boundary-value problem.
  • Explain why a scenario carries no probability, and what it therefore means for a projection to be “wrong”.
  • Name the four links of the emissions-to-response chain and the kind of model that makes each.
  • Decode an RCP and an SSP name.
  • Say why the aerosol forcing declines in every scenario, and why that adds warming.
  • Explain why RCP8.5 should not be called “business as usual”, and why it is still run.
  • Decompose projection uncertainty into internal, model and scenario components.
  • Say which of the three dominates for a given region, averaging period and lead time — and why the answer changes.
  • Explain why decadal predictability is limited to a few years, and what sets the limit.
  • Interpret hatching and stippling on an IPCC change map.
  • Derive the land–sea warming contrast from a moist-static-energy argument.
  • Say why thermal inertia is not the explanation for it.
  • List the mechanisms of polar amplification in roughly the right order, and explain the Planck argument quantitatively.
  • State and use pattern scaling, and name three places it fails.
  • Quote the two rates — 7 %/K for moisture, 1–3 %/K for precipitation — and derive both.
  • Explain why the tropical overturning circulation must weaken.
  • Distinguish the thermodynamic and dynamic contributions to precipitation change, and say which is more reliable.
  • Distinguish meteorological, agricultural and hydrological drought.
  • Compute how a shift in the mean changes the frequency of a tail event.
  • Explain why extreme rainfall intensifies faster than mean rainfall.
  • Define the probability ratio and the fraction of attributable risk, and state what an event attribution does and does not claim.
  • Explain what a compound event is and why independence understates the risk.
  • State the near-linear relation between warming and cumulative emissions, and explain the cancellation behind it.
  • Compute a remaining carbon budget from a target, and say which input dominates its uncertainty.
  • Explain why reaching net zero approximately stops the warming — and what it does not stop.
  • Give the four timescales of the carbon and climate response, from decades to 105 years.
  • Explain marine ice sheet instability from the bed geometry, and identify the bifurcation.
  • Recognise the saddle-node structure as the same one met in Lecture 7 and PC Lab 3.

Refusing the question

08:15 · 10 min

Points

  • Yesterday: the warming is ours, and we can show it with a regression. Today the same machinery gives a different kind of answer, and the difference is the point of the first hour.
  • Ask for the question they came in with. Someone will say “how hot will it be in 2100?”. Take it seriously and then decline it: no model contains an equation for what people will do. Say the word conditional and write it up.
  • Frame the four hours: two hours on what kind of statement we are making and how the response is distributed in space, then the last two on the two things that make this different — a finite budget, and things that do not come back.

Warm-up
“Lecture 6 gave us ECS = 3 K for a doubling of CO2. Why can we not just multiply and be done by half past eight?” Two answers and you want both. First, we do not know the doubling — that is a choice, not a physical constant. Second, even given the concentration, 3 K is the equilibrium answer and 2100 is not equilibrium. The first objection is page 1; the second is the two-layer model inside page 1's demonstration. Both are settled in the first block.
Block 1

Scenarios and the chain

08:25 · 35 min

1 · Why a scenario exists — five minutes, no hedging

  • The chain on the board. Emphasise that a climate model is only the last link; the first two are economics.
  • A scenario is a stated assumption. It carries no probability by design, because attaching one would be a claim about politics dressed as science.
  • Concentration-driven vs emission-driven. Say it once now; it comes back with force in block 6.
Show
Page 1, the chain schematic. Point at each arrow and name the discipline.

2 · RCP and SSP, briefly — they need the vocabulary, not a history

  • RCP: named for 2100 forcing in W m−2. Since a doubling is 3.7, RCP8.5 is a bit over two doublings. That framing lands better than the number.
  • SSP: narrative and forcing. Two numbers, two different things.
  • The table on page 1 is the reference; do not read it aloud.
Slide
Slides 4–7 — Figs. 6.1 and 6.4, the RCP forcing and emission pathways, and the SO2 panel. The SO2 slide is the one that matters; give it thirty seconds of silence and then ask what a falling negative forcing does.

3 · The demonstration — this is the block's payload, and the morning's

  • Run SSP2–4.5 first. Three panels: emissions you chose, concentration the carbon cycle produced, temperature the energy balance produced. Say plainly: this is a projection, not a picture of one.
  • Point at the readout comparing the computed 2081–2100 mean with the AR6 assessed range. 2.57 against an assessed 2.7, inside the very likely range. A four-parameter model in a browser. This buys you credibility for the whole morning — spend a moment on it.
  • Click through all five SSPs. Note the concentration diagnostic going progressively wrong at the high end and flag it as a promissory note for block 6: the missing feedback is the subject of a later hour.
  • Then the two sliders that matter: net zero year, and ECS. Do them one at a time.
Ask
“SSP1–1.9. Emissions are zero after 2050. Why is the temperature not coming down?” Zero emissions is not zero CO2. Make them look at the middle panel before you answer. Then name the near-cancellation — falling forcing against continuing ocean catch-up — and say the phrase zero-emissions commitment, promising block 6. Do not develop it here.
Ask
“Drag ECS from 2.5 to 4.0, the AR6 likely range. Bigger or smaller than the difference between SSP2–4.5 and SSP5–8.5?” Comparable mid-century, smaller by 2100. Which is exactly the question the next block measures properly. Use this as the transition — it is the cleanest one in the morning.

4 · Plausibility — three minutes, and be even-handed

  • Hausfather and Peters: RCP8.5 was the 90th percentile of the no-policy literature of 2005, not a default.
  • Current policies point at roughly 2.5–3 °C. Say both halves: better than the 4 °C world of a decade ago, and nowhere near the target. Students who only ever hear one half stop trusting you when they meet the other.
  • Why high scenarios are still run: clean signal, brackets carbon-cycle surprises, and risk is not expectation.
Slide
Slide 31 (Hausfather & Peters, 2020) and slide 32 (Liu et al., 2022, global emissions 1970–2050). These are the two most current slides in the deck — use them here rather than saving them, because block 6 will need the emissions plateau already established.
Block 2

Projections, predictions and where the uncertainty is

09:00 · 25 min

1 · The distinction, done properly once

  • Projection: boundary-value. Initialised from a control run, ensemble mean averages the phase away. Verified statistically.
  • Prediction: mixed. Initialised from observations, specifically the ocean. Verified date-by-date, and it fails.
  • Why the ocean: the atmosphere forgets in two weeks — their own Lorenz result from yesterday. Skill exists only because the ocean remembers.
Show
Page 2, the predictability demo. Drag initialisation quality from 0.9 to 0 and watch the skill curve flatten to zero. Then drag ocean memory and show that it, and only it, moves where the curve falls off. Ensemble size raises the curve; it cannot move the cliff.
Slide
Slides 8–10 — Fig. 6.7's schematic and Fig. 6.8 (Smith et al., 2013). Fig. 6.8 is the payoff: fewer than five years over most of the globe, longer in the North Atlantic. Say why the North Atlantic is the exception and it lands.

2 · The partition — the block's payload

  • Write the three-term variance on the board before showing anything. Then say how each behaves: internal is flat, model grows with the response, scenario starts at zero and grows fastest.
  • Start on Global, annual, 10-year means. Scenario takes over around mid-century.
  • Then click One grid box (Bø) and set averaging to 1 year. The picture inverts. Pause here. This is the most useful slide of the block for anyone who will ever advise anyone.
  • Then Policy already decided: scenario uncertainty vanishes. Ask whether that is cheating before you answer it.
Ask
“You are sizing a culvert in Bø for the next fifteen years. Which of the three terms should you spend money reducing?” None of them — the dominant term is internal variability and no amount of model improvement or policy clarity touches it. The right move is to design for the range, not the central estimate. This is the honest, useful and slightly deflating answer, and it is worth more to them than any projected number.
Slide
Slide 12 — Fig. 6.10, Kirtman et al. after Hawkins and Sutton, global annual and Europe DJF. Put it next to the live version and note they show the same thing. Keep slide 16 (Fig. 6.14, the precipitation version) back for block 4, where it makes a much sharper point.

3 · Working practice — three minutes, listed not laboured

  • Anomalies not absolutes; multi-model mean and why it works; the danger of shared errors; bias correction and its stationarity assumption; always report the spread.
  • Close with the honest limit: none of it removes the first band.

Block 3

The geography of warming

09:40 · 27 min

1 · How to read the maps — do this before showing one

  • Stippling: signal > 2σ of internal variability and 90 % sign agreement. Hatching: signal < 1σ. It is the σ from yesterday, grid box by grid box.
  • Then the trap: more hatching in a low scenario does not mean worse models, it means a smaller signal.
Slide
Slide 13 — Fig. 6.11, RCP2.6 and RCP8.5 maps. This is the single most important slide in the deck for this block, because the site cannot honestly reproduce it. Take a full two minutes: land–sea contrast, polar amplification, and the difference in hatching between the two panels.

2 · Land–sea contrast — the demonstration

  • Offer the wrong answer first. Ask why land warms faster; someone will say heat capacity. Accept it, then kill it: the contrast survives to equilibrium, where nothing is lagging.
  • Moist static energy. One line on the board: h = cpT + Lvq, shared aloft, split differently at the surface.
  • Run it at the defaults: 1.59, inside the observed 1.4–1.7.
  • Then the two-step diagnostic: set both RH to 80 % and both T equal — ratio exactly 1. Raise only the ocean temperature — ratio rises again. Two ingredients: land is drier, and land is colder.
Show
Page 3. The left panel shows the energy split as a bar — 71 % into evaporation over ocean against 53 % over land at the defaults. That bar is the whole argument in one picture.
Ask
“Where does soil moisture come into this?” Through the effective land RH — the Bowen ratio from Lecture 3. And it is a feedback: warming dries soil, drying raises the contrast, the contrast warms the land further. Flag that this returns twice more, in blocks 4 and 5.

3 · Polar amplification — lead with the surprise

  • Ask why the Arctic warms fastest. They will all say ice–albedo. Tell them it is third. The room wakes up.
  • Do the Planck panel: 4σT3, cold column sheds less per kelvin, so the same forcing warms it more — with no ice and no feedbacks at all. About 1.4× at the default settings.
  • Then lapse rate: the Arctic inversion traps warming near the surface instead of exporting it aloft. Largest single term.
  • Antarctica is different, and the difference is the Southern Ocean, not the physics. Worth thirty seconds — it pre-empts a question.
Slide
Slides 14–15 — Figs. 6.12 and 6.13. Use 6.13 as the checklist while you talk through the table.

4 · Pattern scaling — two minutes, then move

  • ΔT(x,t) ≈ P(x)·ΔTglobal(t). It is why an assessment can say “a 2 °C world” without naming a scenario.
  • The two derivations they just did are P, and neither mentioned the forcing — which is why P is roughly scenario-independent.
  • Where it breaks: aerosols, saturation, circulation shifts, and precipitation. The last one is the next block.
Block 4

The water cycle, and its two different rates

10:07 · 23 min

1 · Set up the paradox before resolving it

  • Write both on the board: +7 %/K moisture, +2 %/K precipitation. Ask how both can be true. Let it sit for fifteen seconds.
  • Supply side: Clausius–Clapeyron, exact, and the same exponential as the water-vapour feedback from Lecture 6.
  • Demand side: the atmosphere must radiate away the latent heat it releases. Point at the 80 W m−2 they assembled in Lecture 2. Precipitation is rationed by the energy budget, not by the moisture supply.
Show
Page 4. The three bars: +6.5, +1.5, −5.0 %/K at the defaults. The gap between the top two bars is the bottom one — say that sentence while pointing.
Ask
“Set the forcing slider to zero. The precipitation rate nearly doubles. The temperature has not changed — what happened?” The fast adjustment: CO2 also makes the atmosphere a worse radiator, which suppresses precipitation immediately, before any warming. Then the punchline worth delivering slowly — a solar forcing does not do this, so it gives more rainfall for the same warming, which is one of the strongest arguments against solar geoengineering as a substitute.

2 · Wet-gets-wetter and the circulation

  • δ(P−E) ≈ αΔT(P−E). Show the amplification slider on the zonal profile. Say out loud that the base profile is a labelled schematic with no value axis — it is a good moment to make the provenance point once.
  • The three caveats matter more than the slogan: it is P−E not P; it fails over land where the bucket is empty; and it assumes the circulation stays put.
  • The three Hadley panels. Terms 1 and 2 partly cancel; term 3 is a displacement, and it is what dries the Mediterranean.
  • The one-sentence summary to write down: temperature projections are mostly thermodynamics; precipitation projections are mostly dynamics. That is why the maps look so different.
Slide
Slide 17 — Fig. 6.15, the precipitation maps. Put it beside slide 13 from the last block if you can get both up. The contrast in hatching between the temperature and precipitation maps is the single best thing in the deck, and it needs both on screen. Then slide 16 (Fig. 6.14) for the uncertainty partition applied to precipitation, and slides 18–19 for Fig. 6.16.

3 · Drought — five minutes, and insist on the adjective

  • Three kinds. The agricultural one is where the confident signal is, and it is mechanical: rising vapour pressure deficit is the driving gradient in the Lecture 3 bulk formula.
  • Then close the loop: dry soil, higher Bowen ratio, hotter surface. Third time they have met it this morning; say so.
  • The honest complication: CO2 makes plants more water-efficient, which opposes it. Do not skip this — students who have read about it and not heard it from you will discount everything else.

Block 5

Extremes, and the arithmetic of tails

10:40 · 27 min

1 · Take the guess first — do not skip this

  • “A normal distribution shifts by one standard deviation. By what factor does the chance of exceeding the old 3σ threshold change?” Take three guesses from the room and write them up. They will be between 2 and 5.
  • The answer is 17. Write it next to their guesses and leave it there.
  • Then explain why: you are comparing two small numbers on a steeply falling curve, and the ratio of two small numbers can be enormous. Nothing climatic about it.

2 · The demonstration, and then the validation

  • Three steps, all visible: global warming → local warming (amplification, from block 3) → shift in units of σ → probability, exactly.
  • Defaults give ×5.6 for a 1-in-10 event at 2 °C, and the same event now 2.6 K hotter.
  • Point at the cold-extreme figure: ×0.03. The cold tail empties at almost exactly the rate the warm tail fills. Note that this symmetry is the signature of a shift rather than a variance change, and it is what observational records are checked against.
  • Then the table. Eight AR6 assessed multipliers against eight computed ones, agreeing to within a few per cent, from two numbers chosen once. Let the room look at it. This is the moment the sceptics and the catastrophists both go quiet.
Show
Page 5. Have slides 25–27 (AR6 SPM.3: hot extremes, heavy precipitation, agricultural drought) open in the deck at the same time — the site computes the left column, the slide is the source of the right one.
Ask
“Set σ to 0.9 K, a tropical value. The multiplier explodes. Is a stable climate more or less vulnerable?” More. Small σ means the same absolute warming is a larger shift in units of σ, so tropical extremes leave the historical range first — the earliest time of emergence anywhere on Earth is in the tropics, not the Arctic. Then the ecological version, which is the one this class will remember: organisms adapted to a narrow range have no margin. This is the point where Lecture 8 meets the case-study lectures from week 33.

3 · Event attribution, and rainfall — the two applications

  • PR and FAR. The demonstration has already computed PR; you are only renaming it. Say clearly what the claim is and is not: probabilities, never a single event's cause.
  • The counterfactual world comes from yesterday's fingerprint machinery. Event attribution is detection and attribution applied to a tail instead of a trend.
  • Rainfall: extremes follow the moisture rate, not the precipitation rate, because a storm rains out what is locally available. More flooding and more drought from one mechanism — say the apparent contradiction out loud before resolving it.
  • Compound events, briefly, ending on hot-and-dry and the soil-moisture link. Then flag PC Lab 3 in the afternoon as the same idea with a threshold.

Slides 22–24 — Fig. 6.20's shifted distribution and Fig. 6.21's five-day precipitation map. Slide 22 asks the class about headlines; use it if they are flagging, skip if they are not.
Block 6

Cumulative emissions — the one number that survives

11:07 · 28 min

1 · Redeem the promissory note from block 1

  • Where the carbon goes: 46 % air, 26 % ocean, 28 % land. Steady for sixty years while emissions quadrupled — the sinks kept pace.
  • β and γ. The ocean's β declines because the Revelle buffer is consumed by the very process that uses it — call back to Lecture 4. γ is negative and the land's is the biggest unknown in the system.
  • Then the payoff: go back to page 1, click SSP5–8.5, and point at the concentration diagnostic running 20 % low. That gap is the missing γ. They watched a model fail in a specific, diagnosable way an hour ago and now they know why.
Slide
Slides 28–29 — Figs. 6.22 and 6.23. Fig. 6.23 (emission-driven vs concentration-driven ESMs) is the one to dwell on; it is the published version of the failure you have just shown live.

2 · The collapse — do it in this exact order

  • Open page 6's first demonstration on the time axis. Six pathways, six different futures, nothing in common. Say so.
  • Ask what they would have to plot to make these agree. Take a guess or two.
  • Press “cumulative CO2 emitted”. Say nothing for five seconds.
  • Then the numbers: scatter of 0.19 K about one line, slope near 0.5 K per 1000 GtCO2. Write ΔT = TCRE × Ecum on the board.
  • Why it works: log forcing saturates, declining ocean uptake accelerates, they cancel. Be explicit that it is a numerical accident, not a law — it holds over exactly the range we are operating in, which is lucky rather than deep.
Show
Page 6. Also worth a moment: follow the overshoot pathway back leftwards and note it sits slightly above the line. Overshoot-and-remove is not the same as not emitting.
Slide
Slide 30 — Fig. 6.24, the published version of what they have just built. Showing it after the demonstration rather than before is the whole point.

3 · The budget — and now write the tally on the board

  • Invert the line. Three divisions. With the AR6 inputs: 511 GtCO2 from 2020 against the assessed 500.
  • Subtract what has been emitted since: about 260 left, about six years at 41 per year.
  • The misreading to pre-empt, and it is the important one: that year is not when 1.5 is breached. It is the year by which emissions must already be zero. Say it twice.
  • Drag TCRE across its likely range: the budget more than doubles. Name it as model uncertainty, and note the asymmetry — you are dividing by the uncertain quantity.
  • Then ZEC and the near-cancellation, which is what makes “net zero” coherent at all. Emphasise it is a physical result that could have come out otherwise.
Ask
“Global emissions have been roughly flat for a decade. Is that progress?” Both answers are true and they need both. Yes — the trajectory bent away from the exponential growth of 1990–2010, and the 4 °C world receded. No — the budget is an area under a curve, and a flat curve has a great deal of area under it. Point at the board tally while you say the second half. Then note the 2020 pandemic as a natural experiment: an unprecedented disruption to global activity, a few per cent, fully recovered in two years.
Slide
Slide 44 — the En-ROADS link. Set it as the thing to try before the afternoon lab, and tell them to watch the cumulative readout rather than the temperature one. After this block they know why.
Block 7

The long view — and the structure they have now seen three times

11:35 · 22 min

1 · Four clocks and the carbon tail — six minutes

  • Decades, centuries, millennia, 105 years. Most misunderstandings are one clock's intuition applied to another's process.
  • Run the tail demo at 1000 PgC: 41 % left after a century, 22 % after a millennium, 12 % after ten thousand years, and still 0.8 K of warming at 10 kyr.
  • The correction worth the whole block: a CO2 molecule leaves the atmosphere in about five years. A CO2 perturbation does not. Conflating them is the commonest error in this material and someone in the room is currently making it.
  • Switch to the unmodified Joos fit and show it never coming down. A fit used outside its range does not warn you — it just keeps returning numbers.
Show
Page 7. The process bands across the top of the upper panel do the explaining for you.
Slide
Slides 33–36 — Figs. 6.25 and 6.26 (Zickfeld et al.; Archer and Brovkin). Slide 34's point — stable temperature from cooling forcing balanced by reduced ocean heat uptake — is the ZEC argument again, so cross-reference block 6 rather than re-deriving it.

2 · Sea level — five minutes, and lead with the shape of the figure

  • Four contributions, four timescales. Only glaciers finish this century.
  • The 2100 figure: scenario separation of only about 0.4 m, comparable to the range within each scenario. Sea level is slow, so a century is not long enough for policy to show up in it.
  • Then 2300, where the separation is enormous. The twenty-first century is where the commitment is made; the following centuries are where it is paid.
  • The number to carry away: 2–3 m per degree, over 2000 years. Converts the targets from block 6 into something physical.
  • And: it does not come back. Hysteresis, for the same reason as everything else this morning.
Slide
Slides 37–39 — Figs. 6.27, 6.28 and 6.29. Slide 39 (Huybrechts, Greenland under 4×CO2 for 3000 years) is the one to spend time on if you have it; the site has no equivalent and the animation of a disappearing ice sheet does work no diagram does.

3 · AMOC — do not rebuild it, connect it

  • They built the Stommel model yesterday. Say explicitly: same model, different source of freshwater. Palaeo had a collapsing ice sheet; we have a hydrological cycle.
  • Run the freshwater panel: at 3 K, about 0.1 Sv from rainfall and 0.009 Sv from Greenland. Point out which term is doing the work. Most students assume it is the ice.
  • AR6: very likely decline, medium confidence no abrupt collapse before 2100. Explain why those are compatible.
  • Then the uncomfortable bit, and give it its full weight: models are probably too stable, for a specific diagnosable reason. Models agreeing with each other for a known bad reason is worse than a wide spread.
Slide
Slides 40–41 — the AMOC discussion and the IPCC-AR6 thermohaline figure.

4 · Marine ice sheet instability — the closer

  • Flotation sets thickness; flux goes as h4.75. Then the geometric argument in one sentence: retreat into deeper water means thicker ice means more outflow means more retreat.
  • Show the bed with its three equilibria: stable at 701 km, threshold at 615, stable at 75. 86 km of margin.
  • Drag buttressing down and let them watch the outer two merge and vanish. Name it: saddle-node bifurcation.
  • Then land the whole course. Same bifurcation as the Stommel model yesterday. Same as Daisyworld in PC Lab 1. Same as the Sahara in PC Lab 3 this afternoon. Three systems that share no physics at all and one piece of mathematics. If they take one thing from four weeks, this is it.
  • And the warning inside it: right up to the bifurcation the system is stable and looks fine. The margin shrinks silently.

Seven sentences, and the afternoon

11:57 · 3 min

Points

Questions that will come

  • “Which scenario is going to happen?” None of them exactly; current policies land near SSP2–4.5, roughly 2.5–3 °C. Then say why that is a genuine improvement on a decade ago and still a long way from the target, in that order.
  • “Weren't the old models wrong?” Almost always this means the scenario was not followed. The 1990 and subsequent projections, evaluated against the forcing that actually occurred, do well. Separate the physics from the scenario and the question answers itself — and that separation is what block 1 was for.
  • “Isn't 1.5 already lost?” On the arithmetic of block 6, holding below it without overshoot is now very unlikely. Say so plainly, then say what does not follow from it: the budget for 1.7 and 2.0 is much larger, and each tenth of a degree changes the extremes by the multipliers from block 5. The framing to give them is a dial, not a switch.
  • “Could we just remove the CO2 later?” The overshoot pathway in block 6 answers this: the line does not retrace exactly, and sea level and ice sheets do not retrace at all. Removal at the scale required is also a large fraction of present global emissions run in reverse.
  • “What about geoengineering?” Two answers already earned. The aerosol lifetime asymmetry from block 1 — masking commits you forever, and stopping unmasks in a decade. And the precipitation result from block 4 — a solar forcing and a CO2 forcing do not have the same hydrological consequence, so you cannot cancel one with the other.
  • “Why should I believe a model of the future at all?” The best answer available is on the screen: page 5's table, where two numbers chosen once reproduce eight assessed multipliers. Then the general form — models are tested against the past (Lecture 7), the mechanisms are separately derivable (blocks 3 and 4), and where they are weak we can say specifically where, as with the AMOC stability bias.
  • “Is it too late?” It will be asked, probably in the last five minutes, and it deserves a straight answer rather than a deflection. Every tenth of a degree avoided is a smaller multiplier in block 5's table and a smaller commitment in block 7's. There is no threshold after which action stops mattering — the budget arithmetic says the opposite, that it matters continuously and linearly. That is a real answer and it is also the honest one.
