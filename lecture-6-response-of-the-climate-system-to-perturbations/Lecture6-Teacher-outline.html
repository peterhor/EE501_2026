Response of the climate system to perturbations — lecture outline

A running order for the single slot the topic occupies — Tuesday 15 September 08:15–12:00 — with the point to make at each step, the number to put on the board, which interactive page to open, and the question to throw back at the room. Built from Lecture 6 — Response of the climate system to perturbations and the six teaching pages that go with it.

The one sentence the whole session is built on

The climate does not respond to a forcing — it responds to a forcing plus its own reaction to it, and the second part is two to three times larger than the first. Everything in this lecture is an accounting of that amplification.

Say it in the first five minutes and land it four times: the forcing is the easy part and it is nearly settled; the feedbacks are the hard part and they are where all the uncertainty lives; the amplification is a division, not a multiplication, which is why the uncertainty comes out lopsided; and the transient response is slower than the forcing, so the number you measure today is not the number you are heading for.

The spine, and it is a single arithmetic thread. The whole lecture is one calculation carried across four pages: 3.7 → λf → ΔT → ΔT(t). Say that out loud at the start and again at the end. If a student can reproduce 3.7 / 1.26 ≈ 2.9 K and explain every symbol, they have the lecture.

The debt from Thursday. Lecture 5 closed with 1.1 K and 3 K on the board and a promise. Do not start this lecture without pointing at those two numbers. Block 3 pays the debt in front of them, live, and it is worth the theatre.

The trap to head off early. Students hear “positive feedback” as “good” and “negative feedback” as “bad”. Kill it in Block 1 with the loop-sign widget: positive means destabilising, and it amplifies cooling exactly as readily as warming. The second trap follows immediately: a positive feedback parameter λi makes λf less negative. Both signs are consistent; both get misread.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the session students should be able to:

  • Define forcing and feedback and explain why the distinction depends on where the system boundary is drawn.
  • State the three clauses in the definition of radiative forcing and say why each is there.
  • Distinguish RF from ERF, and say for which agent the difference matters most.
  • Compute ΔQ for a given CO2 concentration and explain why the response is logarithmic.
  • Quote 3.7 W m−2 for a doubling and use it as a yardstick for every other forcing.
  • List the major forcing agents with sign and rough magnitude, and identify the two that dominate the uncertainty.
  • Explain why aerosol cooling is temporary in a way greenhouse warming is not.
  • Write down ΔR = ΔQ + λfΔTs and say what each term is.
  • Explain why λf must be negative for the planet to have a climate.
  • Derive λ0 = −4σTe3 and get 1.1 K per doubling from it.
  • Decompose λf into five terms and give the sign and mechanism of each.
  • Explain why symmetric uncertainty in λf gives an asymmetric range in ΔT.
  • Say why λw and λL are quoted together and cloud is not.
  • Explain the lapse-rate feedback and why its sign differs between tropics and high latitudes.
  • Explain why low cloud cools and high cloud warms, and why the feedback is uncertain.
  • Write down CsdT/dt = ΔQ + λfΔT, give τ = −Cs/λf, and say what the initial slope does and does not contain.
  • Distinguish ECS from TCR and say why TCR is better constrained.
  • Explain committed warming and relate it to the observed radiative imbalance.
  • Distinguish biogeochemical from biogeophysical feedbacks and give one example where they oppose.
  • Explain the β and γ carbon feedbacks and their effect on the airborne fraction.
  • Place any named feedback on a timescale and give its sign.

Session — Tuesday 15 September, 08:15–12:00

Open

Paying Thursday’s debt

08:15 · 10 min

Points

  • Write 1.1 K and 3 K on the board before saying anything. Ask who remembers what they are.
  • Restate it: same forcing, 3.7 W m−2. A physically correct zero-dimensional model gives 1.1. Every GCM gives about 3. Neither is wrong.
  • Announce the shape: today is entirely about the factor of three. By 10:40 you will have built it out of five numbers, in front of them.
  • Then the arithmetic spine, and leave it up all morning: 3.7 → λf → ΔT → ΔT(t).
Warm-up
The CO2 forcing is 3.7 W m−2. The Sun delivers 340. That is one per cent. How can one per cent matter? Two answers and you want both. First, it is one per cent of the gross flux but an enormous fraction of the net imbalance, which is normally zero — the planet is a balance, and you are leaning on one pan. Second, and better: the seasonal cycle in Bø is driven by an insolation change of hundreds of W m−2 and produces 20 K, so 3.7 producing 3 K is not obviously out of scale. Then note that they have just used a rough climate sensitivity argument, and that making it rigorous is the morning’s work.
Block 1

Two definitions, and one currency

08:25 · 35 min

1 · Forcing and feedback — do this slowly, it is the whole vocabulary

  • Both definitions verbatim from the slides, then the two examples: the Sun dimming (forcing), and warming → evaporation → more vapour → more warming (feedback).
  • The distinguishing test: can the system change it by responding? If no, it is a forcing.
  • Then the awkward bit, and do not skip it: the boundary is a choice. Ice sheets are a feedback in a glacial-cycle study and a boundary condition in a glacial-circulation study. Same ice, same physics, different question.
  • Tie it explicitly to Thursday’s boundary conditions vs external forcings columns. This is the same idea arriving for the third time; say so.
Show
Page 1 — the box-and-loop schematic at the top. Point at the arrow crossing the boundary and the loop that does not. Twenty seconds; it is a picture of a sentence.

2 · Radiative forcing — the three clauses

  • Motivate before defining: a volcano, a power station and a wheat field. You need one number they all produce.
  • Then the definition, and go through the three-panel figure clause by clause: at the tropopause, stratosphere adjusted, surface and troposphere fixed.
  • The clause worth dwelling on is the third. Let the surface warm and you have started including feedbacks, and the number is no longer comparable between agents. The definition is engineered to keep forcing and response apart.
  • Sign convention on the board and leave it: downward positive. It will matter in Block 3 and they will forget.
  • RF vs ERF in ninety seconds. Identical for CO2, very different for aerosols, because most of the aerosol effect runs through cloud.

3 · The calculator — answer the slide’s own question

  • Put ΔQ = 5.4 ln([CO2]/[CO2]r) on the board and ask them to do 2 × CO2 in their heads. Somebody will get 5.4 × 0.69.
  • Then open the demo and land 3.7. Box it. Everything else today is measured in units of this.
  • Now drag to 1112 ppm and let them see the second doubling is worth the same. Logarithmic, because the band core is saturated. Good news and bad news, in that order.
  • Hit Last glacial maximum: −2.2 W m−2 from CO2 alone against a 4–5 K colder world. Ask what supplies the rest — it is ice albedo, and it is a preview of Block 4.
  • Hit 2024: +2.7 W m−2 of greenhouse forcing. Park it; Block 2 will take a watt off it.
Show
Page 1, the GHG calculator. Then, without leaving the page, the loop-sign widget underneath: load water vapour, then snow–ice albedo, and make them count the minus signs before you press anything. Even number → positive loop. That rule earns its keep four times today.
Ask
Methane is a far stronger absorber per molecule than CO2. Why is its bar a quarter the size? Concentration — 2 ppm against 420 — and band position. Its band is nowhere near saturated, which is why the dependence is a square root rather than a logarithm: it grows faster per molecule from a much smaller base. Then the policy line: per kilogram emitted methane is far worse, per kilogram in the air there is very little of it, and it is gone in a decade. Both statements are true and people pick whichever suits them.
Block 2

What has actually been pushing on the planet

09:00 · 35 min

1 · Build the ledger live — do not just show the finished chart

  • Start with Clear, then CO2 alone: 1.82. Then the rest of the greenhouse gases: about 3.3, comfortably more than one doubling.
  • Ask what the net anthropogenic forcing is before you switch on the aerosols. Somebody will say 3.3.
  • Switch them on. 2.25. Let the silence sit for a second. A third of the greenhouse forcing has been cancelled by pollution nobody chose to emit for that purpose.
  • Then the uncertainty bars, which is the real lesson. The two aerosol terms carry nearly all the spread, and they are subtracted from a well-known number.
Ask — this is the good one
Why does an uncertain aerosol forcing make the climate sensitivity uncertain? Because we observe one warming and infer the sensitivity by dividing it by the forcing. A large aerosol cooling means the applied forcing was small, so the climate must be very sensitive to have warmed as much as it did. A small aerosol cooling means the opposite. The same thermometer record is consistent with both. Say plainly: the uncertainty in what we did to the planet is nearly as large as the uncertainty in how it responds, and most people assume only the second exists.

2 · Aerosols — the mechanisms, briskly

  • The schematic: scattering (sulphate, cooling), absorption (black carbon, warming and it darkens snow), and then the cloud pathways, which are the bigger half.
  • Twomey in one sentence: same water, more nuclei, smaller and more numerous droplets, brighter cloud. Then lifetime: smaller droplets rain out less readily.
  • Note that black carbon is a positive forcing and, on some estimates, second only to CO2. Aerosols are not one thing with one sign.
  • Optical depth in one line, since the slides use it: transmitted fraction is e−τ, zero means transparent.
Ask
Aerosols last a week; CO2 lasts a century. What follows? That the cooling is rented and the warming is owned. Clean the air — which is unambiguously the right thing to do for human health — and you unmask warming that has already been paid for. This is not an argument against clean air; it is an argument for knowing what you are holding. Note that this actually happened when marine fuel sulphur limits tightened in 2020.

3 · Land use — one calculation done by hand

  • Do the three factors on the board before opening the demo: albedo change × insolation × area fraction.
  • Then the demo, and let them discover that the plain forest-to-crop contrast gives about −0.44, three times the assessed value. Ask why. (Much cleared land was grassland; clearance is patchy.)
  • Push the snow fraction slider up and watch the number move hard. Boreal clearance is mostly a snow-albedo story.
  • Then the punchline: deforestation warms in the tropics and can cool in the boreal zone. “Plant trees” is a latitude-dependent recommendation. This is the moment the ecologists in the room sit up.

4 · Sun and volcanoes — the natural yardstick

  • TSI varies by 1 in 1361 over the 11-year cycle. Do the (1−α)/4 on the board: 0.18 W m−2. Smaller than they expect.
  • Secular solar change since 1750: +0.05. Less than the cycle. A fortieth of the anthropogenic total. Say it once, plainly, and move on — do not turn it into a debate.
  • Volcanoes: only explosive, only because they reach the stratosphere, where there is no rain to remove the aerosol. One to three years instead of one week.
  • Then the timeline, with everything on. Krakatau and Pinatubo both hit −3 — bigger than the whole anthropogenic forcing of their day — and both are gone in three years. Press Sum. Volcanoes make the record bumpy; the trend is not theirs.
Ask — plant this deliberately, it pays off in Block 5
The solar cycle forcing is bigger than the whole secular solar trend. Why can you barely see an 11-year cycle in the temperature record? Do not answer it. Say “hold that” and write 11 yr? in the corner of the board. Block 5 answers it with the ocean’s heat capacity, and the deferred payoff is worth far more than the immediate answer.
break · 10 min
Block 3

The feedback parameter — the centre of the lecture

09:45 · 55 min

1 · The linearisation — on the board, not the screen

  • Write ΔR = ΔQ + λfΔTs and leave it up for the rest of the morning. Everything else is a gloss on it.
  • Name the terms out loud: what you did, what the system does back, what is left over and currently going into the ocean.
  • Stress that ΔR is not bookkeeping — it is measured, it is about 0.9 W m−2, and Block 5 uses it.
  • Then set ΔR = 0: ΔTs = −ΔQ/λf. Say the words “climate sensitivity is minus one over lambda” and make them repeat it.

2 · Why λf must be negative — the stability figure

  • Show the four lines. Each starts at ΔR = ΔQ and the system slides down its own line until it crosses zero.
  • Steeper line, earlier crossing, less warming. Flatter line, later crossing, more warming.
  • Then the red line, which never crosses. Ask what that world looks like. Venus. The fact that Earth has stayed within a few tens of kelvin for four billion years is a strong empirical statement that λf < 0.
  • Head off the sign trap now, explicitly: a positive λi makes λf less negative and therefore warmer. Write both on the board side by side.

3 · λ₀ — derive it, do not show it

  • Board work, and it takes two minutes. Start from the TOA balance they already have from Lecture 2, differentiate, assume uniform warming: λ0 = −4σTe3.
  • Put Te = 255 in and get −3.8. Then say models give −3.2, and say why: emission from a range of altitudes, some straight through the window, warming not uniform. Not an error — a refinement.
  • Then 3.7 / 3.2 = 1.16 K. Point at the 1.1 still on the board from Thursday. That is where it came from. One feedback, the only one the 0-D model had.

4 · The assembler — the ten minutes the lecture exists for

  • Open on Planck only. 1.16 K. Same number as the board.
  • Add water vapour. 2.6 K. Note the size of the jump from one feedback.
  • Add lapse rate, which pulls it back down — the first negative feedback that is not Planck.
  • Add cloud and albedo. Land on λf = −1.26 and 2.9 K. Point at the 3 on the board. Debt paid.
  • Now the part that matters more than the answer. Nudge λc by ±0.4 — the published inter-model spread, one term — and watch it cover most of 2.2 to 4.4. One uncertain feedback covers nearly the whole assessed range.
  • Then the right-hand panel deliberately. Equal steps in λf, unequal steps in ΔT. It is a hyperbola. The long high tail on every published sensitivity range is geometry, not pessimism.
  • Finish with A glacial climate: λα more than doubles. There is no single true climate sensitivity — it depends on the state you are in.
Ask
Why is every published climate sensitivity range lopsided — 1.5 to 4.5 rather than 2 to 4? Because ΔT = −ΔQ/λf is a hyperbola. Symmetric error in λf becomes asymmetric error in ΔT, and the asymmetry gets worse as λf approaches zero. This is worth labouring: it means the high tail cannot be dismissed as alarmism, and it means a small improvement in constraining cloud feedback is worth a very large improvement in the warming estimate. Show it by dragging rather than asserting it.
Ask, if there is time
The feedback factor ff is about 2.5. What is it a factor of? Of the blackbody response, not of the forcing. It says the real system warms two and a half times as much as bare physics would give. Useful because it separates “what the physics demands” from “what the planet adds”, and the second is the part that is argued about.

5 · The four caveats — read them, do not gloss them

  • No spatial information; forcings are not equally efficient; feedbacks depend on the mean state; thresholds break the linearisation.
  • The fourth is the one to spend thirty seconds on, because PC Lab 2 this afternoon is a threshold and Lab 3 tomorrow is another.
  • Then defend the framework: it gives the right order of magnitude, it says which feedbacks are worth arguing about, and it lets a two-line calculation check a model that cost a million core-hours.
break · 10 min
Block 4

Where the five numbers come from

10:50 · 35 min

1 · Water vapour — two facts and a consequence

  • Clausius–Clapeyron: 7 % per kelvin. That is thermodynamics.
  • Relative humidity stays roughly constant. That is an observation, not a derivation — flag the difference.
  • Then the bit worth the time: the radiative effect goes as the logarithm of vapour, so adding moisture to the already-wet tropical boundary layer does almost nothing and adding it to the dry upper troposphere does a great deal. Same saturation argument as CO2 in Block 1.
  • The ecological consequence, worth thirty seconds: total precipitation rises 2 %/K because the energy budget limits it, while available moisture rises 7 %/K. Extreme rainfall intensifies faster than mean rainfall, and that arithmetic is the whole reason.

2 · Lapse rate — use the demo, this one is genuinely hard to say in words

  • Set up first: the planet radiates from about 5 km, not from the ground. So what matters is how much the emitting layer warms.
  • Load Uniform warming: λL = 0 by construction, which is exactly the assumption λ0 made.
  • Load Tropics: the top warms more, the planet sheds heat more efficiently than the blackbody calculation says, negative feedback.
  • Load High latitudes: warming trapped near the surface under an inversion, planet sheds less, positive. Same physics, opposite sign, different place.
  • Global mean is negative because the tropics are half the planet and emit far more. An area-weighted statement about very unequal places — same lesson as the geography of the budget in Lecture 2.
Show
Then the anticorrelation scatter immediately after. Individually each model scatters widely; the sum does not. Say the sentence: “a model that warms the upper troposphere strongly gets a big negative lapse-rate feedback and, in the same air, a big positive water-vapour feedback”. The errors cancel. That is why the pair is always quoted together — and why cloud, which has no partner, dominates.

3 · Cloud — the height argument, with the explorer open

  • Every cloud does two opposite things. Which wins is almost entirely about height.
  • Marine stratocumulus: net −29. Its top is below the clear-sky emission level, so the vapour above was already doing the trapping. It only reflects. Low cloud cools, unambiguously.
  • Thin cirrus: net +25. Cold top, barely reflects. High thin cloud warms.
  • Deep convective tower: near zero. Both effects maximised and nearly cancelling — a small residual between two numbers of order 50. Say why that is an uncomfortable thing to have to compute.
  • Then Global mean mix: about −20 W m−2. Clouds cool the present Earth by five times the whole anthropogenic forcing.
  • Now pivot to the actual feedback, which is about change: raise high tops (fixed anvil temperature, models agree) and lose low cloud (models disagree). Both positive. The disagreement about how much low cloud is lost is the ±0.4 from Block 3.

4 · Ice, soil, ocean — fast, and pick two

  • Snow–ice albedo: the loop they already counted in Block 1. Note the seasonality — strongest in spring, when snow goes and the sun is high. Then sea-ice insulation, which is not radiative and explains why Arctic warming peaks in winter.
  • Warn against the standard oversimplification: polar amplification is not one feedback. Albedo, positive high-latitude lapse rate, transport, and the flatness of σT4 where it is cold.
  • Soil moisture: straight back to the Bowen ratio from Lecture 3. Dry soil spends its energy heating rather than evaporating. This is the feedback that turns a warm summer into a heatwave, and it acts in weeks, in the growing season. The one on this page an ecologist will actually use.
  • Ocean advective feedback: mention only, but mention it — it is the one with two stable states, and it is the shape of PC Lab 3.
Show
Finish the block on the summary bar chart. Say: read the whiskers, not the bars. On that reading water vapour is large and well constrained, cloud is a third the size with four times the uncertainty, and effort spent on cloud is worth more than effort spent anywhere else. That has been true in every assessment since 1990.
Block 5

Adding time, and answering the 11-year question

11:25 · 20 min

1 · The equation, and the one term that surprises

  • Take the equation already on the board and stop setting ΔR to zero: Cs dT/dt = ΔQ + λfΔT. The leftover energy heats something.
  • Write the solution and τ = −Cs/λf. Then ask them to look at it again. λf is in the response time. A more sensitive climate is also a slower one.
  • Then the initial slope: at t = 0, ΔT = 0, so dT/dt = ΔQ/Cs, with no λf in it at all.

2 · Run it — three scenarios, no more

  • Step 2×CO2. Two climates, 2 K and 4 K, indistinguishable for fifteen years. Let that sit. Watching the early response does not identify the sensitivity. This is the honest technical reason the 1.5–4.5 range has survived forty years of good data, and it is the answer to give when someone implies the uncertainty is carelessness.
  • Volcanic eruption. −3 W m−2, and only a few tenths of a kelvin of cooling. Then drag the slab depth to 10 m and watch it grow. Now answer the 11 yr? on the board. The ocean is a low-pass filter: fast forcings do not get their full response. Deferred payoff, collected.
  • Ramp, then hold. Forcing stops rising, temperature keeps climbing. Name it: committed warming, already forced, not yet realised, currently in transit into the ocean. Point at the imbalance panel — that is the measurable signature, and it is 0.9 W m−2 in the real world.

3 · Two numbers, and one honest calculation

  • ECS vs TCR, thirty seconds and the table. TCR is smaller, better constrained, and closer to what this century actually does. The gap between them is ocean heat uptake and nothing else.
  • If time: the closing card. ΔR ≈ ΔQ + λfΔT with 0.9, 2.7 and 1.2 gives λf ≈ −1.5 and about 1.9 K already committed. A climate sensitivity from three measurements and one linear equation, landing inside the assessed range.
  • Then mistrust it out loud: the aerosol uncertainty from Block 2 is inside ΔQ, and the pattern effect says this λf is probably too negative. Both push the answer up. The calculation is worth doing precisely because every assumption in it is visible.
Block 6

When the biosphere is allowed to respond

11:45 · 10 min

Fast, and set the page as reading

  • The distinction, in one line each. Biogeochemical: the biosphere changes atmospheric composition, so the effect is global. Biogeophysical: it changes surface properties, so the effect is local — and the two can oppose on the same hectare.
  • β and γ, and only the mechanism. β: sinks respond to concentration, and would do so with no warming at all. γ: sinks weaken as it warms. Warn about the sign convention, which reads backwards.
  • Open the airborne-fraction demo and press No climate–carbon feedback, then Best estimate. About 0.6 K of extra warming from no extra emission. Say the consequence: a climate sensitivity quoted for a fixed CO2 concentration systematically understates the response to an emission.
  • Tundra–taiga in thirty seconds, and make it the ecological hook: trees mask snow, albedo falls, warming amplifies — while the same trees are taking up carbon. Boreal afforestation can warm the planet. This is where the case-study weeks and the physics weeks meet.
  • Point at the Sahara loop and say “that is Lab 3”. Mention permafrost and carbonate compensation exist; the page has them.
Show
Finish on the timescale ladder and nothing else. Read it twice, out loud. Vertically: every question has its own effective sensitivity, and it grows as you go down. Horizontally: nearly everything is red. Most loops in the climate system amplify, and the reason the planet is stable anyway is that one negative feedback — σT4, hot things radiate — is bigger than all the amplifiers put together. That is the last sentence of the lecture and it is worth arriving at.

The six points, and the bridge

11:55 · 5 min

From the slides, in their own words if possible

  • Definitions of forcing and feedback, and that the boundary is a choice.
  • Several anthropogenic and natural forcings perturb the climate; some positive (greenhouse gases, black carbon), some negative (sulphate, land use).
  • Many feedbacks act, of both signs. Blackbody, lapse rate, water vapour, cloud, snow-and-ice albedo, tundra–taiga.
  • One number, λf, collects them all, and −1/λf is the climate sensitivity.
  • Cloud dominates the uncertainty; the hyperbola makes the range lopsided.
  • The ocean makes the response slow, so today’s warming is not the eventual warming.

Then the sentence to finish on, and the bridge

  • Point at 1.1 and 3, still on the board since Thursday. The gap is not a disagreement about physics. It is the planet's own response to what we did, and it is two thirds of the total.
  • PC Lab 2 this afternoon is the ice-albedo feedback made runnable — the same loop they counted in Block 1, in a latitudinal model, strong enough to have a threshold. Tell them to expect the ice line and to expect it not to come back.
  • Tomorrow morning is the past and present: the same feedbacks, run backwards against the palaeo record, and the attribution argument that Block 2’s timeline was a preview of.
