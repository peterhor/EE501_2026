Climate change: past and present — lecture outline

A running order for the single slot the topic occupies — Wednesday 16 September 08:15–12:00, room 4-311B — with the point to make at each step, the number to put on the board, which interactive page to open, and the question to throw back at the room. Built from Lecture 7 — Climate change: past and present and the six teaching pages that go with it. PC Lab 3 follows at 12:15, and the last five minutes should set it up.

The one sentence the whole session is built on

You cannot say that anything caused a climate change until you know how much the climate changes when nothing causes it. Everything before the last hour of this lecture is measuring that denominator.

Say it in the first five minutes and land it four times: the climate wanders on its own, and loudly at small scales; the wandering has structure and names; a forcing produces an amplified, non-linear, sometimes abrupt response; and only once all of that is quantified can the twentieth century be tested against it.

The spine, and it is a single logical thread rather than an arithmetic one. Six pages, arranged by timescale: noise → modes → orbits → thresholds → the last 2000 yr → attribution. Write those six words on the board at 08:20 and tick them off. A student who can say why each one is needed for the next has the lecture.

The debt from yesterday. Lecture 6 ended with ΔT = 3 K for a doubling and a transient response that lags it. Today does not extend that calculation — it tests it, against a record. Say so at the start: yesterday was theory, today is evidence.

The trap to head off early. Students arrive believing that “natural variability” is an alternative explanation to be argued against. It is not an alternative; it is the null hypothesis, and taking it seriously is what makes the conclusion strong. Kill the adversarial framing in Block 1 and the last block becomes easy.

The second trap. “CO2 lags temperature in the ice cores.” It will come up, and if it does not, raise it yourself in Block 4 — it is a good question with a good answer, and page 4 handles it in a box. Answer it once, properly, and it stops being a problem.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the session students should be able to:

  • Distinguish forced from internal variability, and explain why only the first has an ultimate cause.
  • Explain why the distinction depends on where the system boundary is drawn, with ice sheets as the example.
  • State why non-linearity gives a finite forecast horizon, and why that does not prevent climate projection.
  • Explain why internal variability is larger at smaller scales, and quote σ for the global mean against a single grid box.
  • Define time of emergence and explain the t3/2 scaling of signal-to-noise.
  • Name the five major modes of variability and give the region and timescale of each.
  • Describe the normal, El Niño and La Niña states of the tropical Pacific.
  • Trace the Bjerknes feedback loop and determine its sign by counting.
  • Say what a positive NAO winter means for Norway, and why the NAO index is nearly white.
  • Name the three orbital parameters, their ranges and periods, and what each does to insolation.
  • Explain why eccentricity alone is a negligible forcing but is required for precession to matter.
  • Compute or read off the 65°N June insolation and compare its magnitude with a CO2 doubling.
  • Explain why the global annual mean insolation barely changes and what that implies about amplification.
  • State the 100-kyr problem and explain how a non-linear response creates a frequency absent from the forcing.
  • Explain the role of CO2 as a feedback in glacial cycles and give two mechanisms for the 90 ppm.
  • Describe Dansgaard–Oeschger and Heinrich events and the evidence for each.
  • Explain how freshwater can shut down the overturning circulation, and why that is a positive feedback.
  • Explain hysteresis and why an abrupt change does not require an abrupt cause.
  • Explain the bipolar seesaw and the phase relationship it predicts.
  • Explain why the Holocene thermal optimum is seasonal and hemispheric rather than global.
  • Give the approximate amplitude of the MCA–LIA difference and say why regional asynchrony matters.
  • Distinguish detection from attribution and state what each requires.
  • Explain what the scaling coefficient β means, including the case β well determined but the contribution nil.
  • List the independent lines of evidence that the CO2 rise is anthropogenic.
  • Explain why a trend from a short window is nearly meaningless, using 1998–2012 as the example.

Session — Wednesday 16 September, 08:15–12:00

Open

The question the whole morning answers

08:15 · 10 min

Points

  • Do not start with the past. Start with the present, and with a challenge. Put up the global temperature curve — the deck’s Fig. 5.40 or page 6’s schematic — and ask: how do you know the climate did not just do that by itself?
  • Take answers for two minutes. Most will reach for “because CO2”, which is an explanation, not evidence. Push once: how would you know if you were wrong?
  • Then the frame for the morning: to answer that you need to know how large the climate’s own variability is. Nobody in the room can currently say. By 11:55 they will be able to give a number, with an uncertainty.
  • Write the six-word spine and say the lecture goes backwards through time in order to come forward again at the end.
Warm-up
Yesterday we got 3 K per doubling from physics. Why not stop there — why does anyone need the ice cores? Two answers, and you want both. First, because 3 K came out of a model, and a model is a hypothesis until it is tested against something it was not built to reproduce — that is Lecture 5’s validation argument. Second, and better: because a number for the response tells you nothing about whether a particular observed change was caused by a forcing. You need the null hypothesis as well as the alternative. Say that this second point is the whole lecture.
Block 1

The climate wanders on its own

08:25 · 35 min

1 · Two definitions, slowly — this is the vocabulary for four hours

  • Both definitions verbatim: forced variability is driven by a change in an external forcing; internal variability comes from interactions between components. Then the pair that makes it stick: ultimate cause against proximate cause.
  • The test: can the system change it by responding? If not, forcing. Same test as Lecture 6, and say so — this is its third appearance in three days.
  • Then the awkward bit, and do not skip it: the boundary is a choice. Ice sheets are a feedback over a glacial cycle and a boundary condition over a millennium. Same ice, different question. Flag that Block 3 depends on this.
Show
Page 1, the two-panel schematic at the top: an arrow crossing the boundary versus a loop that stays inside it. Twenty seconds. It is a picture of a sentence, and it is the sentence they need.

2 · Why internal variability exists — the chaos argument, done in three minutes

  • The equations are non-linear, so two indistinguishable states diverge. Nothing has to push the system for it to wander; the wandering is what the equations do.
  • Run the Lorenz slider live. Start at 10−6, then drag to 10−9 and make them watch the divergence point move only a little. Say the line out loud: a thousand times better initial data buys a fixed extra interval, not a thousand times more forecast.
  • Then the three consequences, in order, and land the third hardest: a model is not expected to reproduce the date of an unforced event. That single sentence prevents most of the confusion in Block 5.
  • Head off the standard misreading immediately: unpredictable weather, predictable climate; initial-value problem versus boundary-value problem. Somebody always asks; answer it before they do.
Ask
If the atmosphere is chaotic, how can anyone project the climate of 2100? Because you are not being asked for the trajectory, you are being asked for the distribution. Analogy that works: nobody can tell you the outcome of the next coin toss and everybody can tell you the outcome of ten thousand of them. Then push it: what would you have to change to alter the distribution of weather? The boundary conditions — which is exactly what a forcing is.

3 · The signal-and-noise demo — this is the block’s payload

  • Open the demo on Global mean, one member, one-year window. The trend is obvious. Emergence in about a decade.
  • Now click straight through to One grid box, Arctic sea-ice edge without changing anything else. Let them sit with it. The trend has vanished into the noise, and nothing about the forcing changed.
  • Point at the σ column on the board: 0.11 against 1.8. Sixteen times. Ask where that factor comes from — area averaging and the sea-ice albedo feedback of Lecture 6.
  • Now raise the trend to 0.6 K/decade, roughly what the Arctic is really doing, and watch it reappear. The Arctic is both the noisiest place and the fastest-warming, and which wins depends on how many years you have.
  • Then the averaging window and the ensemble sliders, quickly, and the scaling rule: noise falls as 1/√n, signal grows as t, so S/N grows as t3/2. Long records are worth disproportionately more than good ones. That is the argument for the next four blocks.
Show
Finish the block on the timescale spectrum at the bottom of page 1. Use it as the lecture’s table of contents: point at the band each remaining block lives in, and at the shaded “where attribution is hard” region, which is where the last two blocks operate.
Ask
Solar output and the AMO both vary on 60–90 year timescales. One is a forcing and one is internal. Given a single temperature record, could you tell which caused an observed 80-year swing? No — and that is the point. Timing alone cannot separate them. This is exactly why attribution uses spatial and vertical fingerprints rather than time series, which is Block 6. Park the question visibly; you will answer it in three hours.
Block 2

The wandering has structure

09:00 · 35 min

1 · What a mode is — five minutes, and be fussy

  • Pattern times index. Write anomaly(x,y,t) ≈ index(t) × pattern(x,y) and say that is the entire content of “the NAO was strongly positive in 1989”.
  • Why patterns exist: the system has geography, and only some arrangements reinforce themselves against it.
  • Kill the word “oscillation” now. These are broadband, not periodic. Hunting for the period of the NAO is a standard way to waste a career.
  • Correlation map versus regression map, in thirty seconds, using the deck’s Fig. 5.8 which shows both for the NAO: correlation says where it is reliable, regression says how many degrees per unit index. Students routinely read a correlation map as if it had units.

2 · ENSO properly — 15 minutes, and do it live rather than describing it

  • Open the cross-section on Neutral. Establish the normal state first and get them to say it back: trades blow west, warm water piles up in the west, thermocline 150 m west and 50 m east, upwelling in the east, convection over the warm pool.
  • Now drag the slider slowly to Very strong El Niño and narrate what changes. Do not click the preset — drag it. The point is that everything moves together and there is no first mover.
  • Stop at +2.5 and read the KPIs: trades at a fraction of normal, the thermocline flat, the SST contrast collapsed, and the global mean up by two-tenths of a degree in the following year. Point at σ = 0.11 on the board. That is where most of it comes from.
  • Then the Bjerknes loop. Make them count the minus signs before you say the answer. Two minuses, even, therefore positive, therefore self-amplifying — the same rule they learned yesterday for water vapour.
  • Finally the question they will ask anyway: why doesn’t it stick? Fast positive feedback plus slow delayed negative feedback from ocean wave adjustment. Do not go further into the delayed oscillator than that.
Show
After the cross-section, put up the deck’s Fig. 5.6 — the correlation between Niño3.4 and global sea-level pressure. The pages deliberately do not draw maps; this is one of the four places where the deck earns its keep. Say the sentence: a regional phenomenon with a nearly global signature.

3 · The other four — 10 minutes total, and resist going deep

  • Use the mode gallery and click through NAO, SAM, PDO, AMO. Thirty to ninety seconds each. The purpose is that they can recognise the names and the timescales, not that they can explain the dynamics.
  • Spend the extra time on the NAO, because it is the one that determines their winters. A positive NAO winter in Bø: mild, wet, windy. Negative: cold and still. Ask who noticed last winter.
  • Point at the synthetic index panels and make the honest disclaimer out loud — these are generated to have the right spectral character, not observed data. Then use them for what they are good for: the five modes are distinguishable by character alone. ENSO buzzes, the NAO is nearly white, the AMO drifts.
  • SAM in one sentence, but include the Southern Ocean carbon link — it connects to Lecture 4 and it is the sort of connection that makes a course feel like a course.
Show
On the SAM, put up the deck’s Fig. 5.9 — the regression of surface pressure on the SAM index, April–June and July–September. The page draws the SAM as a labelled annulus and deliberately does not fake a map; this is where the real field belongs. Two sentences: the anomaly is a ring because the Southern Ocean has no continents to break the symmetry, and a positive index means lower pressure over the pole, so the westerlies contract poleward and intensify near 60°S while weakening near 40°S. Then move on — do not let the SAM eat the NAO’s time.
Ask
The AMO has a 60–80 year timescale. The instrumental record is 170 years. How many independent cycles is that? Two, at most. Let that land before saying anything else. Then: this is why the question of whether the AMO is internal or partly forced by aerosols is genuinely unsettled, and why the answer has to come from long control runs and from palaeo records. It is also a preview of Block 5. If someone objects that surely we can just look at the data — that is the objection you want, and the answer is that there is not enough data, which is a statement about information content, not about effort.

4 · Close the block on the event timeline

  • Show the named El Niño and La Niña events. Point at 1998 and 2016.
  • Then say, without elaborating: remember that 1998 was a very strong El Niño. We will come back to it at 11:45. Do not explain why. The payoff in Block 6 is much better if they have been carrying the fact for three hours.

Break  ·  09:35 – 09:50

Block 3

A forcing you can compute exactly, and a response that ignores it

09:50 · 45 min  ·  protect this block

1 · Cenozoic context — 6 minutes, no more

  • The glacial cycles need a cold planet, and the planet had to become one. Greenhouse to icehouse over 65 Myr: gateways, uplift, weathering, declining CO2.
  • Two threshold crossings: Antarctic ice at ~34 Ma, Northern Hemisphere at ~2.7 Ma.
  • Then the sentence that matters and is usually missed: the orbital forcing did not switch on 2.7 Myr ago. The response did. The wobble had always been there; what appeared was an amplifier.
  • Reuse the boundary point from Block 1: over 65 Myr, CO2 is a forcing; over 100 kyr it is a feedback. Nothing physical changed, only the question. Third time you have said this. Say it anyway.

2 · The three parameters — 8 minutes on the diagram and the table

  • Eccentricity, obliquity, climatic precession. Ranges and periods on the board.
  • The key asymmetry to establish before the demo: obliquity is symmetric between the hemispheres, precession is antisymmetric. Northern summer at perihelion means southern summer at aphelion.
  • And the one that always surprises: eccentricity on its own is worth 0.5 W m−2 in the annual mean. Compare with 3.7 for a doubling. Its real job is to set the amplitude of precession.
  • Put the insolation integral up but do not derive it. Say only that there is no approximation in it, and that the demo evaluates it directly. Then point out that they already met the geometry in Lecture 2’s insolation explorer, which held the orbit fixed and moved through the year; this does the opposite.

3 · The calculator — 14 minutes, and follow the four steps on the page in order

  • Step one, the obliquity experiment. Press Obliquity 22.0°, then Use the current setting as reference, then Obliquity 24.5°. The anomaly field you are now showing is the deck’s Fig. 5.24a, computed live. Same sign in both hemispheres’ summers, growing polewards, and it changes the annual mean at high latitude.
  • Step two, the precession experiment. Back to today, then perihelion at NH winter solstice, set as reference, then perihelion at NH summer solstice. That is Fig. 5.24b. Opposite signs between hemispheres, opposite signs between seasons, and almost nothing in the annual mean.
  • Step three, and this is the one to make them predict first. Ask what happens if you set eccentricity to zero and repeat the precession experiment. Take a vote. Then do it: nothing happens. A circular orbit has no perihelion. Precession without eccentricity is not a forcing at all.
  • Step four. Press 127 ka. 65°N in June goes from 478 to about 546 W m−2. Box the number 68 on the board next to 3.7 and let the comparison sit there.
  • Then the sentence the whole page is built to earn: this is not a bigger version of the greenhouse forcing, it is a different kind of forcing — one that redistributes rather than adds. Which is why it needs an amplifier to produce a global signal, and why the amplifier is the interesting part.
Ask
The global annual mean insolation barely changes. So how can the orbit produce a 4 K global cooling? It cannot, on its own — and that is the correct answer, not a trick. The orbit sets where and when the energy arrives; ice albedo and the carbon cycle convert a seasonal, regional redistribution into a global change. If they get this, the rest of the block is easy. If they do not, do not proceed to the ice model until they do.

4 · The record, and the arithmetic that makes it ordinary — 5 minutes

  • Sawtooth shape. Long glacials, short interglacials. 100 kyr for the last 800 kyr, 41 kyr before about 1 Myr, and the orbit did not change at 1 Myr.
  • CO2 180 to 280 ppm: 90 ppm ≈ 2 W m−2. Make them check it against yesterday’s calculator in their heads. More than half a doubling.
  • Then the LGM sum, on the board, slowly: −2.2 (CO2) −1 (other GHG, dust) −3 (ice albedo) ≈ −6 W m−2 for −4 K. Run it back through yesterday’s λf and you get an entirely ordinary climate sensitivity. The glacial world is not different physics. It is the same physics with ice and carbon moved into the feedback column. This is the best single moment of continuity in the whole course — do not rush it.

5 · The ice model and the spectra — 12 minutes, and this is the best thing on the pages

  • Introduce Imbrie–Imbrie in one sentence: ice relaxes towards what the insolation implies, but it melts faster than it grows. That is the entire model.
  • Set the asymmetry to zero first. Show the output: a smoothed copy of the input, 23 and 41 kyr in, 23 and 41 kyr out, no sawtooth. Point at the two spectra and make them agree that the peaks are in the same places.
  • Now raise b slowly to 0.6 and say nothing while it happens. Two things change at once: the time series develops its sawtooth, and a peak grows in the right-hand spectrum at a period that is not in the left-hand one. Let the room notice it before you name it.
  • Then name it: a non-linear system does not merely amplify the frequencies you give it, it creates new ones. Read the percentages out of the readout so they have numbers.
  • And then take it away again. The caveat box is not optional: the model is too easy to believe, the eccentricity signal is far too weak energetically, and this is the unsolved 100-kyr problem. Real explanations need ice-sheet physics and CO2. Teaching the honest version of this is worth more than teaching the tidy one.
Ask
Where did the 90 ppm of CO2 go during a glaciation? Not the land — the land lost carbon as forests contracted. It has to be the ocean, which holds fifty times as much. Get two mechanisms out of the room: colder water is more soluble, and dust-borne iron fertilised the Southern Ocean. Then note that no single mechanism is enough and they do not simply add, and point at the table on the page. Close with the corollary that makes it matter: if the carbon cycle amplified orbital forcing then, it will amplify anthropogenic forcing now. Same β and γ feedbacks as yesterday, running the other way.

Block 4

Thresholds: when a slow forcing produces a fast response

10:45 · 35 min

1 · The observation first — 7 minutes

  • Twenty-five Dansgaard–Oeschger events. 8 to 16 K in Greenland in a few decades, sometimes one decade. Write the number and let it be shocking; it should be.
  • Walk the four-feature table and say what each one rules out. The one to dwell on is only during glacials — whatever the mechanism is, it needs ice sheets or a cold North Atlantic.
  • Heinrich events: ice-rafted debris layers, which no current could have carried. Icebergs, therefore fresh water, therefore the one thing the deep circulation cannot tolerate.
  • Do the chain of reasoning out loud, arrow by arrow, and stop on the middle link: the step that matters has a threshold.

2 · Why the conveyor can have two states — 6 minutes on the schematic

  • Cold and salty are both dense; in the North Atlantic they work together. The circulation carries heat north — of order 1 PW — and it carries salt north too.
  • That is the loop: flow brings salt, salt keeps the surface dense, dense surface keeps the flow. Positive feedback. Sign rule again, fourth time in two days.
  • And therefore: weaken it enough and the same loop runs the other way and holds it shut.
  • Flag the structural parallel explicitly — this is PC Lab 3, which they run after lunch, with vegetation and precipitation instead of salinity and overturning. Unrelated physics, identical mathematics. Tell them to look for it this afternoon.

3 · The Stommel demo — 12 minutes, and run the experiments in this order

  • Start on the equilibrium diagram at H = 0.10 Sv. Point at the two solid branches and the dashed one between them. Two stable states at the same forcing. Which one the system is in depends on its history, not on the forcing.
  • Drag H up slowly and watch the strong branch shorten and then end. Name the bifurcation, once, and move on.
  • Heinrich event: the pulse collapses the circulation, and when the pulse ends the forcing returns to exactly where it started and the circulation does not. Say that sentence deliberately. This is the memory of the system.
  • A smaller pulse: complete recovery. Below the threshold the response is reversible and roughly proportional. Which is why the threshold, not the sensitivity, is the interesting quantity.
  • Slow ramp up, then back down: the hysteresis loop. Collapse near 0.35, restart only near zero. The path up is not the path down.
  • Gradual increase, held: smooth monotonic forcing, near-vertical response. Land the headline: an abrupt change does not require an abrupt cause.
  • Then the caveat box, out loud: two boxes, no geography, no sea ice. Nobody knows the real threshold to within a factor of two, and some comprehensive models show no bistability at all today. Treat anyone who quotes a precise threshold for the real ocean with suspicion.

4 · The bipolar seesaw — 5 minutes, and it is the evidential clincher

  • Greenland has sharp sawteeth; Antarctica has smooth rounded maxima that peak when Greenland jumps. Show it, then explain it: the Southern Ocean is a reservoir being filled and drained, so it responds to the time integral.
  • Drag τ down to 200 years and show that the two records become mirror images — which is not what the ice cores show. Then back to 1100. One equation, and the observed phasing falls out.
  • Say why this matters evidentially: this phase relationship is not what a common global forcing would produce, and not what an atmospheric teleconnection would produce. It is the reason the ocean-circulation explanation is accepted rather than merely plausible.

5 · The deglaciation, and the lead–lag question — 5 minutes

  • Sequence, on the schematic: insolation rises, ice retreats; Southern Ocean and CO2 rise together from ~18 ka; meltwater interrupts the north twice; Younger Dryas 12.9–11.7 ka and it ends in decades; then the mechanism runs out of fuel.
  • Raise the lead–lag objection yourself if nobody has. “CO2 lags temperature, therefore it cannot cause warming.” Grant the premise partly, then reject the inference: at the start of a deglaciation CO2 is a feedback; once released it is a forcing on everything else, and the global mean lags it.
  • Then the modern contrast in one line: today the CO2 is being added from outside the system, which is the definition of a forcing from Block 1. The situations are not analogous, and the difference is exactly the definition they learned at 08:25.
Block 5

A world like ours: how big is natural variability really?

11:20 · 18 min  ·  cut here if you must

1 · The Holocene in four minutes

  • It begins with northern summer insolation near its maximum and spends 10,000 years losing it. Thermal optimum 9–6 ka, stronger monsoons, green Sahara, then neoglacial cooling.
  • The Sahara collapse: gradual monotonic forcing, abrupt vegetation and rainfall response. Point back at Block 4 and forward at the afternoon lab. Third appearance of the same mathematics.

2 · The proxy-sensitivity demo — 5 minutes, and it settles a real argument

  • Click the five buttons in order without much commentary: summer 65°N (+37 at 9 ka), summer 45°N, annual 65°N (almost nothing), global annual (hundredths of a watt), winter 65°N (opposite sign).
  • Then the conclusion: all four proxies are correct. The disagreement between reconstructions showing a Holocene optimum and models showing none was never about the climate; it was about what the word “warmer” refers to.
  • Generalise it, because this is the transferable skill: whenever you are shown a palaeo comparison, ask what season and what area each side is reporting before asking which one is right.

3 · The last millennium ensemble — 8 minutes, and do the four steps in order

  • Start on volcanic + solar, forced response only. A cool 1450–1850, a mild Medieval interval, a couple of tenths of a degree. Right sign, right size.
  • Switch to hemispheric mean: the forced signal survives but members have cold decades the forcing did not put there.
  • Switch to one region: the noise triples and members acquire their own Little Ice Ages in the wrong centuries. Say it plainly: that is regional asynchrony, and you are watching it being generated. This is the deck’s Fig. 5.37 and Fig. 5.38, simulated.
  • Now click Anthropogenic. The twentieth century separates from everything before it, in every member, in every region. Let that be the last thing on the screen before Block 6.
  • Then the three reading habits, quickly: compare the ensemble mean with the reconstruction, compare the spread with its wiggles, and never match an unforced event by date.
Show
If time allows, put up the deck’s Fig. 5.37 (PAGES2k, seven continental regions) immediately after the one-region run. The point lands twice: once as a simulation, once as data.
Ask
The Medieval Climate Anomaly was renamed from “Medieval Warm Period”. Why? Because it was not uniformly warm. Regions were anomalous, in different directions, at different times — which is the signature of internal variability rather than of a forcing. Then the sentence that sets up Block 6: a forced signal is in phase everywhere, because the forcing acts on the whole planet at once. Synchrony is therefore itself a measurement of how much of a signal is forced.
Block 6

Putting it together: detection and attribution

11:38 · 17 min  ·  do not compress

1 · The record and the CO₂ evidence — 4 minutes

  • 0.89 °C over 1901–2012 on the board. Three independent datasets. Rapid rise to 1940, plateau to the mid-1970s, steeper since. Faster over land, fastest in the Arctic.
  • Land versus ocean in one line each, and both are callbacks: ocean heat capacity (Lecture 6’s transient response) and limited evaporation over land (Lecture 3’s Bowen ratio).
  • Then the CO2-origin table, fast: mass balance, 13C, 14C, declining O2, ocean acidifying. Five independent lines, each of which would have to be wrong in a different way and in a direction agreeing with the other four. That is why the word is unequivocal.
  • And immediately: keep two claims apart. “The CO2 rise is anthropogenic” is settled by that table. “The warming is anthropogenic” is a different claim and needs the next ten minutes.

2 · The two definitions and the regression — 3 minutes

  • Detection: inconsistent with internal variability alone. Names no cause. Attribution: consistent with a particular combination and inconsistent with the alternatives. Both halves required.
  • Write T(t) = Σ βi Respi(t) + u(t) and explain the trick: models supply the shape, observations supply the amplitude. If a model warms too much its β comes out below one; the conclusion does not depend on the model having the right sensitivity.
  • Thirty seconds on why fingerprints are spatial: greenhouse warming cools the stratosphere and warms nights; solar warming does neither. That is the answer to the question you parked at 08:55 — go back and point at it.

3 · The fingerprint demo — 8 minutes, and this is the end of the lecture

  • Try natural forcings only. The volcanic dips fit beautifully; the trend is untouched; the residual is 0.34 K against an internal variability of 0.11 and it is structured, not noise. That is a detection result, and let them see it as one.
Show
Immediately after the natural-only run, put up the deck’s Fig. 5.42 (Jones et al., 2013) — observations against models with natural forcings only, and against models with natural and anthropogenic forcings. This is the single most important slide in the lecture and the pages deliberately do not reproduce it, because it is real observational data and a real multi-model ensemble. Say the sentence explicitly: you have just done by hand, with three curves and a slider, what that figure does with dozens of models. Leave it up while you finish the demonstration — the fitted result on the screen and the published result on the wall should be visible together.
  • Now raise βGHG alone until the end matches. The early century is too warm and the plateau has gone. Bring in βaerosol and both come right. The aerosol term is what makes the shape work.
  • Press Compute the least-squares fit. Residual falls to about 0.12 — indistinguishable from internal variability — and 88 % of variance is explained.
  • Now the error bars, and read them carefully, because this is where students go wrong. βGHG well determined and far from zero: detection. βaerosol with a wide range: the two responses have similar shapes and the data cannot fully separate them. βnatural also well determined — and that is not a result about the warming, it is well determined because the volcanic dips are sharp. Multiply it by the natural trend and you get nothing.
  • Press Draw a new realisation once or twice so they see the coefficients move a little and the conclusion not move at all.
  • Finish with the cherry-picking widget and the 1998 payoff. Set 1998 to 2012 and read the trend. Then say: both ends of that window are things we looked at three hours ago — a very strong El Niño and a La Niña. The arithmetic is correct and the conclusion is worthless. The defence is not to distrust the arithmetic; it is to ask whether the window was chosen before or after looking at the data.
Ask
If a model had twice the true climate sensitivity, would this method give the wrong answer? No — its fingerprint would be twice too large and β would come out near 0.5. The detection conclusion is unaffected. This is the single best question you can leave them with, because it is the reason attribution is more robust than projection. If nobody asks it, ask it yourself.

Appendix — what on the pages is data, and what is not

There is a published appendix for this, and you should point students at it once during the lecture. past-present/provenance.html lists every figure and demonstration in the six pages and classifies it as computed, schematic, real values or not reproduced, with its provenance. Every page footer links to it. Thirty seconds in Block 1 is enough: say that the pages contain no observational datasets by construction, that the appendix says what each figure actually is, and that noticing the difference is a skill the course wants them to have.

Why the pages are built this way. They are self-contained HTML that loads no external data, so they cannot plot EPICA or HadCRUT. Faced with that there are three honest options and one dishonest one, and the dishonest one — a curve that looks like a measurement and is not — is used nowhere. The rule applied throughout, and the one to apply to anything added later:

OptionWhenWhat it must carry
Compute it The physics is closed-form, or a published conceptual model exists. Parameters stated in the grey foot note; anything tuned said to be tuned.
Labelled schematic The shape or sequence is the teaching point and the data are unavailable. A warning drawn inside the SVG, and normally no numeric value axis.
Real values in prose Published numbers, protocol values, event dates. Sourced in the foot note.
Don’t draw it Observational maps and proxy records. Described in text; you show the original from the deck.

The four in-figure warnings students will see, so you are not caught out by a question: SCHEMATIC — shape and sequence only, not ice-core data (D–O figure); SCHEMATIC — sequence and relative timing, not data (deglaciation); SCHEMATIC — an energy-balance reconstruction, not an observational dataset (page 6 global mean); IDEALISED orbital synthesis — dominant periods only, not the Berger (1978) solution (page 3), plus synthetic — character only, not data on the mode indices.

If asked
“So is any of this real?” Yes, and be specific rather than defensive. The insolation calculator is exact and its presets are the international protocol values. The Stommel branches, the Imbrie–Imbrie run, the spectra and the attribution regression are all genuine calculations done live in front of them. What is not reproduced is observational data — and the reason is that a hand-built page cannot plot it honestly, so it does not pretend to. Then turn it into the teaching point: the useful habit is to ask of any figure whether it is a measurement, a calculation or a drawing. Most people never ask, and it is the single cheapest defence against being misled.

Questions that will come, and short answers

QuestionAnswer to give
“Wasn’t it warmer in the Medieval period?” In some regions, at some times, by a few tenths of a degree in the hemispheric mean — and the regions were not warm simultaneously. The last thirty years are likely the warmest thirty-year period in at least 1400 years in the Northern Hemisphere. Point at Block 5.
“The climate has always changed.” Agree, enthusiastically, and say that this is why the whole morning was spent measuring by how much and how fast. Then give the number: a few tenths of a degree per century naturally, about a degree in the twentieth century, several projected. The premise is right and it argues the other way.
“What about the Sun?” Included, as a fingerprint, in Block 6. Its total variation over the last millennium is about 0.2 W m−2 against 2.7 anthropogenic today, and its vertical fingerprint is the wrong shape — solar warming heats the stratosphere, greenhouse warming cools it.
“Is the AMOC going to collapse?” Models mostly project weakening, not collapse, this century; they disagree with each other and with the palaeo record about how easily the real ocean switches; and the honest answer is that nobody knows the threshold to within a factor of two. Say that the uncertainty is a reason for caution, not comfort.
“Wasn’t there a pause in warming?” Run the cherry-picking widget. Then the two-part answer: internal variability of the size measured in Block 1 can flatten any fifteen-year window, and both ends of the usual one are ENSO extremes.
“How do you know the ice-core temperatures?” Water isotopes calibrated against modern spatial relationships, plus borehole thermometry as an independent check on the calibration. Say that the calibration is the hard part and that it is why magnitudes are less certain than timings.
