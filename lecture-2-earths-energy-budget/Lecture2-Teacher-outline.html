The Earth’s energy budget — lecture outline

A running order for the Tuesday 08:15–12:00 slot, with the point to make at each step, the number to put on the board, which interactive page to open, and the question to throw back at the room. Built from Lecture 2 — The Earth’s Energy Budget (77 slides) and the seven teaching pages that go with it.

The one sentence the whole session is built on

The planet must radiate away exactly what it absorbs — and every single thing in this lecture is that statement applied to a different box: to the whole planet, to a layer of the atmosphere, to a circle of latitude, to the ground under your feet.

Say it at the start and land it four times. Around the whole planet it gives Te = 255 K. Around a layer of atmosphere it gives the greenhouse effect. Around a latitude band it gives the circulation. Around the globe at the end it gives the +0.6 W m−2 that defines this century. Everything else is detail hung on that frame.

The second thread, worth naming explicitly once. The greenhouse effect is not a blanket that traps heat. It is a change of altitude: the planet radiates to space from a level about 5 km up, that level is 33 K colder than the ground, and adding CO2 raises it. Students who leave with the blanket metaphor cannot explain why the stratosphere cools, why the effect saturates logarithmically, or why an isothermal atmosphere would have no greenhouse effect at all. Say the altitude version at least three times.

Where the time actually goes. Blocks 2 and 3 are the examinable core and must not be rushed. Block 5 is the one to cut into if you are behind — the orbital geometry survives being pointed at rather than derived, and it is revisited in the palaeoclimate lecture. Do not sacrifice the closing demonstration; it is the only point in the session where the students do the arithmetic.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the session students should be able to:

  • Place a body on the electromagnetic spectrum from its temperature, using Wien’s law.
  • Apply Stefan–Boltzmann correctly — in kelvin, with emissivity, to a stated area.
  • Split incident radiation into transmitted, reflected and absorbed, and define a black body.
  • Derive S0 from the Sun’s luminosity and explain the factor of four.
  • Derive Te = 255 K and say precisely what that temperature is and is not.
  • Build the one-layer and grey (ε) atmosphere models and solve both.
  • Explain the greenhouse effect as a shift in the emission level, not as trapped heat.
  • Explain why absorption bands and the atmospheric window make a single ε wrong.
  • State the role of convection and latent heat in closing the surface budget.
  • Explain the seasons from obliquity, and read a daily-mean insolation figure.
  • Explain a map of absorbed solar and of outgoing longwave, and why they nearly invert.
  • Use ∂Es/∂t = RTOA − Ftransport and say which term dominates on which timescale.
  • Explain maritime versus continental climate from heat capacity.
  • Close the global energy budget at all three levels, and recover a missing flux from the others.

Running order

Timings assume the Tuesday 08:15–12:00 slot with two short breaks. They are a shape, not a schedule. If the Monday afternoon slot ran long on Components of the Climate System, Blocks 1 and 2 are the ones that can be compressed — the Stefan–Boltzmann page carries them well enough for self-study.

Open

Framing: where does the energy come from?

08:15 · 10 min

Points

  • Open on the slide question and take answers before correcting: “Where do we get energy from?” (sun, fossil, wind, solar, nuclear) versus “Where does the Earth get energy from?”
  • Land the scale contrast at once: solar input 340 W m−2, geothermal 0.09, tidal 0.006, all human energy use 0.03. To three significant figures the Earth’s energy budget is sunlight.
  • Then the twist that makes the lecture necessary: the Earth is also emitting, all the time, and it is that second flux — not the first — that everything interesting depends on.
  • Write in = out on the board and leave it there for four hours.
  • Outline the four movements: radiation laws → the bare planet → the atmosphere → the geography.
Warm-up
Why is it cold at night, and why is it much less cold under cloud? Get to: the ground keeps radiating at σT4 after the sun stops arriving, so it cools; cloud radiates back down and nearly cancels the loss. That is the entire greenhouse effect in a sentence they already believe from experience, and you can point back at it in Block 3.
Block 1

Radiation: the spectrum, the law, and what a surface does with a beam

08:25 · 35 min

1 · Put four bodies on the spectrum

  • Run the slide exercise before teaching anything: arrange the Sun, a human, a wood flame and an electric heater on the EM spectrum. Let them argue.
  • Then give them the tool, not the answer: λmax = 2898 / T µm·K. Human 310 K → 9.3 µm. Heater 1100 K → 2.6 µm. Flame 1500 K → 1.9 µm. Sun 5772 K → 0.50 µm.
  • The point to land: everything with a temperature glows; the only question is where. We see the Sun’s glow and feel the rest.
  • Recap c = λν, E = hν = hc/λ quickly — shorter wavelength, more energetic photon. Thirty seconds; they have had it before.
Show
Thermal emitters — the four bodies side by side. Use it to check their answers after the argument, not to pre-empt it.

2 · Stefan–Boltzmann

  • F = εσT4. Insist on three things every time it is used: kelvin, per square metre, and ε.
  • Do one number on the board so the fourth power lands: double the temperature, sixteen times the flux.
  • If the group is mathematically comfortable, show in one line that σT4 is Planck’s law integrated over wavelength and over the outward hemisphere. If not, skip it and say so — do not half-derive it.
Show
Stefan–Boltzmann — the “Drag the temperature” slider. Sweep it from human to Sun and let the T4 do the talking.

3 · Transmissivity, reflectivity, absorptivity

  • ψ + α + ζ = 1 — energy conservation at an interface, nothing more. For an opaque body ψ = 0.
  • Black body: ζ = 1, α = 0, and by Kirchhoff it is therefore also the best possible emitter. Make that link explicitly — good absorber = good emitter is the step that later makes ε appear in both the greenhouse equations.
  • The green-leaf example is the one that sticks: chlorophyll absorbs about 85 % of the visible band but under 50 % of the near infrared. A leaf is nearly black to the light it uses and nearly white to the light it does not. That is why vegetation looks so bright in satellite near-IR, and it is the physical basis of NDVI.
  • Albedo: planetary αp = 0.30. Flag now that this single number hides an enormous geographic range — you will unpack it in Block 5.
Ask
Snow and a black asphalt road are both at 0 °C. Which one emits more infrared? Almost exactly the same — both have ε ≈ 0.95–0.99 in the thermal infrared. “White” and “black” are statements about the visible band only. This is the single most useful sentence in the whole radiation block, and it pre-empts half the confusion in Block 5.
Block 2

The bare planet: from the Sun’s luminosity to 255 K

09:00 · 30 min

1 · Where 1361 comes from

  • S0 = L☉ / 4πr2 = 3.83×1026 / (4π · (1.496×1011)2) = 1361 W m−2.
  • The point to land: nothing is lost on the way. The same power crosses every sphere; it is just spread over a bigger one. The flux has fallen by 46 000 since it left the surface of the Sun, and not one watt has gone missing.

2 · Absorbs on a disc, emits from a sphere — protect this

  • This is the step they get wrong in the exam. Do it slowly and make them say why.
  • Sunlight arrives as a parallel beam, so the Earth intercepts it over its shadow — a disc, πR2. It emits in all directions from its whole surface, 4πR2. Hence the factor of four, and hence 1361/4 = 340 W m−2.
  • Do the arithmetic on the board exactly as the slide does it, one line at a time: R2 = 4.06×1013 → disc 1.275×1014 m2 → 1.735×1017 W intercepted → ×0.7 = 1.214×1017 W absorbed.
  • Then the rhetorical pause from the slide: “Imagine the Earth just kept receiving that. What happens?” It has to get rid of it, and there is only one way to get rid of energy in a vacuum.
  • Set absorbed = emitted, cancel πR2, and solve: Te = [S0(1−αp)/4σ]1/4 = 255 K = −18 °C.
  • Observed surface: 288 K. The 33 K gap is the entire remaining agenda. Write it on the board and box it.
Show
Stefan–Boltzmann — sections “The same power, spread over a much bigger sphere” and “Absorbs on a disc, emits from a sphere”, then the Try it slider for albedo. Ask what albedo would be needed for 288 K before you touch the slider, then show them.
Ask
What exactly is 255 K the temperature of? Nothing, physically. It is the temperature a black body would need in order to emit the observed 239 W m−2. It is a bookkeeping temperature. Insisting on this now pays off in Block 4, where you discover that there is in fact a place in the atmosphere at 255 K — about 5 km up — and that this is not a coincidence.

break · 09:30 · 10 min

Block 3

The greenhouse effect: one slab, then a leaky one

09:40 · 35 min

1 · The two idealisations

  • Add one layer at temperature Ta, totally transparent to sunlight and totally opaque to infrared. State that these are lies and that you will remove them in Block 4.
  • Balance at the top: what leaves to space now comes from the layer, so σTa4 = (1−αp)S0/4 and therefore Ta = Te = 255 K.
  • Balance at the surface: it receives sunlight and the layer’s downward emission, so σTs4 = 2σTe4 and Ts = 21/4Te = 303 K (30 °C).
  • Too warm. Say so out loud. A model that overshoots is more instructive than one that is tuned, and they should see you treat a wrong answer as information.

2 · Let it leak

  • Give the layer an infrared emissivity ε, which by Kirchhoff is also its absorptivity. Two balances, two unknowns, and out comes Ts = [2/(2−ε)]1/4Te.
  • Check the two limits aloud: ε = 0 gives the bare rock, ε = 1 gives the opaque slab. A formula that reproduces both endpoints is worth trusting in between.
  • The slide uses ε = 0.97 → 301 K, still too warm. Do not paper over it. Set up the question: what would you have to add to bring it down?
Show
Why the surface is warmer than space — drag the ε slider live. Ask them to find the ε that gives 288 K (≈ 0.77) and then tell them the real atmosphere absorbs about 90 % of the surface’s infrared. The gap between 0.77 and 0.90 is the whole of Block 4.
Ask
The surface emits 398 W m−2 but only absorbs 161 of sunlight. Is that energy from nothing? No — 342 comes straight back down. The 398 is a gross flux, nearly cancelled by the one flowing the other way; the net is 56 upward, warm to cold, exactly as thermodynamics requires. This is the most common conceptual error in the lecture and it is worth spending three minutes on. It comes back in the closing demonstration.
Block 4

A real atmosphere: altitude, bands, and the half that is not radiation

10:15 · 40 min

1 · Vertical structure, in ninety seconds

  • Four layers, defined by which way the temperature goes, not by composition. Troposphere cools with height because it is heated from below; stratosphere warms with height because ozone heats it from within.
  • Two numbers to keep: Γ ≈ 6.5 K km−1, and 80 % of the mass below 11 km.

2 · The emission level — the centrepiece of the whole lecture

  • Ask it as a puzzle first: “Space sees 239 W m−2, which is 255 K. The ground is 288 K. So where is the 255 K?” Let them find it: ze = (288−255)/6.5 ≈ 5.1 km.
  • Now reverse it and say the sentence slowly: Ts = Te + Γze. The surface is warm because the exhaust pipe is high and the air below it lies on a slope.
  • Doubling CO2 raises ze by about 150 m; multiply by Γ and that is 1 K before feedbacks — which is exactly the textbook no-feedback sensitivity.
  • Then the payoff nobody expects: set Γ = 0. An isothermal atmosphere has no greenhouse effect at all, however opaque you make it, because raising the emission level no longer takes you anywhere colder. This is why the stratosphere cools while the troposphere warms — and why that pattern is a fingerprint no brightening Sun could produce.
Show
A real atmosphere · §3 — the two sliders. Do it in this order: Today → Double CO₂ (watch Ts move by 1 K) → Isothermal, Γ = 0 (watch the greenhouse effect vanish entirely). That third click is the moment of the lecture.

3 · Stack the slabs

  • Ts = (n+1)1/4Te — the chapter’s Exercise 1, and worth two minutes because it gives them the Venus intuition (737 K needs about a hundred layers).
  • Then puncture it: this model never saturates, because every layer is opaque at every wavelength. The real atmosphere has holes.
Show
A real atmosphere · §4 — the n slider, 0 to 6.

4 · Bands, and the atmospheric window

  • Absorption happens only where a photon matches a molecular vibration or rotation. Hence bands, and hence the near-transparent gap at 8–13 µm — sitting almost exactly on the peak of the 288 K Planck curve.
  • Two consequences, both examinable. Saturation: the 15 µm CO2 band centre is already full, so extra CO2 works only at the edges — which is why forcing is logarithmic and every doubling adds the same 3.7 W m−2. Band placement: methane’s 7.7 µm band sits where nothing else absorbs, which is why it is ~30× CO2 per molecule.
  • Ask why N2 and O2 are absent: symmetric diatomics have no dipole to couple to. The greenhouse effect is run entirely by trace gases.
Show
A real atmosphere · §5 — step through the gas buttons: H₂O first (it is everywhere), then CO₂, then O₃ to show the 9.6 µm band sitting in the middle of the window.

5 · Close the surface budget

  • The slide asks it: “What is meant by non-radiative exchanges?” Answer: conduction, convection (sensible heat, 20) and above all evaporation (latent heat, 84).
  • The point to land: of the 161 W m−2 the surface absorbs, about 104 leaves without radiating at all. Radiation-only models have no such escape route, which is exactly why they overshoot. Convection is not a correction to the radiative picture — it is what sets the 6.5 K km−1 you used in step 2.
  • Forward reference: the bulk formulas for these two fluxes are Lecture 3.

Block 5

Insolation, and the geography of what gets absorbed

11:05 · 25 min

1 · Geometry, told rather than derived

  • Three ingredients only: distance (Sr from the ellipse), zenith angle (Sh = Srcos θs), and day length. Everything in Figs. 2.6–2.10 is one of those three.
  • Obliquity 23°27′ causes the seasons. Say explicitly that distance does not: Earth is closest to the Sun in early January, and S0 swings only ±3.4 % over the year.
  • Use the slide question on Fig. 2.11 and make them work: “Why is the daily-mean maximum at the summer pole, not at the equator?” Because 24 hours of moderate sun beats 12 hours of intense sun. Day length wins.
Show
Insolation — the orbit and declination animation, then the daily-mean heat map. Let the polar-day ridge answer the question before you say it.

2 · Absorbed solar and outgoing longwave, and why they nearly invert

  • Two fields, two different controls: absorbed solar is set by albedo; outgoing longwave is set by the temperature of the emitting level. Keep the questions separate.
  • Run the slide’s Africa questions as a pair, because the answer is the same object seen twice. Low absorbed solar in the north (bright sand, α ≈ 0.35). High outgoing longwave in the north (hot, dry, cloud-free, window wide open). Minimum outgoing longwave at the equator, because the satellite is looking at cloud tops at −60 °C.
  • The result worth stating baldly: the Sahara loses about 45 W m−2 at the top of the atmosphere. A desert is not a place with an energy surplus — it has a water deficit, which is an entirely different thing.
  • “Properties of the atmosphere” in the slide caption means precisely three things: cloud cover and cloud-top height, water vapour, aerosols.
Show
The geography of the budget · §3–4 — the six-site bar chart. Switch Absorbed solar → Outgoing longwave → Net and let them watch the Sahara travel from near the bottom to the top and back to the bottom. Then the zonal figure with the surplus and deficit shading.
Block 6

Storage and transport

11:30 · 20 min

1 · One equation, two regimes

  • ∂Es/∂t = RTOA − Ftransport. On seasonal timescales storage wins; on the annual mean storage nearly vanishes and transport must carry everything.
  • Storage is entirely heat capacity: ocean : land ≈ 100 : 1 per square metre, and the whole atmosphere holds less heat than the top 2.5 m of ocean.
  • Use the slide’s July−January map: over 40 K in Siberia, under 6 K over the North Pacific, at the same latitude. Nothing about the radiation differs. It is all what is underneath.
  • The phase lag comes free with the same equation — it is why the warmest month at the coast is August, not June.
Show
The geography of the budget · §6 — press Open ocean, 50 m then Continental interior back to back. Both the height and the horizontal shift of the blue curve change, and both are the same physics.

2 · Six petawatts, and where they come from

  • Make the methodological point, because it is the kind of thing that makes a lecture stick: nobody measures the heat transport. It is inferred by integrating the satellite radiation imbalance from one pole. Peak roughly 5.5 PW near 35–40°, about 300 times all human energy use.
  • Then show the closure error deliberately — the integral does not return to zero at the far pole, because the radiation data do not average to zero. Real analyses have exactly this problem and adjust it exactly this way.
  • Split: atmosphere dominates polewards of 30°, ocean peaks near 15–20° N. The slide’s Atlantic question: the Atlantic carries heat northward at every latitude, including in the Southern Hemisphere, because of the AMOC — which is why the Atlantic ITCZ sits north of the equator and why northern Europe is anomalously warm.
  • Finish with the anomaly bars: Bergen is +9.6 K above its own zonal mean and Yakutsk is −7.8 K below it, at the same latitude and the same annual sunlight.

Close

The demonstration: close the ledger

11:50 · 10 min

Run it as an exercise, not a summary

  • Put up the full budget diagram. Do not read the numbers out. Take the three balances in this order, using the highlight buttons: top of atmosphere (340 in, 100 + 199 + 40 out), surface (161 + 342 in, 398 + 84 + 20 out), atmosphere (79 + 358 + 84 + 20 in, 199 + 342 out).
  • Land the atmosphere balance hardest, because it closes the loop back to Block 4: the atmosphere absorbs only 79 of sunlight but 358 of infrared from the ground plus 104 of turbulent heat. The atmosphere is heated from below. That is why temperature falls with height, which is why the greenhouse effect works.
  • Then hide a flux and make them find it. Two or three rounds, thirty seconds each, hands up. This is the only point in four hours where they do the arithmetic, so protect the time for it.
  • The slide question — “what is the most important difference from Fig. 2.5?” — answers itself once the diagram is on screen: 104 W m−2 of non-radiative flux that the slab model does not have.
  • Finish on the residual: +0.6 W m−2, a quarter of one per cent of the flux passing through, 91 % of it going into the ocean. Every arrow on that diagram is uncertain by more than 0.6 — which is why the planet’s energy imbalance is measured by Argo floats, not by satellites. Then the four summary points from the last slide.

Things that reliably go wrong

  • The factor of four goes missing. They divide by 2, or not at all. Make them say “disc in, sphere out” aloud in Block 2 and it survives to the exam.
  • Celsius in Stefan–Boltzmann. Cheap to prevent, expensive to fix. Say “kelvin” every single time you write σT4.
  • “The greenhouse effect traps heat.” The blanket metaphor is worse than useless because it cannot explain stratospheric cooling, logarithmic saturation, or the Γ = 0 case. Replace it with the altitude story every time it appears.
  • “Back radiation sends heat from cold to hot.” Gross versus net. The 398/342 pair is the antidote and it is on the closing diagram.
  • Confusing albedo with emissivity. Snow is white in the visible and black in the infrared. The snow-versus-asphalt question in Block 1 pre-empts this.
  • Thinking the seasons are about distance. Perihelion is in January. Give them the number before they give you the misconception.
  • Reading an outgoing-longwave map as a surface-temperature map. It is a map of emission-level temperature, which is why the wettest, warmest place on Earth is a minimum.
  • “Deserts are hot because they get a lot of energy.” The Sahara runs a top-of-atmosphere deficit. It is hot because subsiding air keeps it dry and cloudless, not because it is being given more energy.
  • Treating Te = 255 K as a physical surface temperature rather than a bookkeeping device — and then being unable to explain what changed when the atmosphere arrived.
