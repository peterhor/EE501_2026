Why is the surface warmer than space?

Part 2. Same law as before — A↑ = σT⁴ — applied to three progressively more realistic pictures of the planet: a bare rock with no atmosphere, a blanket that blocks all infrared, and a blanket that only partly blocks it. Each step adds one idea and one equation, following Goosse et al. (2010), §2.1.1–2.1.2, Eq. 2.1–2.13.

1 · No atmosphere 2 · Opaque blanket 3 · Leaky blanket (ε) 4 · Compare 5 · Real Earth 6 · Limits

1 · Baseline — a planet with no atmosphere at all

One number: the effective emission temperature Te

Strip away the atmosphere entirely. The bare planet absorbs sunlight over its shadow disc πR² and radiates as a blackbody from its whole sphere 4πR² (the same "factor of four" derived on the previous page). Setting absorbed = emitted gives the planet's effective emission temperature:

A↑ = σTe4    (2.1) Stefan–Boltzmann for a blackbody (ε = 1) — this equation defines Te
πR²(1−αp)S₀ = 4πR²σTe4    (2.2)   ⟹   Te = [ S₀(1−αp) / 4σ ]1/4    (2.3–2.4) R cancels — Te does not depend on planet size, only on S₀, αp and σ
Te is not a temperature you could measure anywhere. It is the temperature a perfect blackbody would need in order to radiate away exactly as much energy as the planet absorbs. It is also the temperature the surface would have, uniformly, if there were no atmosphere at all (Goosse et al. 2010, p. 27). Real measured surface temperature is 288 K — 33 K warmer. Explaining that gap is the rest of this page.

2 · Add an atmosphere that blocks all infrared

The simplest possible greenhouse model

Now wrap the planet in a single, well-mixed atmospheric layer at temperature Ta, with two idealised properties: it is totally transparent to sunlight (shortwave) and totally opaque to infrared (longwave). This is the textbook's first greenhouse model (Goosse et al. 2010, §2.1.2, Fig. 2.4) — every photon the surface emits upward is absorbed by the layer.

Top of atmosphere: because the layer is opaque, all the outgoing radiation to space comes from the atmosphere itself, not from the surface. Balance at the top requires:

σTa4 = ¼(1−αp)S₀ = σTe4    (2.5) ⟹ Ta = Te = 255 K — the layer sits at exactly the planet's emission temperature

At the surface: the ground absorbs both the sunlight coming through the transparent atmosphere and the infrared radiated back down by the layer above it:

σTs4 = ¼(1−αp)S₀ + σTa4    (2.6)

Substituting Eq. 2.5 into Eq. 2.6 — both terms on the right are now σTe4:

σTs4 = σTe4 + σTe4    (2.7)   ⟹   Ts = 21/4Te = 1.19 Te    (2.8)
303 K is 15 K too warm. The textbook attributes this to "some crude approximations in this simple model," and it is worth being precise about which one dominates. Making the blanket leaky (Section 3) helps only slightly — even a realistically leaky atmosphere still lands near 296–301 K. The bigger omission is that this model moves energy away from the surface only by radiation, when in reality convection and evaporation carry most of it. Section 5 quantifies that.

3 · A leaky blanket: giving the atmosphere an emissivity ε

Let the atmosphere absorb only a fraction ε of the infrared

Real greenhouse gases don't absorb every outgoing infrared photon. Give the single layer an infrared emissivity ε (0 ≤ ε ≤ 1) — by Kirchhoff's law this is also its infrared absorptivity. A fraction ε of the surface's upward radiation σTs4 is absorbed and the remaining (1−ε) passes straight through to space. The layer, being a greybody at its own temperature Ta, emits εσTa4 upward and εσTa4 downward (Goosse et al. 2010, §2.1.2, Fig. 2.5).

σTs4 = ¼(1−αp)S₀ + εσTa4    (2.9) surface balance: absorbed sunlight, plus the layer's downward greybody emission
¼(1−αp)S₀ = εσTa4 + (1−ε)σTs4 = σTe4    (2.10) top-of-atmosphere balance: what leaves to space is the layer's own emission plus the surface radiation that escaped through the gap

Solving Eqs. 2.9–2.10 together (they share the unknowns Ts and Ta):

Ts = [ 2 / (2−ε) ]1/4 Te    (2.11–2.12)        Ta = [ 1 / (2−ε) ]1/4 Te    (2.13) ε = 0 recovers Section 1 (Ts = Te, no atmosphere); ε = 1 recovers Section 2 (Ts = 21/4Te)

A typical value, ε ≈ 0.97, gives Ts ≈ 301 K (28 °C). Still 13 K above the observed 288 K — because a single well-mixed layer is itself a crude stand-in for a real atmosphere with a temperature and humidity profile, convective heat transport, and latent heat exchange, none of which this radiative-only model includes (Goosse et al. 2010, p. 29). See Section 5.

5 · What the single layer leaves out

The observed global energy budget

Trenberth et al. (2009), reproduced as Goosse et al. (2010) Fig. 2.19, measured the actual global- and annual-mean flows. They look like the single-layer diagram in shape, but with the numbers spread across more pathways than ε alone can represent:

341 W/m² incoming solar (S₀/4)
102 W/m² reflected (αp ≈ 0.30 × 341)
161 W/m² solar absorbed at the surface
78 W/m² solar absorbed directly by the atmosphere
396 W/m² emitted upward by the surface, σTs⁴ at Ts = 288 K
40 W/m² of that escapes straight to space (the "atmospheric window")
333 W/m² back-radiation returned to the surface
17 + 80 W/m² leave the surface as sensible + latent heat, not radiation
239 W/m² outgoing longwave at the top — balancing the 239 W/m² absorbed
Two things the ε-model cannot capture — and only one of them matters much. First, the window: 396 − 40 = 356 W/m² of the surface's radiation is absorbed by the atmosphere, i.e. an effective ε ≈ 0.90, not 0.97. But feeding ε = 0.90 back into Eq. 2.12 gives Ts = 296 K — better, still 8 K too warm. Second, and this is the real culprit: of the 161 W/m² the surface must shed to balance its absorbed sunlight, 97 W/m² (60%) leaves as sensible heat (thermals) and evaporation — Eq. 2.33–2.34 — and only 63 W/m² as net radiation. A radiation-only model has no such escape route, so it must raise Ts until σTs⁴ alone carries the whole load. Convection and evaporation cool the surface for free, and that is most of the missing 8–13 K.

6 · The fine print

  • "Emissivity" here is a single number standing in for a spectrum. Real greenhouse gases absorb strongly in some infrared bands and barely at all in others (the atmospheric window, ≈8–13 µm). Replacing the single ε with a wavelength-resolved, multi-level radiative transfer calculation is the next step in realism, and is what climate models actually do.
  • One layer means one atmospheric temperature. The real atmosphere cools with altitude (the lapse rate, set mostly by convection, not radiation) and that vertical structure is itself what makes the greenhouse effect work: the level that radiates to space is colder than the surface precisely because it sits higher up.
  • Sensible and latent heat fluxes are absent. Eq. 2.33–2.34 carry 97 of the 161 W/m² the surface must shed. Leaving them out is the single biggest reason the simple model overshoots the observed Ts.
  • The chapter's Exercise 1 generalises this to n layers: stack n blackbody layers in radiative equilibrium and Ts keeps climbing with each one added — a crude but instructive analogue of thickening the blanket. (Worth deriving; the ε = 1 case above is n = 1.)
  • Balance still holds exactly at the top of the atmosphere — that is the "Check" tile in Section 3, and it is why Te = 255 K is untouched by any of this. Warm the surface as much as you like with a thicker blanket: the planet must still shed 239 W/m² to space on average, or it is not in equilibrium.
