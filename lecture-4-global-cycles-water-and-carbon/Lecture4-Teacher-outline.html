Global cycles: water and carbon — lecture outline

A running order for the Wednesday 08:15–12:00 slot, with the point to make at each step, the number to put on the board, which interactive page to open, and the question to throw back at the room. Built from Lecture 4 — Global cycles of water and carbon and the four teaching pages that go with it.

The one sentence the whole session is built on

Every cycle in this lecture is the same equation — dS/dt = in − out — applied to a different box. What distinguishes one reservoir from another is not the equation but the residence time, and residence time is what decides whether a perturbation is a blip or something the planet keeps.

Say it at the start, and then land it four times: the atmosphere holds nine days of water and so cannot force anything; the ocean holds three thousand years and so remembers everything; CO2 has no chemical sink and so accumulates; CH4 has one and so can be turned off. Everything else in the four hours is detail hung on that frame.

The second thread, worth naming explicitly once. Both cycles are enormous fluxes that very nearly cancel. 486 units of water evaporate and 486 fall. 203 PgC leaves the atmosphere each year and about 201 comes back. We are not overwhelming either cycle — we are biasing it by a few per cent, one way, for two centuries. That is a different and much less intuitive kind of problem, and students who leave with only "humans emit a lot of CO2" have missed it.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the session students should be able to:

  • Apply the reservoir balance equation to any box and derive the runoff and E−P relations from it.
  • Compute a residence time from a stock and a flux, and say what it does and does not tell you.
  • State roughly where the world’s water is, and why availability is the binding constraint.
  • Explain the soil water balance and distinguish water-limited from energy-limited evapotranspiration.
  • Read an E − P field and connect it to sea surface salinity and to ocean circulation.
  • Write down the global carbon budget and identify which term is missing from any given version of it.
  • Define the airborne fraction and say why it has stayed near 45 %.
  • Explain why carbonate chemistry, not solubility, lets the ocean hold so much carbon.
  • Distinguish DIC and alkalinity, and predict how each responds to adding CO2 or to shell formation.
  • Describe the three pumps and explain why one of them works against the other two.
  • Trace the cascade GPP → NPP → NEP → NBP and say what is subtracted at each step.
  • Explain the weathering thermostat and its timescale.
  • Explain why methane responds to emissions cuts and CO2 does not.

Running order

The 08:15–12:00 slot is four 45-minute teaching segments with three 15-minute breaks — 180 minutes of teaching, not 225. Breaks fall at 09:00, 10:00 and 11:00. Earlier versions of this running order assumed only two breaks and declared 200 minutes of teaching, which does not fit; the clocks below have been corrected and the block durations cut to match. Block 3 is the one to protect — the budget arithmetic and its missing term is the single most examinable thing in the session. Block 5 is the one to cut into if you are running long; the geological cycle survives being told rather than derived.

How this maps onto the slide deck. The 2026 deck reorganises these six blocks into four parts, one per quarter and one per teaching page: Part 1 is the Open plus Blocks 1 and 2 (page 1), Part 2 is Block 3 (page 2), Part 3 is Block 4 (page 3), and Part 4 is Blocks 5 and 6 plus the Close (page 4). That grouping is what lets every quarter end on a break and on a QR code, and it is why the deck gives the carbon budget a whole 45 minutes where the six-block shape below can only give it 29. If you are teaching from the deck, follow its four parts; the blocks below are the same material with the same clocks.

Open

Framing: two cycles, one equation

08:15 · 8 min

Points

  • Previous sessions were about energy. Today is about mass — where two particular substances are, and how fast they move between the places they can be.
  • Write dS/dt = inputs − outputs on the board and leave it there for four hours. Every diagram today is a special case of it.
  • Introduce the two quantities that will be asked of every reservoir: how big (the stock) and how fast (the residence time). Stress that they are independent — big and slow, small and fast, and every combination.
  • Outline the four sections: the hydrological cycle → the carbon budget → the ocean → land, rock and methane.
Warm-up
A glass of water and a glass of Coca-Cola are left open on the windowsill for a week. What is left in each? Get to: the water evaporates entirely; the Coke goes flat first and then evaporates. Two different substances, two different escape routes, two different timescales — and the fizz going first is quite literally today’s ocean chemistry running backwards. Come back to it in Block 4.
Block 1

The global water balance and residence time

08:23 · 37 min

1 · The figure, one arrow at a time

  • Put up Fig. 2.21 (the interactive version is cleaner). Do not read all the numbers out. Take the four vertical arrows first: ocean 413 up / 373 down, land 73 up / 113 down.
  • Ask the room to spot the pattern before you say it: each pair fails to balance by exactly 40, in opposite directions. Then reveal the two horizontal arrows that close it.
  • The point to land: 86 % of the water cycle never touches land. It is an ocean cycle with a small terrestrial branch. The 40 that crosses the coastline is the entire renewable freshwater supply of the continents.
  • Units housekeeping: reservoirs in 103 km3, fluxes in 103 km3 yr−1. Worth 20 seconds; saves confusion later.
Show
Page 1 · The global water balance — use the Highlight switch. Go Ocean loop → Land loop → The net 40 in that order, and let the caption under the diagram carry the argument each time.
Ask (from the slides)
Where in this figure is condensation included? Nowhere explicitly — it is hidden inside the precipitation arrows. The figure is a mass balance between reservoirs, and condensation is a phase change within the atmospheric reservoir, not a transfer between reservoirs. Good follow-up: so where is the latent heat release in this diagram? Also nowhere — and it is the single largest energy transfer in the climate system. This diagram deliberately shows only half the story.

2 · Where the water is

  • 96.5 % ocean. Of the fresh remainder, about 70 % is ice and most of the rest is groundwater.
  • Fresh, liquid and accessible is well under 0.1 % of the total. That is the sentence for the exam.
  • Connect forward: the reason this matters for ecology is not the global number but the distribution, which Block 2 shows is set by atmospheric circulation.

3 · Residence time — the core idea of the whole lecture

  • τ = S / Fout. Derive it out loud: at steady state the whole reservoir is replaced once every S/F years.
  • Do two on the board and let the page do the rest. Atmosphere: 12.7 / 486 = 0.026 yr = 9.5 days. Ocean: 1 335 040 / 413 = 3200 years.
  • The point to land: the atmosphere holds one thousandth of one per cent of the water and turns it over forty times a year. The ocean holds 96.5 % and turns over once every three millennia. So the atmosphere is where the cycle responds; the ocean is where it remembers.
  • Flag now that this reasoning will fail for CO2 in Block 3, and that the way it fails is the most important asymmetry in the course. Planting the flag here makes the payoff land.
Show
Page 1 — the residence-time strip. Nine days to nine thousand years on one logarithmic axis. Let them look at it for a moment before you say anything.
Ask
Water vapour rises 7 % per kelvin but precipitation only 2–3 %. What happens to τ? S rises faster than F, so τ increases — vapour stays airborne longer and therefore travels further before it falls. That is a large part of why the wet regions get wetter and the dry regions drier: moisture is being exported further from where it evaporated.
Block 2

Water on land, and E − P at the ocean surface

09:15 · 16 min

1 · Shrink the box to one field

  • dS/dt = P − ET − R − D — the same equation, fourth time of asking. This is Fig. 2.23.
  • Name the two regimes explicitly and make them stick: water-limited (ET is set by how much rain there is) and energy-limited (ET is set by how much radiation there is, and the surplus becomes runoff).
  • The aridity index P/PET is the number that tells you which you are in. Below about 0.65, water-limited.
  • Connect back to the previous lecture. ET here is FLH there. 1 mm of water = 2.45 MJ m−2. The Bowen ratio and the soil water balance are the same constraint in different units. Students consistently fail to see this unless it is said out loud.
Show — the demonstration to spend time on
Page 1 · the soil water bucket. A three-step routine that works well live. Read the four KPI tiles under the chart as you go rather than quoting numbers from here — the rainfall is generated, so the exact figures shift a little with the sliders. (1) Load Temperate oceanic. Storage rides near capacity most of the year and only dips toward the stress line in the driest summer spells; the aridity index is far above 1 and ET runs close to potential. Energy-limited — the surplus is going down the river, and adding rain would change the river, not the ET.
(2) Switch to Semi-arid without touching anything else. Storage collapses below the stress line for most of the year, ET falls to roughly a quarter of demand, and the runoff ticks nearly vanish. Water-limited — same equation, same model, opposite answer.
(3) Switch to Monsoon and let them watch storage fill in three months and drain for nine. Then ask what would happen to a plant that could root twice as deep — and drag the capacity slider to show them. Panel layout: rain hangs downward from the ceiling on its own scale, ET rises from the mid-line on a much finer one. Say so once — a wet day is twenty times a day’s ET and they cannot share an axis.
Ask
Doubling the soil water capacity raises annual ET without a drop more rain. Where did the water come from? From runoff and drainage. A bigger bucket intercepts water that would otherwise have left the same day and holds it until the plants can use it. Nothing was created; a flux was redirected. Good follow-up: so what does deep ploughing, or soil compaction, or losing topsoil do to a catchment’s water balance — and to its Bowen ratio?

2 · E − P and the salinity fingerprint

  • Fig. 2.24. Two maxima at 25°N and 25°S, minima at the ITCZ and the storm tracks.
  • The pattern is set by circulation, not by local energy supply. Subtropics are dry because of Hadley descent, not because they evaporate especially hard. Show E and P separately to prove it: E is comparatively flat, P does all the varying.
  • Then the payoff: switch to the salinity overlay. Salinity maxima sit on the E−P maxima. The ocean has been recording the water cycle for us, everywhere, continuously.
  • Close the loop to circulation: freshwater flux is a buoyancy flux, and buoyancy drives the overturning. Forward reference to PC Lab 3.
Show
Page 1 — the E − P panel, all three toggle states, in the order E − P → E and P separately → vs salinity.
Ask
Salty water got saltier and fresh water got fresher, with no change in the mean. What does that say? That the pattern of E − P has amplified without the global total changing much — an intensifying hydrological cycle. And that the ocean is a better rain gauge than the rain gauges: it integrates over years and covers 71 % of the planet, where the gauge network is sparse, land-only and biased.

break · 09:00–09:15

Block 3

The global carbon budget

09:31 · 29 min  ·  protect this one

1 · Start with the observation, not the theory

  • Keeling curve. 315 ppm in 1958, 425 ppm in 2024. A 35 % rise inside one human lifetime, measured continuously at one mountain by one instrument lineage.
  • Then the wiggle. Ask what it is before telling them. ~6 ppm at Mauna Loa, ~16 ppm at Barrow, ~1 ppm at the South Pole.
  • The amplitude follows the land, not the latitude. That is the whole answer, and it is worth extracting from the room rather than giving.
  • CH4: ~1930 ppb on the NOAA global mean. Note the slide says 1950 — that is closer to a northern station such as Mauna Loa, which runs above the global mean. Show the 1999–2006 pause and say you will explain it in Block 6.
Show
Page 2 · The global carbon budget — toggle the seasonal cycle off and on, then tick show the growth rate to make the point that the curve is not merely rising but steepening. Then the amplitude vs latitude panel with the land fraction behind it.

2 · The scale of the perturbation

  • Reservoirs first, on a log axis: crust 5×107, deep ocean 37 100, soils ~1950, atmosphere ~900, vegetation ~550 PgC.
  • The atmosphere is one of the smallest boxes. Which is exactly why it is the one that moves.
  • Then the fluxes on a linear axis, deliberately: GPP 123, respiration 119, ocean 80 each way — and human emissions 11. Let the visual do the work.
  • Say the sentence: a cycle that cancels to within 1 % is a cycle in which a 5 % bias accumulates relentlessly.

3 · The budget, and the missing term

  • Efossil + Eland-use = Gatm + Socean + Sland
  • Do the slide’s arithmetic on the board exactly as it appears: 123.3 − 119.8 = 3.5 land; 80.0 − 78.4 = 1.6 ocean; total uptake 5.1; fossil 7.8; so the atmosphere gains 2.7.
  • Then say: the measured growth for that decade was 4.0. Stop. Let it sit. This is the moment of the session — do not rescue them too quickly.
  • The missing term is land-use change, about 1.1 PgC yr−1. 2.7 + 1.1 = 3.8, and with rounding across the terms you recover 4.0.
  • Land-use change is not a rounding error. In the 1960s it was nearly half of total emissions. It is the term that makes deforestation a climate issue in its own right, not merely an ecological one.
Show — the demonstration to spend time on
Page 2 · the budget ledger. (1) Load Lecture arithmetic. The two columns balance and the atmosphere gets 2.7. State that the measured value was 4.0 and ask what is missing.
(2) Drag the land-use change slider from 0 to 1.1 and let them watch the atmospheric slab grow to 4.0 in real time. It is a five-second move and it is worth the whole build-up.
(3) Then step the era presets 1960s → 2014–2023 and ask them to watch the airborne fraction KPI. It barely moves while emissions triple. Ask why. That is the sinks doing an enormous amount of unpaid work — and it is the cue for Blocks 4 and 5.
Ask
Why is the airborne fraction roughly constant at 45 % while emissions have tripled? Because both sinks are driven by the atmospheric partial pressure, so they have grown roughly in proportion to the emissions. Follow up with the important half: nothing guarantees it continues. The ocean’s buffer capacity falls as it fills (Block 4) and the land sink weakens as respiration rises with temperature (Block 5). A rising airborne fraction means the atmosphere climbs faster with no change in emissions at all.

4 · Why carbon is not water — pay off the Block 1 flag

  • Molecular residence time of CO2: 900 / 203 ≈ 4 years. Write it on the board next to the atmosphere’s 9.5 days for water and let the parallel look convincing for a moment.
  • Then break it: the number is true and it tells you nothing. For every molecule that leaves, one comes back. Only the imbalance matters, so the quantity does not decay on 4 years — it decays on 10–100 years (surface ocean and biosphere), then ~1000 (deep ocean), and the last fifth on 105 years (weathering, Block 5).
  • The consequence worth stating slowly: water vapour rains out in nine days, so its concentration is set by temperature — it can only be a feedback. CO2 does not rain out, so its concentration is set by emissions — it is a forcing. Same absorption physics, opposite roles, and the difference is entirely residence time.
Ask
All emissions stop tomorrow. Sketch CO2 over the next century. It falls fast at first — the sinks keep working while the gradient lasts — then flattens well above 280 ppm and stays there for centuries. Roughly a quarter of an emitted pulse is still in the air after a thousand years. It is not a tap you can turn off; it is a bathtub with a very slow drain.

break · 10:00–10:15

Block 4

The oceanic carbon cycle

10:15 · 40 min

1 · The flux equation

  • ΦCO2 = kCO2 (pWCO2 − pACO2)  (Eq. 2.41)
  • Partial pressure, not concentration — worth a sentence, because it is why the ocean can hold vastly more carbon than the air and still be a sink.
  • Three controls on pW, and they fight: temperature (warm → out), biology (photosynthesis → down), circulation (upwelling → up).
  • Show the zonal flux: the equator is a source and the mid-latitudes are sinks. Ask why the warm tropics are not simply the strongest sink you would expect from Henry's law — then reveal upwelling.

2 · Dissolve, then react — the trick

  • Eq. 2.42–2.45 in order. Do not dwell on each; the point is the chain.
  • Then Eq. 2.47, the net reaction, and slow right down: H2CO3 + CO32− ⇌ 2 HCO3−
  • Read it aloud in words. Adding CO2 consumes a carbonate ion and makes two bicarbonates. The only form that exerts a partial pressure is converted into a form that does not. That is worth ~50× the physical solubility — and it is the flat-Coke question from the warm-up, run in reverse.
  • Then the price, in the same breath: every step releases H+ and consumes CO32−. Acidification is not a side effect of the ocean sink; it is the ocean sink. This sentence tends to be the one students remember.
  • Alkalinity (Eq. 2.48): the excess of bases over acids. The key property is that it is conservative under CO2 addition but not under CaCO3 formation. Park that; it detonates in step 4.

3 · The solver — the demonstration to spend time on

Show
Page 3 · the carbonate solver. It is a real equilibrium calculation, so the numbers it produces can be quoted. (1) Speciation vs pH, water preset Temperate surface. Point at the KPI row: ~89 % bicarbonate, ~10 % carbonate, ~0.5 % dissolved CO2. Say: less than one part in a hundred of the ocean’s carbon can ever leave. Note that seawater happens to sit almost exactly between pK₁ and pK₂.
(2) The solubility pump. Drag temperature 25 °C → 0 °C and stress that nothing entered or left the water — only the constants changed. pCO₂ falls by a factor of three, about 4 % per degree. That is the solubility pump derived, not asserted.
(3) Acidification. Step the milestone markers 280 → 425 → 800 µatm. pH 8.16 → 8.01 → 7.7 at full equilibrium, carbonate ion falling throughout, and the Revelle factor rising: the ocean gets worse at its job the more it does it.
Ask
A drop of 0.15 pH units sounds trivial. Is it? The scale is logarithmic: that is a ~40 % rise in [H+] at equilibrium (~30 % observed, since the surface lags the atmosphere). And the ecologically relevant variable is not pH at all but [CO32−] and the saturation state Ω, both of which the page shows falling steadily. Ask which organisms care, and why the Southern Ocean and the deep sea cross Ω = 1 first.

4 · Three pumps, one of which is a traitor

  • Soft tissue: photosynthesis at the surface, remineralisation at depth. Largest of the three. Nutrient-limited, not CO2-limited.
  • Solubility: cold water sinks and takes its carbon with it. Purely physical, and step 3 already derived it.
  • Carbonate: the one worth deriving on the board. Making CaCO3 removes 1 DIC and 2 alkalinity. Look at Eq. 2.48 — carbonate is counted twice. Net effect on pCO2 is to raise it. Marine calcifiers are, on balance, a source.
  • Result: DIC ~15 % higher at depth. Switching the biological pump off in a model takes the pre-industrial atmosphere from 280 ppm to 450–500; running it at maximum efficiency pulls it toward 150. The glacial 180 ppm sits inside that range — which is why the ocean is the first place to look for the ice-age CO2 problem.
Show
Page 3 — step the four pump buttons in order and watch the two depth profiles build up. On + Carbonate pump, point at how much more the alkalinity profile steepens than the DIC profile. That asymmetry is the whole argument.
Ask
Deep water eventually upwells with its extra 15 % of DIC. Why does that not undo the pumps? Two reasons. It does outgas — that is exactly what the equatorial source in step 1 is. But biology at the surface immediately takes much of it back up, and the water is cold when it first surfaces. The pumps are a steady-state circulation, not a one-way trip: what matters is the standing gradient they maintain, not any individual parcel.

break · 11:00–11:15

Block 5

The land, and the rocks

11:15 · 18 min  ·  cut here if you are behind

1 · The production cascade

  • Write the chain on the board and subtract as you go: GPP 123 → NPP 63 → NEP 8 → NBP 2.3.
  • Name what is removed at each step: autotrophic respiration (the plant), heterotrophic respiration (the decomposers), disturbance (fire, harvest, erosion).
  • The land sink is 2 % of the gross flux. Say the consequence out loud: a few per cent change in respiration relative to production flips the sink into a source without anything visible happening. That is the mechanism behind nearly every carbon-cycle feedback in the models.
  • The terms are not synonyms and students routinely use them as if they were. A high-GPP forest can have zero NBP.
Show
Page 4 — the cascade waterfall, then the NPP heat map. On the heat map, point at the boxed northern growing season and say: that rectangle is the Keeling sawtooth from Block 3. Two completely different datasets, one phenomenon.

2 · Soil, and fire

  • Soils hold ~3× the vegetation and ~2× the atmosphere; permafrost alone is about twice the atmosphere.
  • The pools differ by four orders of magnitude in turnover. Peat and permafrost are not chemically stable — they are cold and wet. Remove either condition and millennia of storage decomposes in decades.
  • Fire returns ~2.2 PgC yr−1, comparable to the whole land sink — but half of it is African savanna that regrows within the year and nets out to nothing. What matters is fire not followed by regrowth: deforestation fire, and Indonesian peat.
  • The 1997 Indonesian peat fires: 0.8–2.6 PgC in one season, up to a quarter of that year’s fossil emissions, from one event.

3 · The rocks — the slow thermostat

  • Three reactions, and they form a loop: sedimentation → metamorphism → weathering.
  • Each moves only 0.1–0.3 PgC yr−1 — a hundredth of fossil fuel burning. Utterly negligible today.
  • But 39 000 PgC ÷ 0.1 PgC yr−1 = ~400 000 years to cycle the entire ocean–atmosphere inventory. On that timescale it is the only thing that matters.
  • The thermostat. Weathering goes faster when warm and wet → consumes more CO2 → cools. Cooler → slower weathering → volcanic CO2 accumulates → warms. A negative feedback with a 105-year time constant. Far too slow to help us, and quite possibly why the Earth has had liquid water for four billion years.
Ask — the link back to PC Lab 1
This is planetary self-regulation with no foresight and no purpose. What was the other example? Daisyworld. Map the two: the daisies ↔ silicate rock; albedo ↔ weathering rate; the range of luminosity over which regulation holds ↔ the range of outgassing rate. Both are negative feedbacks that emerge from ordinary physics, and neither requires the planet to want anything. Good follow-up: what is the equivalent of Daisyworld’s catastrophic collapse at high luminosity?
Block 6

Methane, and the close

11:33 · 15 min  ·  then close at 11:48

1 · The one property that changes everything

  • 2 ppm against CO2's 425, and still about a fifth of the anthropogenic forcing.
  • CH4 + OH· → CH3· + H2O — roughly 90 % of the sink, and it is destruction, not transfer. CO2 has no equivalent.
  • Therefore dB/dt = S − B/τ with τ ≈ 9 years, and therefore a genuine steady state B* = Sτ.
  • Say the asymmetry explicitly, and then say it again: stabilising methane needs constant emissions; stabilising CO2 needs zero emissions. This is the single most useful thing in the section and it is routinely misunderstood in public debate.
  • Organising idea worth 20 seconds: almost every anthropogenic methane source is a leak or an anaerobic process — a stomach, a paddy, a landfill, a bog. Methane is what carbon becomes when it decomposes without oxygen.
Show — the closing demonstration
Page 4 · the methane box model. (1) Today. Sources exceed the sink; methane is heading for ~2080 ppb.
(2) Methane Pledge (−30 % anthropogenic). Watch the trajectory panel: a new, much lower plateau inside about twenty years. Read off the temperature figure in the readout.
(3) Say: there is no CO2 equivalent of that curve at any cut short of zero.
(4) If time, Wetland feedback — natural emissions up 90 Tg yr⁻¹ and it outweighs the entire pledge. The term policy cannot touch, and the argument for cutting the ones it can.

2 · Close

  • Go back to the board and to dS/dt = in − out, still sitting there from 08:15.
  • Recap by residence time rather than by topic: 9 days (atmospheric water, a feedback), 9 years (methane, switchable), centuries to millennia (CO2, effectively permanent), 105 years (rock, the thermostat).
  • Two lines forward: the modelling sessions turn this accounting into equations that integrate; PC Lab 3 takes one coupling (vegetation and precipitation) across a threshold.

Things that reliably go wrong

  • Residence time transferred uncritically from water to carbon. They will compute 4 years for CO2 and conclude the problem is short-lived. Pre-empt it in Block 1 and detonate it in Block 3.
  • GPP, NPP, NEP and NBP used interchangeably. Insist on the cascade and on what is subtracted at each step.
  • “The ocean absorbs CO2 because it is soluble.” It is the reaction, not the solubility. The solver’s speciation KPI settles this in one glance.
  • Assuming shell-building removes CO2. The alkalinity bookkeeping is genuinely counter-intuitive; derive it rather than asserting it.
  • Confusing DIC with alkalinity. Give them the one-liner: DIC is how much carbon; alkalinity is how much charge the bases carry. Adding CO2 changes the first and not the second.
  • Treating methane as “a weaker CO2”. It is a different kind of problem because it has a sink. Everything follows from that.
  • Reading the salinity–(E−P) correspondence backwards, as if salinity drove evaporation.
