2 · Three balances, all of which must close

Draw a box around anything and count what crosses the boundary

This is the whole method, and it is the same one used for the reservoirs in the water and carbon cycles: enclose a region, add up what enters, subtract what leaves, and the difference is the rate of change of what is inside. There are three useful boxes here, and the numbers close in all three.

The atmosphere balance is the one students skip and the one that carries the physics. Look at what dominates it. The atmosphere absorbs only 79 W m−2 of sunlight directly — it is nearly transparent to the Sun. What actually heats it is 358 W m−2 of infrared from the ground, plus 104 of latent and sensible heat. The atmosphere is not warmed from above by the Sun; it is warmed from below by the surface, which is exactly why temperature falls with height, which is exactly why the greenhouse effect works at all (Part 1, §3). One column of arithmetic, and the whole vertical structure of the atmosphere follows from it.

4 · What changed since the slab model

The question from the slides: what is the most important difference with Fig. 2.5?

Fig. 2.5 — the leaky one-layer model of the greenhouse-effect page — has four arrows: absorbed sunlight, surface emission, atmospheric emission up, atmospheric emission down. Compare it with what is on this page:

The answer to the slide's question, in one line: the real budget contains 104 W m−2 of non-radiative flux, and the slab model contains none. That is the single most important difference — not the window, not the absorption of sunlight by the air. Of the 161 W m−2 of sunlight the surface absorbs, only 57 leaves as net radiation (398 up minus 342 down = 56, plus rounding); the other 104 leaves as evaporation and thermals. A radiation-only model has no such escape route, so it must drive Ts up until σTs4 alone carries the whole load — which is precisely why the leaky slab overshoots the observed surface temperature by 8–13 K.
Ask yourself The surface emits 398 W m−2 upward but only absorbs 161 of sunlight. Where does the rest come from — is this not energy from nothing?
Answer
No: it comes back down. The surface receives 161 (solar) + 342 (back radiation) = 503 and loses 398 + 84 + 20 = 502. The 398 is a gross flux, not a net one, and it is nearly cancelled by the 342 flowing the other way. This is the most common conceptual error in the whole lecture. Two bodies facing each other both radiate; only the difference is a heat flow. The same distinction appears in Part 1's note about your body radiating 940 W but netting 190 in a room at 20 °C. When someone objects that back radiation "sends heat from cold to hot", the answer is that it does not — the net radiative flux is 56 W m−2 upward, from warm surface to cooler atmosphere, exactly as thermodynamics requires.

5 · The residual that matters

+0.6 W m−2, and where it is going

As printed, the three balances close to about ±1 W m−2, which is mostly rounding. But the underlying budget does not close, and the amount by which it fails is the most consequential number in this lecture: the planet absorbs roughly 0.6–0.9 W m−2 more than it emits.

Why the imbalance is measured in the ocean, not at the top of the atmosphere. Every arrow on this diagram is known to no better than a few W m−2 in absolute terms — the uncertainty on the latent heat flux alone is ±20. A residual of 0.6 is far too small to see by subtracting two numbers near 240. So it is measured the other way round: from ∂Es/∂t directly, by the Argo float array watching the ocean's heat content rise. The energy imbalance of the planet is a oceanographic measurement.
Ask yourself (from the slides) Where does the 340 W m−2 at the top come from?
Answer
From S0/4. The solar constant is 1361 W m−2 on a surface facing the Sun; Earth intercepts it over a disc πR2 and spreads it over a sphere 4πR2, so the global mean is a quarter of it: 1361/4 = 340. That factor of four is pure geometry, it was derived on the Stefan–Boltzmann page, and it is the single most frequently misremembered step in the lecture. Note also that 340 here uses the modern measured TSI, while the textbook's Fig. 2.5 uses 1368 and so writes 342 — a 0.5 % difference with no consequence for anything on this page.

Four pages, and the thread through all of them is a single equation applied at different scales: at the top of the atmosphere it gives Te; resolved vertically it gives the greenhouse effect; resolved by latitude it gives the circulation; and closed globally it gives the number that defines the present century.
