1 · Three assumptions, and what each one costs

Where the grey slab runs out

The leaky-atmosphere model gets a long way on almost nothing: Ts = [2/(2−ε)]1/4 Te. But to reproduce the observed 288 K it needs ε ≈ 0.77, while the real atmosphere absorbs about 90 % of the surface's infrared. Put the measured ε in and the model overshoots by 8 K. Something structural is missing, not just a tuned number.

Assumption in the slab modelWhat the real atmosphere doesWhere it bites

The claim this page builds to. The greenhouse effect is not "heat trapped under a blanket". It is this: the planet radiates to space from an altitude, and that altitude is cold. Add greenhouse gas and the altitude rises; because temperature falls with height, the planet then emits less, and the whole profile must warm until 239 W m−2 leaves again. Everything below is a way of making that sentence precise.

2 · The vertical structure of the atmosphere

Four layers, defined by which way the temperature goes

The layers are not defined by composition — below about 90 km the air is well mixed and has essentially the same composition throughout. They are defined by the sign of the temperature gradient, and each reversal marks a different heat source.

Troposphere
0–11 km
heated from below; cools at 6.5 K/km; holds 80 % of the mass and almost all the water
Stratosphere
11–50 km
heated from within by ozone absorbing UV; warms with height, so it does not overturn
Mesosphere
50–85 km
too little ozone to heat it, enough CO2 to radiate; falls to the coldest temperature on Earth, ~187 K
Thermosphere
> 85 km
extreme UV and X-rays ionise a very thin gas: high temperature, negligible heat content

Two facts carry into the rest of the lecture. The lapse rate Γ ≈ 6.5 K km−1 in the troposphere — the exchange rate between altitude and temperature. And that 80 % of the mass, and so almost all the greenhouse absorbers, sit in the bottom 11 km. That is where the action is.

Ask yourself Why does the troposphere cool with height while the stratosphere warms with height?
Answer
Because of where the heat enters. The troposphere is heated at its base — the surface absorbs 161 W m−2 of sunlight — so warm air ends up under cold air, which is unstable, and convection follows. The stratosphere is heated in its middle by ozone absorbing ultraviolet, so warm air sits on top of cold air, which is stable. "Strato-" is not decoration: it is stratified. That is why aircraft cruise at its base, and why volcanic aerosols injected into it linger for years while tropospheric ones rain out in days.

3 · The emission level — the actual mechanism

Space sees 255 K. Where is that?

This is one deduction, and it is the most important one on the page. Satellites measure the outgoing longwave flux: 239 W m−2. Invert Stefan–Boltzmann and that flux corresponds to 255 K. But 255 K is not the temperature of anything at ground level — the surface averages 288 K. So the radiation escaping to space is not coming from the surface. It is coming from wherever in the atmosphere the temperature happens to be 255 K.

ze = (Ts − Te) / Γ = (288 − 255) / 6.5 ≈ 5.1 km the effective emission level — mid-troposphere, around the 500 hPa surface
ze effective emission height, km
Ts surface temperature, K
Te effective emission temperature, K
Γ lapse rate, K km−1

Now run the argument the other way, which is how the greenhouse effect actually works. The emission temperature is pinned at 255 K, because energy balance at the top of the atmosphere pins the outgoing flux at 239 W m−2. Then the surface temperature is not a free parameter at all:

Ts = Te + Γ · ze the surface is warm because the emission level is high and the air below it lies on a slope

Adding CO2 makes the atmosphere opaque a little higher up, so ze rises. At the new, higher level it is colder, so the planet emits less than 239 — it is now gaining energy. The whole profile warms until the emission level is back at 255 K, and because the profile shifted upward bodily, the surface has warmed by Γ · Δze.

Read the two sliders against each other. The greenhouse effect needs both an opaque atmosphere (large ze) and a temperature that falls with height (large Γ). Set Γ = 0 — an isothermal atmosphere — and the surface warming vanishes no matter how much CO2 you add, because raising the emission level no longer takes you anywhere colder. This is also why the stratosphere, where temperature rises with height, cools as CO2 increases while the troposphere warms. A brighter Sun would warm both. That contrast is one of the strongest attribution arguments there is.
Ask yourself Doubling CO2 lifts the emission level by only about 150 m. Why is that enough to matter?
Answer
Because it gets multiplied by the lapse rate: 0.150 km × 6.5 K km−1 ≈ 1 K of direct warming — almost exactly the textbook no-feedback response to doubled CO2 (3.7 W m−2 of forcing divided by the 3.8 W m−2 K−1 Planck response). Feedbacks, water vapour above all, then multiply it by roughly three. Note what this reframes: the top-line number is not "CO2 traps 3 K". It is "CO2 raises the exhaust pipe 150 metres, and water vapour does the rest."

Where this model is right, and where it lies. Right: opacity and surface warmth go together, and it gives the runaway intuition — Venus, with an extremely opaque CO2 atmosphere, sits at 737 K on a Te of 232 K, which this model would need roughly a hundred layers to produce. Wrong: it predicts warming that keeps growing with n and never saturates, because every layer is perfectly opaque at every wavelength. The real atmosphere has holes in it. That is the next section, and it is why Earth's climate sensitivity is a few kelvin rather than a few hundred.

Connect it back: n = 0 reproduces the bare rock and n = 1 reproduces the opaque slab (21/4 Te = 303 K) from the previous page. Same family of models, one parameter further.

5 · Why a single ε is a lie

Absorption happens in bands, not everywhere

A greenhouse gas absorbs infrared only at wavelengths matching a vibration or rotation of its molecule. Each gas therefore has its own set of narrow bands, and the atmosphere's opacity is whatever those bands happen to add up to. Between them — particularly between about 8 and 13 µm — the air is nearly transparent and surface radiation goes straight to space. That gap is the atmospheric window, and it carries roughly 40 of the 398 W m−2 the surface emits.

The window
8–13 µm
near-transparent, and it sits almost exactly on the peak of the 288 K Planck curve at 10.1 µm — the planet's best escape route
CO2's main band

the bending mode; already saturated at its centre, which is why forcing grows with the logarithm of concentration

9.6 µm

Water vapour
most of the rest
rotation band beyond 18 µm plus the 6.3 µm bending band; supplies about half of the total greenhouse effect
Two consequences worth saying out loud. First, saturation: the centre of the 15 µm CO2 band already absorbs everything there is to absorb, so extra CO2 can only work at the band's edges. That is the physical origin of the logarithmic forcing law — each doubling adds the same 3.7 W m−2. Second, why methane punches so far above its weight: its 7.7 µm band sits where little else absorbs, so every molecule works on virgin territory. Per molecule CH4 is some 30 times the greenhouse agent CO2 is, and it is band placement rather than chemistry that does it.
Ask yourself Nitrogen and oxygen are 99 % of the atmosphere. Why is neither on this chart?
Answer
Because N2 and O2 are symmetric diatomic molecules: stretching them does not shift charge from one end to the other, so there is no oscillating dipole for an infrared photon to couple to. A molecule needs three or more atoms, or two different ones, to absorb in the infrared. Which is why the greenhouse effect is run entirely by trace species — and why an atmosphere can be almost perfectly transparent to its own planet's heat while being perfectly opaque to the eye.

6 · The half of the surface budget that is not radiation

Convection and evaporation close the balance

Everything so far has been radiation. But if radiation were the only transport, the troposphere would settle at a lapse rate of roughly 10–16 K km−1 and a surface near 333 K. That profile is convectively unstable: warm air beneath cold air overturns. Convection is not a correction applied to the radiative picture — it is what sets the lapse rate to 6.5 K km−1 in the first place, and therefore what fixes the Γ you were sliding in §3.

So of the energy the surface must shed, about half leaves without radiating at all. This is the single biggest reason radiation-only models overshoot: with no convective escape route, the surface has to raise its temperature until σT4 alone carries the entire load.

Where this goes next. The two turbulent fluxes are the subject of Climate at a local scale, Part 2, where they get written as bulk formulas and measured site by site. Here they are simply the two arrows that make the global budget close — which is Part 3 of this series.

Ask yourself (from the slides) What is meant by "non-radiative exchanges"?
Answer
Everything that moves energy without a photon: conduction (molecular contact, effective only in the bottom millimetres of air), convection and turbulence (sensible heat — bulk air movement carrying its heat with it), and above all the latent heat of evaporation, which moves energy by moving mass and releases it somewhere else entirely. Latent heat is the largest single non-radiative flux on the planet, and it is also the coupling between the energy budget of this lecture and the water cycle of the next one.
