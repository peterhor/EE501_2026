The geography of the budget

Part 2 of 3 of the global view. Everything so far has been a global average — one number for the whole planet. But the planet does not absorb where it emits, and it does not emit where it absorbs. This page takes the budget apart geographically, and then follows what that imbalance forces the climate system to do about it. Goosse et al. (2010) §2.1.5, Figs. 2.12–2.18.

Part one The pattern — where the energy lands

1 · Two fields, one difference

The same equation as before, but as a function of place

The planetary balance 239 in = 239 out holds for the global annual mean and nowhere else. Written locally, the top-of-atmosphere net radiation is:

RTOA(φ,λ) = Q(φ) · [1 − α(φ,λ)]  −  OLR(φ,λ) absorbed solar radiation minus outgoing longwave radiation, W m−2

The two terms are controlled by completely different things, and this is the point of the whole page:

Absorbed solar — ASR
Q · (1−α)
set by geometry (how much sunlight arrives, from the insolation page) and by reflectivity — clouds, snow, desert sand, ocean
Outgoing longwave — OLR
≈ σTemit4
set by the temperature of whatever is doing the emitting — and from Part 1 we know that is not the surface but an altitude
Keep the two questions separate. "Why is this place bright?" is a question about albedo. "Why is this place radiating a lot?" is a question about how high and how cold its emission level is. Places can be extreme in one and ordinary in the other, and the Sahara — which is extreme in both, in opposite directions — is the example that makes the distinction stick.

2 · What sets each of the two fields

Absorbed solar: sunlight arriving, minus sunlight thrown away

The incoming part is pure astronomy. Annual-mean insolation at the top of the atmosphere falls smoothly from about 422 W m−2 at the equator to 176 W m−2 at the poles — a factor of 2.4, and no more, because the poles get six months of continuous daylight to partly compensate for the low sun. That gentle gradient is not enough on its own to explain the observed pattern of absorbed radiation.

The albedo does the rest, and it is far less smooth. It ranges from 0.06 over calm tropical ocean under a high sun to 0.85 over fresh snow, with clouds sitting anywhere between 0.2 and 0.7 depending on their thickness. Because albedo multiplies the insolation, a bright surface at low latitude can end up absorbing less than a dark surface much further poleward.

Ask yourself (from the slides) Fig. 2.12 shows annual mean net solar flux at the top of the atmosphere. How do you explain the differences within Africa, with low values in the north?
Answer
Not because less sunlight arrives. At 23° N the annual-mean insolation is 385 W m−2 against 422 at the equator — a difference of under 10 %, and the Sahara is far more cloud-free, so at ground level it is the sunniest place in Africa by a wide margin. The reason it absorbs so little at the top of the atmosphere is the albedo: pale sand under a cloudless sky returns about 35 % of the beam, against 12–15 % for dark rainforest canopy. Multiply and the desert absorbs some 50 W m−2 less than the Congo basin. That figure is essentially an albedo map with a gentle latitude gradient behind it. The same signature appears over the Arabian peninsula, the Kalahari, central Australia and above all Antarctica — every bright surface on Earth prints itself onto the absorbed-solar field.

Outgoing longwave: a measure of altitude, not of ground temperature

A satellite looking down does not see the surface, except through the atmospheric window. What it measures is the emission from whatever is optically thick at each wavelength — which, over a deep convective cloud, is the cloud top. So the strongest control on OLR is how high and how cold the emitting surface is:

Deep convective cloud top, 15 km
≈ 210 W/m²
emitting at about 215 K — σT4 = 121 W/m² from the cloud, plus warmer emission from the cloud-free fraction of the grid box
Cloud-free hot desert
≈ 290 W/m²
a 320 K surface radiates 595 W/m², and with almost no water vapour a large share of it goes straight out through the window

Which produces the result students find genuinely surprising: the hottest place on the map is the brightest thing in the infrared, and the wettest place is the dimmest. The tropical rainforest belt is a minimum of outgoing longwave radiation, even though the surface underneath it is warm, because that surface is hidden under cloud tops at −60 °C.

Ask yourself (from the slides) Fig. 2.13 shows outgoing longwave radiation, with maxima north and south of the equator over Africa and a minimum at the equator. And what is meant by "properties of the atmosphere"?
Answer
The maxima are the subtropical desert belts: hot, cloud-free and above all dry, so the atmospheric window is wide open and the emission level sits low and warm. The equatorial minimum is the ITCZ, where deep convection puts cold cloud tops between the surface and the satellite. "Properties of the atmosphere" means the three things that set the emission level: cloud cover and cloud-top altitude, water vapour (by far the most variable greenhouse gas, and the one that closes the window), and aerosols. Note that this makes OLR nearly the mirror image of the absorbed-solar field over Africa — and that the two partly cancel, which is exactly why the tropics do not run away.

PlaceLatQ αASR OLRNet
The Sahara is a radiative sink. It absorbs less than almost anywhere else at its latitude because it is bright, and it emits more than anywhere else on the planet because it is hot, dry and cloud-free. Both effects push the same way. The desert therefore loses around 45 W m−2 at the top of the atmosphere all year, and stays hot only because the atmosphere keeps importing energy into it — subsiding air from the Hadley circulation, warming as it descends. A desert is not a place with an energy surplus. It is a place with a water deficit, which is a different thing entirely, and the two get confused constantly.

This is also the physical core of the Sahara feedback you will simulate in PC Lab 3: darken the surface with vegetation, absorbed solar goes up, and the circulation that keeps the desert dry is altered.

4 · The zonal picture

Average around every circle of latitude and the noise disappears

Averaging over all longitudes removes the land–ocean detail and leaves the structure that actually matters. Absorbed solar falls steeply from equator to pole. Outgoing longwave falls too — but far more gently, because the atmosphere has already moved heat poleward and warmed the high latitudes above what they could sustain on their own. Three readings of this one figure. (1) The tropics gain energy every year and the poles lose it, yet neither is heating or cooling without limit. Something must be carrying the difference poleward — that is the second half of this page, and this figure is its entire justification. (2) The OLR curve is much flatter than the ASR curve. If the planet had no circulation, OLR would have to match ASR everywhere and the pole–equator temperature contrast would be enormous. The flatness is the transport. (3) The crossing sits near 35°, not at the pole — so the "tropics" that export energy are much wider than the astronomical tropics at 23.4°.
Ask yourself (from the slides) Why is the longwave flux larger than the shortwave flux at high latitudes?
Answer
Because the high latitudes are being kept warm by imported heat, and they radiate at a temperature they could never sustain from local sunlight alone. A polar cap absorbs about 60 W m−2 of sunlight; if it were radiatively isolated it would settle near 185 K. It actually radiates about 170 W m−2, which needs roughly 235 K. The extra 110 W m−2 is delivered by the atmosphere and ocean. The deficit in this figure is not a sign that the poles are cooling — it is the receipt for the energy they are being sent.

An honest note on the numbers. The tabulated curves here average to +2.1 W m−2 rather than to the observed +0.6 W m−2. That residual is the accuracy of a hand-tabulated teaching dataset, and it is worth keeping in view: in §7 it shows up as a transport curve that does not quite return to zero at the pole, which is exactly the diagnostic real analyses have to confront.

Part two The response — what the pattern forces

5 · Storage, and the two terms that absorb an imbalance

The budget for a column of the climate system

If a column takes in more radiation than it emits, exactly two things can happen to the surplus. It can be stored — warming the ocean, the land or the air beneath it — or it can be moved somewhere else:

∂Es/∂t = RTOA − Ftransport rate of change of stored energy = net radiation at the top − net export sideways

Three quantities, and any two of them determine the third. What makes this equation useful is that the two terms on the right dominate on completely different timescales, so you can nearly always neglect one of them — provided you know which.

Days to seasons
storage wins
The ocean charges through summer and discharges through winter. Transport barely changes over a season, so almost the whole radiative swing goes into ∂Es/∂t.
Annual mean and longer
transport wins
Averaged over a year, storage integrates to nearly zero. Whatever the radiation budget does not balance locally must be exported — there is nowhere else for it to go.
Both halves have a modern twist. The annual-mean storage term is not exactly zero today: the planet runs a persistent top-of-atmosphere surplus of roughly +0.6 W m−2, and about 90 % of it is going into the ocean. Global warming is, in the most literal sense, the storage term failing to close. Small as it is, integrated over the ocean's mass it is the dominant fact about the current climate.

How much energy does it take to warm something by one kelvin?

The heat capacity of a column, per square metre of surface, is C = ρ cp h — density times specific heat times the depth actually involved in the exchange. Two things vary, and they multiply:

Ask yourself Why is the ocean's depth "50 m" rather than 3800 m?
Answer
Because only the wind-stirred mixed layer participates on seasonal timescales. Below it the thermocline is a strong density barrier and heat crosses it slowly. The relevant depth is set by the timescale you are asking about: about 50 m for the seasonal cycle, a few hundred metres for decades, and the full 3800 m for millennia. This is the same idea as the damping depth in Heat in the ground, applied to a turbulent fluid instead of a conducting solid — and it is why the ocean's response to warming has both a fast component and a very slow one.

6 · The seasonal cycle as a test of heat capacity

Drive a column with a sine wave and see what comes out

Take one column, force it with the seasonal swing in net radiation, and let it shed heat in proportion to how warm it has become. That is two terms:

C · dT/dt = A sin(ωt) − λT ω = 2π per year; A is the seasonal amplitude of the forcing; λ is how strongly the surface sheds heat per kelvin

It has an exact periodic solution, and both parts of it are worth having:

amplitude = A / √(λ² + (Cω)²)   ·   lag = arctan(Cω / λ) a bigger heat capacity does two things at once: it damps the swing and delays it

Compare with the real map. The July-minus-January surface temperature difference exceeds 40 K over the Siberian and central Canadian interiors, and is under 6 K over the mid-latitude North Pacific — at the same latitude. Nothing about the radiation differs between those places to any comparable degree. The whole contrast is the depth of the heat-storage medium underneath. Two orders of magnitude of heat capacity, one order of magnitude of temperature swing.
Ask yourself (from the slides) What causes the large difference between the continental interior and the North Pacific in the July–January map?
Answer
Heat capacity, through two compounding effects. Water has roughly twice the volumetric heat capacity of soil — and, far more importantly, the ocean mixes, so the incoming energy is distributed through 50 m of water instead of concentrating in the top metre of ground. Around a hundredfold more heat capacity, so around a hundredfold less temperature swing for the same forcing. The phase lag comes along with it: coastal stations reach their warmest in August rather than July, and their coldest in February rather than January. There is a second contributor worth naming — evaporation. The ocean can convert absorbed energy into latent heat without warming at all, which land can only do while it still has water.

7 · Transport: integrating the imbalance

The annual-mean picture, straight from §4

On the annual mean, storage nearly vanishes and the column budget collapses to RTOA = Ftransport. Integrate the net radiation from the South Pole up to some latitude φ and you have the total energy that must cross that circle of latitude, northward:

F(φ) = 2πa² ∫−π/2φ RTOA(φ′) cosφ′ dφ′ a = 6371 km; the cos φ is the shrinking circumference of a latitude circle

This is not a new measurement. It is the same zonal curve from §4, integrated. And that is the point worth making in class: the entire poleward heat transport of the climate system is a derived quantity — nobody measures 5 petawatts crossing 35° N. It is inferred from satellite radiation data by exactly this integral.

Real observational estimates face exactly this problem: satellite radiation budgets are not accurate enough in absolute terms to close, so they are adjusted the same way, and the size of the adjustment is a fair measure of how well anyone actually knows these numbers.

For scale: 1 PW = 1015 W. Total human primary energy use is about 0.019 PW. The climate system moves roughly three hundred times that across a single circle of latitude, continuously.

Who carries it — atmosphere or ocean?

The integral gives the total, but not the carrier. Observational estimates split it roughly like this: the ocean dominates in the deep tropics, peaking near 15–20° N at about 1.7 PW; the atmosphere takes over polewards of about 25° and does almost all of the work in mid-latitudes, where the storm tracks carry warm moist air poleward and cold dry air equatorward.

Ask yourself (from the slides) This is the global-mean picture. How does it look for the Atlantic basin alone?
Answer
Quite different, and it is the single most consequential exception in the climate system. In the Pacific and Indian Oceans the heat transport is roughly antisymmetric — poleward in both directions from the equator, as you would expect. The Atlantic transports heat northward at every latitude, even at 30° S, because the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation carries warm surface water north and returns cold deep water south. The Atlantic therefore steals heat from the South Atlantic and delivers it to the North Atlantic, on the order of 1 PW. That is why northern Europe is anomalously warm, why the Atlantic ITCZ sits north of the equator, and why a slowdown of the AMOC is a question about where heat goes rather than how much there is.

8 · The regional signature of transport

Subtract the zonal mean and what is left is circulation

A clean way to see transport in observed temperatures: take the annual mean temperature at a place and subtract the average over its entire circle of latitude. Whatever remains cannot be insolation — every point on that circle receives the same annual sunlight. It is heat capacity and heat transport, and nothing else.

All five of these places sit within four degrees of latitude of one another and receive, to within a percent or two, the same annual sunlight. They span just over 17 kelvin.

Ask yourself (from the slides) What causes the strong positive values over the North Pacific and especially the North Atlantic?
Answer
Three things, working together. (1) Ocean heat transport: the North Atlantic Current delivers warm water far into the Norwegian Sea, and the AMOC keeps it coming. (2) Heat storage: the ocean releases in winter what it stored in summer, so the winter half of the annual mean is enormously milder than it would otherwise be. (3) The comparison itself is unfair — the zonal mean at 60° N is dragged down by Siberia, central Canada and Greenland, which are cold for the mirror-image reason. So the Atlantic anomaly is partly the Atlantic being warm and partly everything else at that latitude being cold. Both halves of that are the same physics seen from two sides.

The sentence to leave with. The radiation budget of the first half of this page sets up an imbalance the planet cannot tolerate. Storage buffers it on short timescales, transport removes it on long ones, and the two together are why Earth has a climate rather than just a temperature. What remains is to check that all the arrows really do add up — which is Part 3.
