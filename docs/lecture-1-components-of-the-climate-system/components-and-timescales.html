Five components, five clocks

The climate system has five parts, and almost everything that is confusing about climate comes from the fact that they run at wildly different speeds. This page puts a number on each clock. One quantity does most of the explaining: how much energy it takes to warm a square metre of the thing by one kelvin.

1 · The five components

What each one is, and what it is made of

Goosse et al. (2010), §1.2, Fig. 1.1. The names vary between textbooks — some merge the land surface into the biosphere, some split the anthroposphere out. Five is the useful number for this course.

ComponentWhat it containsMass The thing it is good at
Atmosphere The gaseous envelope: 78 % N2, 21 % O2, 0.93 % Ar, plus the trace gases that do all the radiative work and a highly variable amount of water. 5.1 × 1018 kg Speed. It moves heat and water across a hemisphere in days.
Hydrosphere All liquid water: ocean (97 %), plus lakes, rivers, groundwater and soil moisture. The ocean covers 71 % of the surface and averages 3700 m deep. 1.4 × 1021 kg Memory. It stores heat and carbon for centuries.
Cryosphere Every form of frozen water: ice sheets, glaciers, sea ice, lake and river ice, snow cover, permafrost. 2.6 × 1019 kg Leverage. Very reflective, and it hides a huge latent-heat buffer at 0 °C.
Biosphere All living things, marine and terrestrial, plus the dead organic matter they leave behind. ~2 × 1015 kg C Chemistry. Tiny in mass, decisive for CO2, and it changes the surface it grows on.
Pedosphere
and land surface		 Soils, the weathering rock beneath them, and the topography that organises everything above. ~2 × 1019 kg Boundary conditions. Albedo, roughness, water storage, and the carbon in soil.

Look at the mass column before anything else. The ocean outweighs the atmosphere by a factor of about 270, and per kilogram water holds four times more heat than air. The biosphere is seven orders of magnitude lighter than the ocean and still runs the carbon cycle. Mass is not importance — but it is exactly what sets the response time, which is the subject of the rest of this page.

2 · One quantity explains most of it

Effective heat capacity, per square metre

Take a column of one square metre through any component and ask how many joules it takes to warm the whole column by one kelvin. That is its effective heat capacity C, in J m−2 K−1:

C = ρ cp d density × specific heat × the depth that actually participates

The last factor is the one that requires judgement. The whole atmosphere participates on any timescale longer than a few weeks, so d is its full scale height's worth of mass. The ocean is different: on a seasonal timescale only the mixed layer (50–100 m) is in play, because below it the stratification blocks the heat; on a centennial timescale the deep ocean joins in, and C jumps by two orders of magnitude. The ocean therefore has more than one heat capacity, depending on how long you wait. That single fact is why the climate has both a fast transient response and a slow commitment.

Given C, the response of a component to a change in the energy reaching it follows immediately. Perturb the energy balance by ΔF watts per square metre and let the restoring feedback be λ watts per square metre per kelvin:

C · dT/dt = ΔF − λT   ⇒   T(t) = (ΔF / λ)(1 − e−t/τ),    τ = C / λ the equilibrium warming is set by λ alone; how long you wait for it is set by C
You will meet this equation twice more. It is the zero-dimensional energy balance model you build in PC Lab 2, and it is the fast layer of the two-layer model in Lecture 6, part 5, where λ is taken apart feedback by feedback. Here it is used only to get a timescale out of a heat capacity.

The two ends of that table are the whole story. The atmosphere reaches equilibrium in about a month; the deep ocean takes centuries. So on any timescale you can observe directly, the atmosphere is always essentially in equilibrium with the ocean beneath it, and the ocean is never in equilibrium with the forcing. Which is why sea surface temperature predicts next season's weather, and why the planet is still warming after the forcing stops.

Question. Set λ to 3.2 — the Planck response alone, with all other feedbacks switched off. Every τ shortens by a factor of about 2.5. Why should a feedback change how long the system takes to respond, rather than only how far it goes? Because τ = C/λ and λ is the rate at which the system sheds the extra energy. A weak restoring feedback means the imbalance closes slowly, so the same heat capacity takes longer to fill. This is a genuinely counter-intuitive and examinable point: a more sensitive climate is also a slower one. It is why models with high equilibrium sensitivity do not necessarily warm faster in the next thirty years, which is Lecture 6, part 5.

Question. The “whole ocean” row gives τ of a few centuries, but the standard statement is that the deep ocean takes millennia to equilibrate. Where does this calculation go wrong? It assumes the column is well mixed — that heat arriving at the surface is instantly available to the whole 3700 m. It is not. The ocean is stably stratified almost everywhere, and heat reaches depth only through slow overturning and weak vertical mixing. The heat capacity is real; the access to it is the bottleneck. A single-slab τ is therefore a lower bound on the true equilibration time, and it is exactly why models use two layers with a transfer coefficient between them rather than one deep slab.

What is computed here. Every curve is the exact analytic solution T(t) = (ΔF/λ)(1 − e−t/τ) with τ = C/λ and C from the ρcp and depth values in the table — nothing is fitted and no data are loaded. It is a single-slab caricature: it has no transport between components, no stratification and no phase change, all of which matter and all of which are the subject of the pages that follow.

3 · The clock each component keeps

Response times, and the different reasons for them

Not every slow component is slow for the same reason, and the distinction matters.

Component or processTimescaleWhat sets it
Weather systemsdays Advection: a mid-latitude depression crosses the Atlantic in three days.
Atmospheric radiative adjustment1 – 3 months Heat capacity. C/λ for the whole air column.
Snow cover, soil moistureweeks – months Small stores, and a seasonal reset every year.
Ocean mixed layer1 – 10 years Heat capacity. This is the memory that makes seasonal forecasting possible at all.
Sea icemonths – years Latent heat and area. Thermodynamically fast, but its extent integrates the ocean beneath it.
Vegetation coveryears – centuries Biology. Leaves respond in days, species composition and treeline in decades to centuries.
Deep ocean and overturningcenturies – millennia Transport, not capacity. The heat capacity is available; getting heat into it is slow.
Ice sheetsmillennia Mass balance and flow. Nothing to do with thermal inertia: an ice sheet grows or shrinks as fast as snow accumulates or ice flows to the sea.
Rock weathering, carbon burial105 – 106 yr Chemistry. The ultimate thermostat, and hopeless on any human timescale.

Three different mechanisms hide in that one word “slow”. Thermal inertia (heat capacity), transport limitation (stratification, mixing) and mass turnover (accumulation, flow, chemistry). They behave differently under a change in forcing: a thermal-inertia lag catches up once the forcing stops, a transport limitation delays the same total response, and a mass-turnover process may never return to where it started. Recognising which is which is most of what makes commitment and irreversibility in Lecture 8 comprehensible.

A related figure elsewhere in the course. Lecture 7's forced and internal variability page carries a spectrum of the periods of variability — how often things oscillate. The table above is about response times — how long things take to settle. They are related (a component cannot oscillate much faster than it responds) but they are not the same quantity.

4 · Who talks to whom

The interaction matrix

A system is not a list of parts; it is a list of parts and their couplings. Click any cell to see what crosses that particular interface, in which direction, and how quickly. The diagonal is what each component does to itself.

Two things to notice about the matrix. First, it is not symmetric: what the atmosphere does to the ocean (wind stress, heat and freshwater fluxes) is not what the ocean does to the atmosphere (sea surface temperature, evaporation, heat release). Second, the atmosphere's row and column are the fullest, because the atmosphere is the medium through which most of the other couplings are actually transmitted — the ice sheet does not talk to the rainforest directly.

Question. Pick any two components and construct a loop: A changes B, B changes A. Is the loop you built self-reinforcing or self-limiting? Both kinds exist, and telling them apart is the entire content of Lecture 6. Cryosphere → atmosphere → cryosphere via albedo is self-reinforcing: less ice, more absorbed sunlight, warmer, less ice. Biosphere →  atmosphere → biosphere via CO2 fertilisation and uptake is, for now, mostly self-limiting: more CO2, more growth, more uptake, less CO2. The sign is not obvious from the diagram — you have to do the physics, which is Lecture 6, part 4.

5 · Where do human activities go in this diagram?

A question worth arguing about before you answer it

After Ruddiman (2005), reproduced in the lecture slides as the global climate system diagram.

The textbook diagram of the climate system usually shows human activities as an external forcing: an arrow entering from outside, changing atmospheric composition and land cover, with nothing pointing back. That is a modelling convention, and a defensible one — it is what lets you prescribe an emissions scenario and run the model forward.

It is also, taken literally, wrong. Human activity responds to climate: to harvests, to sea level, to policy driven by observed warming. A system in which the forcing depends on the state is a system with a feedback, not an external forcing, and the sign of that feedback is a question about economics and politics rather than physics.

Question. Should the anthroposphere be drawn as a sixth component, or as an external forcing? Argue it both ways before deciding. As a forcing: it makes the physics tractable and the experiment clean — you can ask “what would happen if emissions followed this path?” without having to model the decisions that produce the path. Every scenario in Lecture 8 depends on this convention. As a component: it is physically more honest. Human activity has a mass, a metabolism, an energy budget and a land footprint; it responds to the climate it changes; and integrated assessment models do couple it. The pragmatic answer: draw it as a forcing when you want to isolate the physical response, and as a component when the question is about outcomes. Being explicit about which convention you are using is the actual skill.

What to carry into the next five pages

  • Five components: atmosphere, hydrosphere, cryosphere, biosphere, pedosphere.
  • Each one has its own clock, and the clock is mostly set by τ = C/λ — except for the ones limited by transport or by mass turnover instead.
  • The atmosphere is fast, light and always nearly in equilibrium; the ocean is slow, heavy and never is.
  • The couplings are not symmetric, and most of them are routed through the atmosphere.
  • Everything after this page is one component at a time, asking the same four questions of each: what is in it, how much can it hold, how fast does it respond, what does it do to its neighbours.
