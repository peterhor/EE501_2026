The atmosphere

Not “a layer of air”. A compressible fluid held down by gravity, with a composition that is 99.9 % irrelevant to climate and 0.1 % decisive, a pressure that falls exponentially, a temperature that falls linearly and then stops, and a vertical stability that decides whether the sky is clear or a thunderstorm.

1 · What the air is made of

The first three rows are 99.96 % of dry air and are climatically inert. Every radiative effect in this course — the 33 K greenhouse effect, the entire anthropogenic forcing — is done by the highlighted rows, which together make up well under a tenth of one percent of the air. Students find this implausible. It follows directly from the physics of molecular absorption: only molecules with three or more atoms have the bending and stretching modes that can absorb terrestrial infrared, which is why the two gases that make up 99 % of the atmosphere are transparent to it.

Why a molecule absorbs or does not is set out in Lecture 2's structure of the real atmosphere, which draws the absorption bands and the atmospheric window. The concentrations above become a forcing in W m−2 in Lecture 6, part 2, and their sources and sinks are Lecture 4.

Question. The table is a snapshot. Which rows would have looked materially different in 1750, and which in the year 20 000 BP? In 1750: CO2 280 ppm, CH4 720 ppb, N2O about 270 ppb, and essentially no ozone hole. At the last glacial maximum: CO2 about 190 ppm, CH4 about 350 ppb, and water vapour markedly lower because the whole atmosphere was colder. The first three rows were the same in all three cases. The climatically important part of the atmosphere's composition is precisely the part that changes.

2 · Dry air is a perfect gas; humid air is a trap

From pV = nRT to p = ρRgT

In almost all circumstances dry air behaves as an ideal gas. Start from the form you already know and divide through by the mass to get the form meteorology actually uses:

pV = nRT   →   p = ρ Rg T   (1.1) Rg = R/M = 8.314 / 0.02896 = 287.0 J K−1kg−1 for dry air
p pressure, Pa
V volume occupied by the gas, m3
n amount of gas, mol
R universal gas constant, 8.314 J K−1 mol−1
ρ air density, kg m−3
Rg specific gas constant for dry air, 287.0 J K−1 kg−1
T absolute temperature, K

The four gas laws in the slides are all special cases of this one equation with something held fixed: Boyle (V ∝ 1/p at fixed T), Charles (V ∝ T at fixed p), Gay-Lussac (p ∝ T at fixed V) and Avogadro (V ∝ n at fixed p, T). There is no need to memorise four laws.

Question, and it catches almost everyone. Is humid air heavier or lighter than dry air at the same temperature and pressure? Lighter. A water molecule has a molar mass of 18.0 g mol−1; the average dry-air molecule has 29.0. Adding water vapour means replacing heavier molecules with lighter ones, because at fixed temperature and pressure the total number of molecules per unit volume is fixed (that is Avogadro's law). Intuition says humid air feels heavy, and intuition is wrong. The consequence is real: moist air is buoyant, which is one reason humid air rises and makes storms, and it is why low pressure and humidity go together.

Rather than change Rg for every humidity, meteorology keeps Rg fixed and adjusts the temperature instead, defining the virtual temperature Tv — the temperature dry air would need in order to have the density this moist air actually has:

Tv = T (1 + 0.608 q) q = specific humidity, kg of vapour per kg of moist air. In the humid tropics Tv − T reaches about 4 K.
Tv virtual temperature — the temperature dry air would need to have this moist air's density, K
q specific humidity, kg of vapour per kg of moist air (dimensionless)
0.608 (1 − ε)/ε with ε = 0.622, the ratio of the molar masses of water vapour and dry air

The curve, not the number, is the point. Warm the air from 0 °C to 10 °C and its capacity for vapour rises by about 90 %; from 30 °C to 40 °C it rises by about 75 % in relative terms but by nine times as many hectopascals. This is why a given warming adds far more water to a tropical atmosphere than to a polar one, and it is the entire basis of the Clausius–Clapeyron scaling used in Lecture 8, part 4 and the water-vapour feedback in Lecture 6, part 4.

3 · Why the atmosphere neither collapses nor escapes

Hydrostatic balance, from three forces

After the lecture slides' force diagram (W.H. Brune, Penn State), and Goosse et al. (2010) §1.3.1.

Take a slab of air of area A and thickness Δz. Pressure is force per unit area, so F = pA, and three forces act on the slab:

  • Fbottom = pbottomA, pushing up from the denser air below;
  • Ftop = −ptopA, pushing down from above;
  • Fg = −mg = −ρAΔz g, the weight of the slab itself.

The atmosphere is very close to hydrostatic equilibrium: the three sum to zero, so

ρ A Δz g = pbottomA − ptopA   ⇒   ∂p/∂z = −ρg the pressure drop across any layer is exactly the weight of the air in it
A horizontal area of the slab, m2
Δz thickness of the slab, m
g acceleration due to gravity, 9.81 m s−2
pbottom, ptop pressure on the lower and upper faces, Pa
∂p/∂z vertical pressure gradient, Pa m−1, with z height above the surface, m

Say what each force is preventing. Without the pressure-gradient force the atmosphere would collapse under gravity into a thin dense layer on the ground. Without gravity the pressure-gradient force would blow it into space. The atmosphere we have is the standoff between the two, and the standoff is so nearly exact that the residual — the vertical acceleration — is about a millionth of g in a weather system. That is why models simply assume the balance rather than solving for it, and why they must stop assuming it once the grid is fine enough to resolve a thunderstorm (Lecture 5, part 3).

Combine hydrostatic balance with the gas law by eliminating ρ:

dp/p = −(g / RgT) dz   ⇒   p(z) ≈ ps e−z/H   (1.2),    H = RgT/g an exponential decay with a scale height H of 7–8 km — exactly, if T is constant with height
p(z) pressure at height z, Pa
ps surface pressure, Pa
z height above the surface, m
H scale height — the height over which pressure falls by a factor e, m

Where the exponential comes from is worth one sentence. Each layer must support the weight of everything above it, and the weight of everything above it is proportional to how much is there. Anything whose rate of decrease is proportional to how much is left decays exponentially. The atmosphere has no top; it just becomes negligible.

Question. Set Γ to 0. The profile becomes a pure exponential and the pressure at 11 km rises substantially. Why does a colder upper atmosphere mean less pressure up there? Because H = RgT/g: cold air is dense, a dense layer is physically thinner for the same mass, so the pressure surfaces are squeezed downwards and any fixed height sits higher in the mass column. This is exactly why the 500 hPa surface is around 5.0 km over the winter pole and 5.9 km over the tropics, and it is the reason weather maps aloft are drawn as height of a pressure surface rather than pressure at a height — those height contours are a temperature map of the air below.

Question. The standard atmosphere gives about 314 hPa at the summit of Everest, but climbers measure closer to 335 hPa. Where does the standard atmosphere go wrong, and does the error help or hinder them? Everest is at 28 °N, where the real troposphere is warmer and deeper than the mid-latitude standard, so the pressure surfaces are further apart and there is more air at 8848 m than the standard profile predicts. It helps — by about 7 %, which is a non-trivial amount of oxygen at that altitude. Summit pressure also varies seasonally by roughly 10 hPa, higher in summer, which is one of several reasons the climbing season is when it is.

5 · Why the temperature turns round

Four layers, two heating mechanisms

Goosse et al. (2010), Fig. 1.3.

LayerTop atTemperatureWhy
Troposphere8–18 kmfalls, ~6.5 K km−1 Heated from below. The surface absorbs sunlight and warms the air in contact with it; convection carries that heat up and, in doing so, sets the lapse rate. Contains ~80 % of the mass and essentially all the weather.
Stratosphere~50 kmrises Heated from within, by ozone absorbing solar ultraviolet. Temperature increasing with height makes it extremely stable — hence “strat”-ified, hence no vertical mixing, hence a smooth flight above the weather.
Mesosphere~85 kmfalls Above the ozone, so back to cooling with height. Contains the coldest temperatures on Earth, around 180 K.
Thermosphere—rises steeply Extreme UV and X-rays absorbed by the few molecules left. Very hot, and irrelevant to climate: there is almost no mass there to hold any heat.

The two reversals are the diagnostic. A layer heated from below convects and cools with height; a layer heated from within does neither. Every turning point in the profile marks a change in where the energy is coming from, not a change in the air.

The tropopause is not at one height

The troposphere is the layer convection reaches, so it is as deep as convection can push. Warm surface air rises further before it runs out of buoyancy, so:

LatitudeTropopause heightTropopause temperature Consequence
Equator16–18 km~190 K Deep convection, tall thunderstorms, and the coldest cloud tops on the planet.
Mid-latitude10–12 km~215 K The jet stream sits at the break, where the tropopause height drops sharply.
Pole8–9 km~220 K Shallow, weak convection; in winter a surface inversion instead.

Note the temperature column runs the wrong way. The tropical tropopause is colder than the polar one, even though the tropical surface is 50 K warmer, simply because the air has had 8 km further to rise and cool. That cold trap is what keeps the stratosphere dry, and a dry stratosphere is a large part of why the greenhouse effect works the way it does.

The jet streams live at the tropopause breaks, and why they are there is the subject of the next page. The radiative consequence of the vertical temperature structure — that the planet radiates to space from about 5 km up, where it is 255 K — is Lecture 2, part 4.

6 · Stability: will the parcel keep going?

Two lapse rates, and the gap between them

Lift a parcel of air. It expands, because pressure falls, and it cools — not because it lost heat to anything, but because it did work pushing its surroundings aside. If no heat is exchanged the process is adiabatic, and the cooling rate follows from the first law plus hydrostatic balance:

Γd = g / cp = 9.807 / 1004 = 9.8 K km−1 the dry adiabatic lapse rate — a constant, the same everywhere on Earth
Γd dry adiabatic lapse rate — cooling of an unsaturated parcel per metre of ascent, K m−1
cp specific heat capacity of dry air at constant pressure, 1004 J kg−1 K−1

Once the parcel cools to its dewpoint, water starts to condense, and condensation releases latent heat into the parcel — about 2.5 MJ for every kilogram of water. The parcel still cools, but more slowly. That is the answer to the question in the slides:

Γm = g · (1 + Lvrs/RdT) / (cp + Lv2rsε/RdT2) the saturated adiabatic lapse rate. Not a constant: about 4 K km−1 in warm moist air, tending to Γd when it is too cold to hold any water.
Γm saturated (moist) adiabatic lapse rate, K m−1
Lv latent heat of vaporisation of water, 2.5×106 J kg−1
rs saturation mixing ratio, kg of vapour per kg of dry air
Rd specific gas constant for dry air, 287 J K−1 kg−1
ε ratio of the molar masses of water vapour and dry air, 0.622

Compare either of these with the environmental lapse rate — what the surrounding air actually does, which is a matter of observation, not theory — and you have the stability of the atmosphere.

The three regimes, and the odd one in the middle. If the environment cools faster than 9.8 K km−1 it is absolutely unstable — any parcel, dry or moist, runs away. This happens only in a shallow layer over a strongly heated surface, because convection destroys it within minutes. If the environment cools more slowly than the saturated adiabat it is absolutely stable and nothing rises on its own. Between the two lies conditional instability: stable to a dry parcel, unstable to a saturated one. Most of the troposphere, most of the time, is in that middle state — which is precisely why the weather depends so much on humidity and on whether anything gives the air an initial shove.

Question. Press a winter inversion. The parcel is colder than its surroundings everywhere. What does that do to the air near the ground, and why is it a public health problem in Norwegian valleys? Nothing can rise, so nothing mixes. Anything emitted at the surface — wood smoke, traffic exhaust, road dust — stays at the surface and accumulates for as long as the inversion lasts, which in a sheltered valley under clear winter skies can be days. The physics is identical to the stability calculation above, and the reason inversions form in the first place is the surface energy balance: on a clear calm night the ground radiates away more than it receives and cools below the air above it. That balance is Lecture 3, part 1.

Question. Press hot and bone dry. The environment is nearly at the dry adiabat and the parcel is buoyant — but no cloud forms. Why, and what does form instead? The condensation level is above the level where the parcel runs out of buoyancy, so the air rises, cools, and sinks back without ever saturating. You get vigorous dry convection — thermals, dust devils, a very deep and turbulent boundary layer, glider-friendly conditions — and a cloudless sky. This is the normal daytime state over a desert, and it is why deserts have enormous diurnal temperature ranges: nothing condenses, so no latent heat is released and no cloud forms to slow the nocturnal cooling.

Computed. The dry adiabat is g/cp exactly. The condensation level is found from the Magnus dewpoint and the 125 m K−1 dewpoint-depression rule. Above it the parcel is integrated in 20 m steps using the saturated lapse-rate expression above with Lv = 2.501×106 J kg−1, cp = 1004, ε = 0.622, and pressure from the standard-atmosphere profile of Section 4. It ignores the weight of condensed water, entrainment of drier surrounding air and any freezing above the 0 °C level — all three matter in a real storm, and all three are parameterised rather than resolved in a climate model (Lecture 5, part 1).

What to carry forward

  • Air is an ideal gas: p = ρRgT. Humid air is lighter, handled through the virtual temperature.
  • The vertical structure is hydrostatic: ∂p/∂z = −ρg, giving an exponential pressure profile with a scale height of 7–8 km. Half the mass is below 5.5 km.
  • Temperature falls at about 6.5 K km−1 through the troposphere because the layer is heated from below, and rises through the stratosphere because that layer is heated from within by ozone.
  • Stability is a comparison of the environmental lapse rate against 9.8 (dry) and about 4–7 (saturated) K km−1. Most of the troposphere is conditionally unstable.
  • All of this is vertical. The next page adds rotation, and with it the horizontal circulation.
