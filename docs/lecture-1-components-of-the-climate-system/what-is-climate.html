From weather to climate

Climate is not weather averaged for a while — it is a statistic with a sampling error, a baseline and a shelf life. This page builds the definition from the ground up, works out where the famous thirty years comes from, and shows why the same year can be called “+0.5 °C” or “+1.5 °C” without anybody lying.

1 · Two definitions, forty years apart

The narrow definition — climate as atmospheric statistics

Goosse et al. (2010), §1.1.

Climate is traditionally defined as a description, in terms of the mean and the variability, of relevant atmospheric variables such as temperature, precipitation and wind. Three things in that sentence do real work, and students routinely keep only the first.

  • Mean. The part everybody remembers. Bergen is wetter than Oslo.
  • Variability. Equally part of the definition. Two places with identical annual mean temperature but different ranges have different climates — and the range is what an ecosystem, a crop or a building is actually built for. Extremes live here, and Lecture 8 spends a whole page on them.
  • Relevant variables. Temperature, precipitation and wind, but also humidity, cloud, radiation, snow cover, growing-season length. Which ones are “relevant” depends on the question you are asking, which is a scientific choice and not a given.

Weather is a state; climate is a distribution. Weather is what the atmosphere is doing at one moment, and it is predictable for about a week. Climate is the shape of the distribution that the weather is drawn from, and it is predictable in a completely different sense — you cannot say whether 3 March 2087 will be wet, and you can say something useful about how many wet days that March will have. Lecture 8 makes that distinction formal under the heading projections versus predictions.

Question. A colleague says “the climate has changed — last summer was the hottest I remember.” What, precisely, is wrong with that as evidence? One summer is one draw from the distribution. A single hot summer is entirely compatible with an unchanged climate, because the distribution has width; that is what variability means. Evidence for a changed climate has to be a statement about the distribution — a shifted mean, a changed variance, a changed frequency of exceeding a threshold — and that requires many draws. The demonstration below quantifies exactly how many.

The wider definition — climate as the state of a system

Climate is now increasingly defined as the description of the whole climate system: not just the atmosphere but the ocean, the ice, the living world and the soil beneath it, together with everything they exchange. The atmosphere is the component we experience, and it is the fastest and the lightest of the five. It is also the one that would forget everything within weeks if the others stopped talking to it.

That shift — from a list of atmospheric statistics to a coupled system — is the reason this lecture exists, and it is what the remaining six pages are about. It is also a warning about the first definition: a thirty-year mean is only a stable description of climate if the slow components are not moving. They are moving.

2 · Where the thirty years comes from

Average some weather

Thirty years is a convention, not a law of nature. Read the second figure, not the first. Sampling error falls as 1/√N — fast at the start, then barely at all. Staleness grows linearly and never stops. Their sum therefore has a minimum, at N = (2σ²/trend²)1/3, and where that minimum sits depends entirely on how fast the climate is moving.

And the answer is uncomfortable. Press the climate 30 years was chosen for — a trend of about 0.07 K per decade, roughly the early-twentieth-century rate — and the optimum lands near 25 years. Thirty is a perfectly sensible number for that world. Now press the normal we use, the climate we have: at 0.2 K per decade the optimum collapses to about 12 years, because staleness now grows three times faster while the sampling error is unchanged. By its own criterion the standard 30-year normal is roughly twice as long as it should be, and it is kept because comparability between countries and across decades is worth more than the extra tenth of a degree.

Be careful how far you push this. The 30-year convention was fixed in the 1930s for standardisation, not by anyone minimising this expression. The trade-off above is a reconstruction of why the number was a reasonable one and why it is periodically re-argued — not the historical reason it was adopted.

Question. Set the trend to zero. The “best window” runs off the end of the slider. Why, and what does that tell you about what a climate normal is for? With no trend the climate is stationary, so there is no such thing as out-of-date data and every extra year purely buys precision — the optimum is “all the data you have”. A finite window only makes sense because the thing being estimated is itself moving. A climate normal is therefore not an estimate of the climate; it is an estimate of the climate of a particular period, and it has an expiry date.

Question. Norway's normals are computed for the whole country from the same period, yet σann is roughly twice as large at Karasjok as at Bergen. What does that do to the meaning of a 30-year normal at the two sites? The same 30 years buys twice the precision at Bergen. A 30-year normal at Karasjok has a sampling error of order 0.2 K, at Bergen of order 0.1 K, so a difference between two consecutive normals that is significant on the coast may be pure noise inland. Continental and high-latitude sites need longer records — or larger changes — before the same statement can be made about them. This is the spatial-averaging argument in Lecture 7's signal and noise page, seen from the other end.

What is real and what is drawn here. The daily series is synthetic — a seasonal cycle, an autocorrelated weather term and a linear trend, generated in your browser from a fixed seed. It is not a station record and no observations are loaded. What is real is the arithmetic: σN = σann/√N and the linear staleness term are exact, and they are the entire content of the figure below the series.

3 · The number depends on the baseline you chose

Same year, six different anomalies

An anomaly is a difference, and a difference needs two ends. Change the reference period and the number changes, with no new physics and no new data. This is the single most common source of confusion when students compare figures from different agencies.

Reference period

The curve never moves. The axis does. Every point in the series is shifted by the same constant, so every difference — every trend, every ranking of years, every statement about how fast it is warming — is completely unaffected. Only the labels change. If a figure gives you an anomaly without naming its baseline, the number is meaningless on its own.

Synthetic again. The curve is a smooth function plus autocorrelated noise, chosen so that its shape resembles the instrumental record. It is not the instrumental record, no values should be read off it, and it is drawn here only so that the shifting of the zero line is visible. The published numbers are in the table below.

Question. The Paris Agreement targets are stated relative to pre-industrial. Why is that choice awkward, and what would have been the alternative? Because there are no global instrumental data before about 1850, so “pre-industrial” has to be operationalised as 1850–1900 — a period that already has some anthropogenic warming in it, and whose coverage is thin, especially over the Southern Ocean. Using a modern, well-observed baseline would make the observations far more precise but would make the target incomparable with the physical quantity people mean by warming since industrialisation. The choice trades observational precision against conceptual relevance, and the community chose relevance. Lecture 8's carbon budgets page shows how much the remaining budget depends on it.

Two things to notice in that table. First, both entries are differences between two thirty-year windows — the definition from Section 2 used as a working tool, not as a piece of theory. Second, the day length does not change. A Telemark with the temperature of central Europe still has midwinter darkness and midsummer light, and no ecosystem currently on Earth is adapted to that combination. This is why the ecology half of this course is not a footnote to the physics.

Where the +4.0 °C comes from. A downscaled regional projection under a scenario, with all the layers of uncertainty that implies. 5 · From the atmosphere to the system

Why a purely atmospheric definition cannot survive

Everything on this page so far treats climate as a property of the air. That is workable for describing a place, and hopeless for explaining it. Three observations break it:

  • The atmosphere has almost no memory. Its heat capacity is about a thousandth of the ocean's. Left to itself it would come to equilibrium with its boundaries in weeks. Anything that persists for longer than a season is being persisted by something else.
  • The boundaries are not fixed. Sea surface temperature, sea ice extent, snow cover, soil moisture and vegetation all change on the timescales we care about, and all of them are partly caused by the atmosphere they are forcing. That two-way street is what makes it a system rather than a forcing and a response.
  • The interesting timescales belong to the slow components. Glacial cycles, ocean overturning, carbon uptake, ice-sheet decay: none of them are atmospheric, and all of them set the atmospheric statistics we then call climate.

So the rest of this lecture takes the components one at a time — the atmosphere, the ocean, the ice, the living surface — and asks of each: what is in it, how much energy and water can it hold, how fast does it respond, and what does it do to its neighbours.

Where humans go in this picture. Deliberately left until the end. Every component below has a human fingerprint on it — composition, land cover, water, ice — and the question of whether to draw the anthroposphere as a sixth component or as an external forcing is a real one, taken up in Lecture 8. Hold the question until then; it is worth asking a room.
