The land surface and the biosphere

The atmosphere does not touch the Earth. It touches leaves, soil, snow and rock — and what it touches decides how much sunlight is absorbed, how much water is returned, how rough the flow is and where the carbon goes. This last page closes the loop: the biosphere is not only a passenger of climate, it is one of the boundary conditions.

1 · What the surface actually controls

Six properties, all of them set by what is growing

PropertyWhat it controlsWhere it returns in the course
AlbedoThe fraction of sunlight reflected rather than absorbed — the biggest single lever, and the one vegetation changes most. L3.4, L6.4, Lab 1
Roughness lengthHow efficiently the surface exchanges momentum, heat and moisture with the air. A forest is aerodynamically a hundred times rougher than grass. L3.2

Notice what is missing from that list: temperature. The surface does not have a temperature that it imposes on the atmosphere. It has properties, and the temperature is what comes out when those properties are fed into an energy balance. That distinction is the whole of Lecture 3, and it is why a change in vegetation is a change in climate rather than a response to one.

Now do the experiment that matters. Press all tundra, then all forest, with the snow on. Replacing tundra with forest at the same latitude changes the winter albedo from about 0.75 to about 0.20 — the trees stick up through the snow and hide it. That single change absorbs tens of watts per square metre more sunlight, which is a warming effect, and it is large enough to compete with the cooling effect of the carbon those same trees take up.

Question. Planting boreal forest therefore has two opposing climate effects. Which wins, and what does that imply about tree-planting as climate policy? In the high-latitude boreal zone the albedo effect frequently wins: the forest is dark all winter under a low but non-zero sun, and the carbon uptake per hectare is modest because growth is slow. Several studies find boreal afforestation to be net warming. In the tropics the reverse is emphatically true: the albedo change is small, the carbon uptake is large, and there is a third effect — intense evapotranspiration cooling the surface and forming cloud — that also cools. The policy conclusion is not “trees are bad”; it is that where you plant determines the sign of the answer, and a carbon-only accounting is the wrong accounting. The same vegetation–albedo mechanism, run forward until it becomes an abrupt transition, is PC Lab 3.

Question. Drag the insolation down to 40 W m−2 — a polar winter. The albedo difference is unchanged but the watts almost vanish. What does that tell you about when the ice–albedo feedback operates? Albedo can only act on light that is arriving. In the polar night it does nothing at all, however white the surface is, which is why the ice–albedo feedback is a summer feedback and why the Arctic amplification observed in winter must be explained by something else — principally the loss of sea ice as an insulating lid, letting ocean heat escape to the atmosphere. Two different mechanisms, two different seasons, one phenomenon. Lecture 8, part 3.

3 · Why vegetation zones look the way they do

Two variables predict most of it

Look at any map of world vegetation and the pattern is dominated by bands running east–west, not north–south. That is because the first-order control is temperature, which is set by latitude through insolation. The second-order control is precipitation, which is set by the circulation from page 4 — and precipitation is what breaks the bands up, because it varies strongly with longitude.

The Köppen–Geiger classification formalises exactly this: it is a decision tree operating on twelve monthly temperatures and twelve monthly precipitation totals, and nothing else. No vegetation data go into it, and yet it reproduces the world's biomes remarkably well — which is itself the evidence that temperature and precipitation are the controls.

Set an annual climatology and the classifier runs the published Köppen–Geiger criteria on it, exactly as Peel, Finlayson and McMahon (2007) state them. Watch which threshold you are closest to — that is where a climate zone will move first.

Approximate real places

Question (the breakout discussion from the lecture). Are vegetation and climate zones more dependent on latitude or on longitude? Are there exceptions? And which two climate parameters do most of the work? Latitude first, because insolation and therefore temperature are set by it — that is why the zones form bands. The exceptions are all about circulation and ocean. Western Europe is several classes milder than eastern North America at the same latitude, because of the North Atlantic Current and the prevailing westerlies. The Atacama and the Namib are deserts on the coast, because of cold upwelling water offshore — the Ekman argument from page 5. Mountains create their own zones vertically, compressing into two kilometres what latitude spreads over four thousand. And the great continental interiors are dry simply because they are far from any ocean. The two parameters are temperature and precipitation, which is not a coincidence — they are the two things the classifier is given, and it works.

Marine life is three petagrams and runs a global cycle. Stock and flux are different quantities, and the biosphere is the clearest case in the system where the small number is the misleading one. Land vegetation holds half as much carbon as the atmosphere and exchanges about a seventh of the atmosphere's entire content with it every year — which is why the Keeling curve has visible teeth.

Those teeth, the annual growth rate and the closure of the global budget are Lecture 4, part 2.

The feedbacks vegetation runs

Each of these is a two-way coupling in the interaction matrix on page 2. The sign is given here; the physics is Lecture 6.

MechanismSignHow it works
Snow masking by tall canopypositive Warmer → treeline advances → darker winter surface → warmer. The boreal feedback of Section 2.
CO2 fertilisationnegative More CO2 → faster growth → more uptake → less CO2. Saturates, and is limited by nitrogen and water.
Soil respirationpositive Warmer → faster decomposition → more CO2 released → warmer.
Vegetation–precipitation couplingpositive More vegetation → more evapotranspiration and lower albedo → more rainfall → more vegetation. The Sahara mechanism.
Evaporative coolingnegative Warmer and wetter → more transpiration → more latent heat flux → cooler surface.
Fire and disturbanceboth Releases carbon (positive) and resets albedo (either sign, depending on what regrows).

Both signs are present, which is the honest summary. The biosphere is not a thermostat and it is not an amplifier; it is a set of competing mechanisms whose net effect depends on region, on timescale and on how far the system has already moved. Where a positive feedback happens to dominate strongly enough, the response stops being proportional to the forcing and becomes a threshold — which is what PC Lab 1 and PC Lab 3 are both about.

And the pedosphere underneath

Soil and topography are the slowest boundary condition of all, and the easiest to forget:

  • Topography lifts air and makes it rain on one side and not the other; it steers the jet stream; and it decides where ice sheets can grow. On the timescale of this course it is fixed, which is exactly what makes it a useful constraint.
  • Soil depth, texture and nutrients set the ceiling on what can grow. Two places with identical climate and different soil carry different vegetation — which is why the Köppen classifier above, good as it is, is not perfect.
  • Rock weathering consumes CO2 and is the ultimate long-term thermostat, operating over 105–106 years. It is the reason Earth has stayed habitable for four billion years and is completely irrelevant to the next four hundred. Lecture 4, part 4.

5 · The whole lecture in one place

What to take away from Lecture 1

  • The climate system has five components — atmosphere, hydrosphere, cryosphere, biosphere, pedosphere — and climate is a description of the whole thing, not just the air.
  • Each component has its own timescale, from days for the atmosphere to millennia for the deep ocean and the ice sheets, and mostly it is set by heat capacity.
  • Every component interacts with every other, usually through the atmosphere, and the couplings are not symmetric.
  • The general circulations of the atmosphere and ocean exist to move heat from where there is a surplus to where there is a deficit, and rotation is what gives them their structure.
  • Recurring themes, all of which return in later lectures: the interface between atmosphere and ocean; the distribution of land and sea; differences in heat capacity; topography; seasonality in the biosphere and cryosphere; albedo; and the carbon cycle.
  • And the question deliberately left open: where do human activities belong in this diagram — as a sixth component, or as a forcing from outside?
