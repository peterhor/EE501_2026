The land surface

The atmosphere never touches the Earth. It touches soil, leaves, snow and rock — and what it touches decides how much sunlight is absorbed, how much water is returned to the air, how much runs off, and how rough the flow is. The land surface is only 29 % of the planet and it sets the boundary conditions for most of what people care about.

1 · Six levers 2 · Albedo 3 · Where the rain goes 4 · Soils and topography
A note on the name. Goosse et al. call this component the land surface, and that is the name used throughout this course. You will also meet pedosphere (strictly the soil mantle — a part of the land surface, covered in Section 4) and lithosphere or geosphere (the solid Earth, including the rock beneath the soil). They are not synonyms, and the differences matter: topography is lithosphere, soil carbon is pedosphere, and albedo is neither — it is a property of whatever happens to be on top.

1 · What the surface actually controls

Notice what is missing from that list: temperature. The surface does not have a temperature that it imposes on the atmosphere. It has properties, and the temperature is what comes out when those properties are fed into an energy balance. That distinction is the whole of Lecture 3, and it is why a change in land cover is a change in climate rather than a response to one.

Now do the experiment that matters. Press all tundra, then all forest, with the snow on. Replacing tundra with forest at the same latitude changes the winter albedo from about 0.75 to about 0.20 — the trees stick up through the snow and hide it. That single change absorbs tens of watts per square metre more sunlight, which is a warming effect, and it is large enough to compete with the cooling effect of the carbon those same trees take up.

Question. Planting boreal forest therefore has two opposing climate effects. Which wins, and what does that imply about tree-planting as climate policy? In the high-latitude boreal zone the albedo effect frequently wins: the forest is dark all winter under a low but non-zero sun, and the carbon uptake per hectare is modest because growth is slow. Several studies find boreal afforestation to be net warming. In the tropics the reverse is emphatically true: the albedo change is small, the carbon uptake is large, and there is a third effect — intense evapotranspiration cooling the surface and forming cloud — that also cools. The policy conclusion is not “trees are bad”; it is that where you plant determines the sign of the answer, and a carbon-only accounting is the wrong accounting. The same vegetation–albedo mechanism, run forward until it becomes an abrupt transition, is PC Lab 3.

Question. Drag the insolation down to 40 W m−2 — a polar winter. The albedo difference is unchanged but the watts almost vanish. What does that tell you about when the ice–albedo feedback operates? Albedo can only act on light that is arriving. In the polar night it does nothing at all, however white the surface is, which is why the ice–albedo feedback is a summer feedback and why the Arctic amplification observed in winter must be explained by something else — principally the loss of sea ice as an insulating lid, letting ocean heat escape to the atmosphere. Two different mechanisms, two different seasons, one phenomenon. Lecture 8, part 3.

3 · Where the rain goes

The land splits water two ways, and energy decides how

Rain arriving at the land surface has exactly two fates: it goes back to the atmosphere as evapotranspiration, or it leaves sideways as runoff. Over a long enough period and a large enough catchment, storage does not change and the split is complete:

P = E + R precipitation in, evapotranspiration up, runoff away — the long-term land water balance
P precipitation, mm yr−1
E evapotranspiration — evaporation plus transpiration, mm yr−1
R runoff, everything that leaves sideways, mm yr−1

What sets the split? Two ceilings, and the lower one wins. Evaporation cannot exceed the water that arrives (P), and it cannot exceed what the available energy can vaporise (Rn/λ, the net radiation divided by the latent heat of vaporisation). Their ratio is the dryness index:

Φ = Rn / (λP) Φ < 1: more water than energy — energy-limited, and the surplus runs off. Φ > 1: more energy than water — water-limited, and nearly everything evaporates.
Φ dryness index, Rn/(λP), dimensionless
Rn net radiation at the surface, W m−2
λ latent heat of vaporisation, 2.45 MJ kg−1
λP the energy it would take to evaporate all the rain

Budyko's curve interpolates between those two limits and describes catchments across the world remarkably well for something with no free parameters at all:

E/P = [ Φ · tanh(1/Φ) · (1 − e−Φ) ]1/2 Budyko (1974). As Φ→0 it gives E/P→Φ (all the energy is used); as Φ→∞ it gives E/P→1 (all the water is used).

Why this belongs in a lecture about components. The same curve that predicts how much water a river carries also predicts how the surface divides its energy between latent and sensible heat — because every millimetre evaporated is 2.45 MJ of latent heat that did not go into warming the air. Water and energy at the land surface are the same problem written twice, and the Bowen ratio in Lecture 3, part 2 is the other half of it.

Question. Press Sahara, then Amazon. Both have plenty of energy. Why does one produce the largest river on Earth and the other produce none? Because the binding constraint is different. The Sahara sits far out at Φ > 10: energy is abundant, water is not, so E/P is pinned at essentially 1 — every drop that falls evaporates and runoff is nil. The Amazon sits near Φ ≈ 1.1, where the two ceilings are comparable, so a large fraction of a very large precipitation total is left over. Note what this means: the Amazon is a big river not because the fraction running off is unusual, but because P is enormous. The fraction is ordinary; the quantity is not.

Question. Deforest a tropical catchment. Roughness falls, rooting depth falls, and albedo rises slightly. Which way does runoff move, and is that good? Runoff rises, at least at first: shallower roots reach less water and a smoother, brighter surface evaporates less, so more of the same rainfall leaves sideways. Whether that is good depends entirely on the timescale. Locally and immediately it means more water in the river — and more flooding, more erosion, and less soil. Regionally and over years it can mean less rainfall, because a large share of Amazon precipitation is water recycled from the forest itself; cut enough of it and the moisture that would have fallen downwind never arrives. That vegetation–precipitation coupling is a positive feedback, and it is the mechanism behind PC Lab 3.

4 · Soils and topography — the slow boundary conditions

The pedosphere, properly

Strictly, the pedosphere is the soil mantle: the weathered, biologically worked layer between the rock and the air. It is a part of the land surface, not a separate component — but it does three jobs no other part of the system does.

JobScaleWhy it matters for climate
Water store~10–30 cm
of water		 The buffer between rainfall and evaporation. A soil that has run dry cannot cool itself by evaporating, so the energy goes into sensible heat instead and the air above it heats sharply. This is why a dry spring makes a hot summer, and it is one of the strongest amplifiers of heatwaves.
Carbon store~1500–2400 Pg C
to 1 m		 More than the atmosphere and all vegetation combined. Cold, wet soils decompose slowly and accumulate; warm the soil and respiration speeds up, which is a positive feedback.
Nutrient supply— Depth, texture and nutrients set the ceiling on what can grow. Two places with identical climate and different soil carry different vegetation — which is exactly why a climate classifier, good as it is, is not a vegetation map.

Soil is the slowest thing on the land surface that can still change within a human lifetime. Vegetation responds in years, soil carbon in decades to centuries, and the mineral soil itself in millennia. That intermediate timescale is what makes soil carbon both a serious climate lever and a very slow one to pull back.

Topography, and the rock underneath

  • Topography lifts air and makes it rain on one side and not the other. It steers the jet stream, anchors the storm tracks, and creates rain shadows — the mechanism you met in part 4. On the timescale of this course it is fixed, which is exactly what makes it useful: it is the one boundary condition that does not answer back.
  • Bedrock decides where ice can grow, and whether it is stable. Ice grounded below sea level on a bed that deepens inland retreats unstably — the fact behind the missing seven metres on the previous page.
  • Rock weathering consumes CO2 and is the ultimate long-term thermostat, operating over 105–106 years. It is the reason the Earth has stayed habitable for four billion years, and it is completely irrelevant to the next four hundred. Lecture 4, part 4.

Question. If weathering is a thermostat that has kept Earth habitable for four billion years, why is it no use to us? Timescale, not mechanism. The feedback is real and it works: warmer and wetter conditions speed up silicate weathering, which draws CO2 down, which cools. But the response time is of order half a million years, because that is how fast rock dissolves. Against a perturbation delivered over two centuries it is not a thermostat, it is a geological footnote. This is the clearest case in the course of a genuine negative feedback that is irrelevant because of its clock — and it is why the τ column on page 2 matters as much as the sign column.

What to carry forward

  • The land surface is a set of properties, not a temperature: albedo, roughness, evapotranspiration, water storage, soil carbon, topography.
  • Albedo is the biggest lever, and forest hides snow — which is why afforestation warms at high latitude and cools in the tropics.
  • The land splits precipitation into evaporation and runoff according to whichever of water and energy is scarcer, and Budyko's curve does it with no free parameters.
  • Soil stores more carbon than the atmosphere and all plants together, and it buffers the water that keeps summers cool.
  • Topography is the one boundary condition that does not respond — and weathering is a real thermostat with a useless clock.
