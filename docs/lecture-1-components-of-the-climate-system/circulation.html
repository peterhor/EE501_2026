Why the wind blows where it does

The tropics receive more energy than they emit and the poles emit more than they receive, so something has to carry heat poleward. On a non-rotating Earth that would be one enormous convection cell per hemisphere. Rotation breaks it into three, turns every wind sideways, and produces the deserts, the storm tracks and the monsoons.

1 · Start without rotation

A fluid heated from below and cooled from above

Goosse et al. (2010), Fig. G.2.

Heat a shallow layer of fluid at the bottom and cool it at the top and it will not conduct the heat through — it will overturn. Warm fluid is less dense, rises, spreads out at the top, cools, sinks and returns. That is convection, and on the rotating Earth it operates at every scale at once:

  • A thermal over a ploughed field, a few hundred metres across, lasting minutes.
  • A cumulonimbus, 10 km across and 15 km tall, lasting an hour.
  • A sea breeze, tens of kilometres, reversing daily.
  • A monsoon, thousands of kilometres, reversing annually.
  • The Hadley cell, a third of the way to the pole, permanent.

The same physics, five times over. Every one of them is the buoyancy calculation from the previous page applied at a different scale, and the only thing that really changes between them is whether rotation has time to matter. It takes about a quarter of a pendulum day — a few hours in mid-latitudes — for the Coriolis force to bend a flow appreciably. Anything faster than that goes straight; anything slower turns.

On a non-rotating Earth the whole picture would be simple: air rises at the equator where it is heated, flows poleward aloft, sinks at the pole, and returns along the surface. One cell per hemisphere, surface winds everywhere from the pole towards the equator. The Earth does not do this, and the reason is that it spins.

2 · The Coriolis force

An apparent force, and a real deflection

Stand on a merry-go-round and roll a ball straight across it. To anyone standing beside the merry-go-round the ball goes in a straight line. To you, spinning with it, the ball curves. Nothing pushed it — you moved. The Earth is the merry-go-round, and it turns once per sidereal day.

Because the Earth is a sphere, only the component of the rotation vector perpendicular to the local surface matters, and that varies with latitude:

f = 2Ω sinφ the Coriolis parameter, in s−1. Ω = 7.292×10−5 s−1. Zero at the equator, maximal at the poles, and of opposite sign in the two hemispheres.
f Coriolis parameter, s−1
Ω Earth's angular rotation rate, 7.292×10−5 s−1
φ latitude, degrees (positive north)

A moving parcel with no other force acting on it is turned continuously to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern, at a rate f × speed. Turning at a constant rate means going in a circle — an inertial circle — of radius U/f and period 2π/f, which is half a “pendulum day”.

Question. Set the latitude to 0. The parcel goes perfectly straight and the inertial period becomes infinite. What does that do to weather systems near the equator? It removes the mechanism that holds them together. A mid-latitude depression survives for days because Coriolis turns the inflowing air until it circles the centre instead of filling it in. With f = 0 there is nothing to turn the inflow, so air simply rushes into any low pressure and erases it. This is why hurricanes never form within about 5° of the equator and why none has ever been observed to cross it. It is also why the equatorial atmosphere has to be described with a different set of balances entirely.

Question. Someone tells you the Coriolis force determines which way water spins down a bathtub drain. Estimate whether that can be true. Use the numbers on this page. For a bath, U is maybe 0.1 m s−1 over a length L of 0.5 m, so the time available is about 5 s, against an inertial period of roughly 17 hours at 45°. The Coriolis deflection over that time is of order ½fUt² ≈ 10−5 m — a hundredth of a millimetre. Any asymmetry in the tub, the plug or the way the water was filled swamps it by orders of magnitude. The formal version of this argument is the Rossby number Ro = U/(fL), which for the bath is about 2000: rotation is entirely negligible. For a depression, U = 20 and L = 106 m give Ro ≈ 0.2, and rotation dominates.

Computed, and idealised. The trajectory is the exact solution of du/dt = fv, dv/dt = −fu on an f-plane — a local tangent plane with f held constant at its value for the chosen latitude. A real parcel travelling hundreds of kilometres poleward would feel f change along the way (the β effect), which turns the closed circle into a westward-drifting loop; and a real parcel is never free of pressure gradients or friction, which is the subject of the next section.

3 · Geostrophic balance

Why wind blows along the isobars rather than across them

Air is pushed from high pressure towards low pressure by the pressure-gradient force. As soon as it starts moving, Coriolis turns it. It keeps turning until the two forces are opposed — and at that point the flow is at right angles to the pressure gradient, i.e. parallel to the isobars. That balance is geostrophy, and it holds to within about 10 % for large-scale flow away from the surface:

|∇p| / ρ = f ug   ⇒   ug = |∇p| / (ρ f) the geostrophic wind. Stronger where the isobars are packed, and — for the same packing — stronger nearer the equator, because f is smaller there.
ug geostrophic wind speed, m s−1
|∇p| magnitude of the horizontal pressure gradient, Pa m−1
ρ air density, kg m−3
f Coriolis parameter, s−1

Near the ground, friction enters and the balance becomes three-way. Friction always opposes the motion, so the wind slows and turns: it now crosses the isobars at an angle, blowing partly inward towards low pressure. That inward component is what feeds the rising motion at the centre of a depression and makes it rain.

Two things fall out of the formula that are worth saying aloud. First, ug ∝ 1/f: the same pressure gradient produces a much stronger wind at low latitude, because a slower Coriolis turn needs a faster wind to generate enough of it. Second, the wind speed can be read straight off a weather map — closely packed isobars mean strong wind, and the relationship is quantitative, not qualitative. That is why forecasters draw isobars at all.

Question. Increase the friction. The wind slows and turns towards low pressure. Follow that through: what happens in the middle of the depression, and what does it produce? Air spirals inward all the way round, so mass converges at the centre. It cannot go down through the ground, so it goes up. Rising air cools adiabatically, reaches its condensation level, and makes cloud and rain. This is why lows are wet and highs are dry — and it is friction, the term everyone treats as a nuisance, that is doing it. Without friction the flow would be exactly along the isobars, nothing would converge, and depressions would neither rain nor fill in.

Computed. ug = |∇p|/(ρf) with ρ = 1.225 kg m−3 and f = 2Ωsinφ; the three-way balance with a linear drag −kv gives a speed u = ugf/√(f²+k²) and a cross-isobar angle arctan(k/f), both exact for that drag law. Real surface drag is not linear in the wind — it goes roughly as the square — so k here is an effective coefficient, not a measured one. Straight isobars also mean no centrifugal term; around a tight low the gradient-wind balance applies instead and the wind is slower than geostrophic.

4 · Three cells, not one

What rotation does to the single overturning cell

Goosse et al. (2010), Fig. 1.5. The figure below is a schematic of the zonal-mean circulation, not a plot of measured winds.

Why the single cell breaks. Air leaving the equator aloft is turned east by Coriolis as it moves poleward. By about 30° it is moving almost due east and can go no further poleward — the flow has become a jet rather than a transport. It piles up, sinks, and returns along the surface. That closes the Hadley cell, and everything else follows from where its descending branch lands.

Question. The Sahara is one of the sunniest places on the planet. Why is it a desert? Not because of the sunshine — because of where it sits in the circulation. It lies under the descending branch of the Hadley cell, where air that rose over the equator, rained out all its moisture there, and travelled north aloft is now sinking. Sinking air is compressed and warms, its relative humidity collapses, and cloud cannot form. The dryness comes first; the heat and the sunshine are consequences of the cloudlessness, not causes of the dryness. Lecture 2's geography of the budget takes this further and shows the Sahara is actually a net radiative deficit at the top of the atmosphere.

Question. The Ferrel cell is drawn like the other two, but it is not really the same kind of thing. What is different about it? The Hadley and polar cells are direct: warm air rises, cold air sinks, and the cell converts potential energy into kinetic energy, as any convection cell does. The Ferrel cell is indirect — it has warm air sinking at 30° and cold air rising at 60°, which costs energy rather than releasing it. It exists only as a residual: it is what you get when you take the zonal average of a mid-latitude flow whose real heat transport is done by travelling depressions and anticyclones, not by any overturning circulation at all. Drawing it as a cell is a convenience of the zonal mean, and it is the reason mid-latitude weather looks nothing like tropical weather.

5 · The ITCZ moves, and the monsoon follows

Land heats fast, the ocean does not

The rising branch of the Hadley circulation — the intertropical convergence zone — follows the sun, migrating north in northern summer and south in southern summer. Over the oceans it moves a few degrees. Over the great continents it moves enormously, because land and ocean respond to the same seasonal forcing at completely different speeds.

That is the heat-capacity argument from page 2, used to make a wind reverse. Here it is with numbers.

The reversal, in one sentence each way. Summer: the land is far warmer than the sea, the air over it rises, surface pressure falls, and moist air is drawn in from the ocean — the wet southwesterly monsoon. Winter: the land is colder than the sea, air sinks over it, surface pressure rises, and dry air flows outward from the continent — the dry northeasterly monsoon. The ITCZ is dragged along with the rising branch, which is why the rain arrives with the wind.

Question. Push the ocean mixed layer down to 10 m. The contrast shrinks and the lag almost vanishes. What kind of coastline does that describe, and what would its climate be like? A shallow shelf sea or a large lake — the Baltic, the North Sea, the Great Lakes. With a small heat capacity it heats and cools nearly in step with the land, so it produces very little land–sea contrast in summer and therefore no monsoon; but it does freeze, and in autumn it is still warm while the land has already cooled, which is where lake-effect snow comes from. Heat capacity is not a detail of the coastline; it is the coastline's climate.

Computed. Each slab is C dT/dt = A cos(ωt) − λT with ω = 2π/year and λ = 5 W m−2K−1, solved for its steady periodic state: amplitude A/√(λ²+ω²C²) and phase lag arctan(ωC/λ)/ω. Land uses ρcp = 2.0×106 and ocean 4.10×106 J m−3K−1. This is a caricature: it has no advection, no clouds, no evaporative cooling that varies with surface type, and a single λ for both surfaces. It reproduces the observed order of magnitude — a subtropical continental interior swings by roughly 20 K about its mean and lags the sun by a few days, the sea by 2–4 K and lags by 6–10 weeks — and it is that contrast, not either number alone, that the demonstration is for.

What to carry forward

  • f = 2Ωsinφ. Zero at the equator, and that single fact reorganises tropical meteorology.
  • Geostrophy: wind blows along the isobars, at ug = |∇p|/(ρf). Friction turns it inward near the surface, and that is what makes lows rain.
  • Three cells, of which only two are real overturning circulations. Rising at 0° and 60°, sinking at 30° and 90°, and the world's deserts sit under the sinking branches.
  • Monsoons are heat capacity. Land and ocean given the same sun end up 20 K apart, and the wind follows the pressure that difference creates.
