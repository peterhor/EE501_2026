The cryosphere

Frozen water is a small part of the climate system by mass and an enormous one by leverage. It is the brightest surface on the planet, it hides a latent-heat buffer that holds the temperature at zero while it absorbs energy, it insulates the ocean from the air, and it holds enough water to move sea level by sixty-five metres.

That mismatch is the whole structure of the cryosphere's role: the thin, fast, seasonal ice does the radiative work, and the thick, slow, permanent ice does the sea level. They are almost separate subjects that happen to be made of the same substance.

Question. Switch on all floating sea ice. The sea-level contribution barely moves. Why — and why is it not exactly zero? Archimedes: floating ice already displaces a volume of water equal to its own weight, so melting it replaces the displaced water with an equal mass and the level does not change. That is why the ice cube in your drink does not overflow the glass. It is not exactly zero because sea ice is fresher than the seawater it floats in — roughly 4–6 psu against 34.7 — so the meltwater is less dense than the water it displaced and takes up slightly more room. Melting all the world's floating ice would raise sea level by of order 4 cm (Noerdlinger and Brower, 2007). A real effect, a genuinely negligible one, and a good test of whether someone understands the argument rather than remembering the slogan.

Question. The naive calculation gives about 65 m for Antarctica and the published figure is 58.3 m — an 11 % discrepancy. Where does the missing 7 metres go? A large fraction of the West Antarctic ice sheet, and parts of East Antarctica, sit on bedrock that is below sea level. That ice is already partly supported by, and already displacing, seawater. Only the portion above flotation adds new water to the ocean when it melts; the rest simply exchanges ice for water in a hole the sea already reaches. Published sea-level equivalents correct for this, and the naive calculation does not. It also matters far beyond bookkeeping: ice grounded below sea level on a bed that deepens inland is dynamically unstable, which is the marine ice-sheet instability in Lecture 8, part 7.

3 · The latent-heat buffer

Why ice holds the temperature at zero

Warming a kilogram of ice by one kelvin takes about 2100 J. Melting that same kilogram takes 334 000 J — enough to have warmed it by 160 K, if only it could get that cold. As long as ice and water coexist, the energy arriving goes into the phase change and the temperature does not move.

Lf = 3.34 × 105 J kg−1 the latent heat of fusion — the energy cost of turning ice at 0 °C into water at 0 °C, with nothing else changing
Lf latent heat of fusion of water — energy per kilogram needed to melt ice with no change in temperature, J kg−1

This is why melting is slow even when the forcing is large. It is also why the cryosphere's response is so nonlinear: nothing happens to the temperature at all until the ice is gone, and then everything happens at once.

Put the planetary energy imbalance against the ice

Earth is currently absorbing more energy than it emits. Ask how long that surplus would need to run to melt each piece of the cryosphere — first if every joule went into the ice, and then at the share that actually does.

IPCC AR6 assesses ~0.79 W m−2 for 2006–2018 and higher since.
Observed 2006–2018: ~89 % ocean, ~6 % land, ~4 % ice, ~1 % atmosphere.

The comparison to make out loud. All of Greenland represents roughly 9 × 1023 J of latent heat. The entire planetary energy imbalance, over the whole surface of the Earth, X every joule of the imbalance were pointed at Greenland it would take several decades to melt it, and at the few percent that actually goes into ice, thousands of years. Ice sheets are not slow because the physics is slow; they are slow because the energy is enormous. That is also exactly why the sea-level commitment is so long: nothing about the arithmetic gets faster if we stop emitting.

Question. If the arithmetic says thousands of years, why do glaciologists worry about metres of sea-level rise within a few centuries? Because ice sheets do not have to melt in place to reach the ocean. They can flow into it, and flowing does not require the latent heat — the melting then happens in the ocean, over a vastly larger area, at ocean temperatures. The energy argument above bounds the thermodynamic route. The dynamic route — acceleration of outlet glaciers, loss of buttressing ice shelves, retreat of a grounding line on a reverse-sloping bed — has no such bound, and it is where the deep uncertainty in sea-level projections lives. Lecture 8, part 7.

Computed. Energy required is V × ρi × Lf with ρi = 917 kg m−3 and Lf = 3.34×105 J kg−1; the energy supply is the imbalance times Earth's surface area, 5.10×1014 m², times the share you set. The imbalance and the partition are IPCC AR6 WG1 (2021) assessed values; everything else on the figure follows from them arithmetically. It is not a melt projection — real melting is governed by where the energy is delivered, not by the global total.

A factor of three in area, every year, on schedule. No other component does anything like this. It is why the cryosphere dominates the seasonal cycle of planetary albedo, and why high-latitude ecosystems are organised around the date the snow goes rather than around the temperature.

Question. The Antarctic seasonal swing is far larger than the Arctic one. Why? Geography. The Arctic Ocean is an almost enclosed basin surrounded by land, so its winter ice has nowhere to grow beyond the coastlines, and much of it survives the summer as thick multi-year ice. Antarctic sea ice grows outward into an open Southern Ocean with no boundary at all, reaching far equatorward in winter, and then melts almost completely because that same open ocean is warm and stormy in summer. The result is that Antarctic ice is mostly thin, mostly first-year, and mostly seasonal, and Arctic ice is not. The same hemispheric asymmetry of land and sea explains a great deal of the difference between the two polar climates.

5 · Permafrost: the part that is not about water

Frozen ground, and what is frozen into it

Permafrost is ground that has stayed below 0 °C for at least two consecutive years. It underlies roughly 15 million km² of the northern hemisphere — about a sixth of the exposed land in the north — and in places it is over 1000 m deep. Each summer a shallow active layer, a few tens of centimetres to a few metres, thaws and refreezes; everything below stays frozen.

Its climatic significance is carbon. Cold, waterlogged, frozen soils do not decompose. Over tens of thousands of years they have accumulated an estimated 1400–1600 Pg of carbon in the top three metres alone — roughly twice as much as the entire atmosphere holds.

Question. Why is permafrost thaw treated as a feedback rather than as an emission? Because it is caused by the warming rather than by a decision. An emission is a forcing we choose; permafrost carbon is released because the climate warmed, and the release then warms it further. That makes it a positive carbon-cycle feedback, and it means it cannot be mitigated directly — only by not causing the warming in the first place. It also means it must be subtracted from the remaining carbon budget rather than added to the emissions, which is precisely how Lecture 8, part 6 treats it. The further complication is which gas: dry aerobic thaw releases CO2, waterlogged anaerobic thaw releases methane, and a tonne of carbon as methane is far more potent over the timescales that matter.

The permafrost carbon feedback is quantified in Lecture 6, part 6, and the methane cycle itself in Lecture 4, part 4.

What to carry forward

  • Two cryospheres. Thin, seasonal, enormous in area — snow and sea ice, doing the radiative work. Thick, permanent, enormous in volume — the ice sheets, doing the sea level.
  • 65 m of sea level is stored as ice, of which 58 in Antarctica; floating ice contributes essentially nothing when it melts.
  • The latent-heat buffer is huge. Ice holds the temperature at 0 °C while it absorbs energy, and melting it takes centuries of the entire planetary imbalance.
  • Permafrost is a carbon store, about twice the atmosphere, and its release is a feedback rather than an emission.
  • Everything here interacts with the ocean through brine and freshwater, and with the atmosphere through albedo and insulation.
