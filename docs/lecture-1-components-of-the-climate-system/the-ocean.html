The ocean

Two things move the ocean: the wind rubbing on the top of it, and differences in density. The first drives the gyres and, through a beautiful and thoroughly unintuitive piece of physics, the upwelling that feeds most of the world's fisheries. The second drives the overturning that stores heat and carbon for a thousand years.

2 · The wind drives the surface

Gyres, and why their western sides are so violent

Goosse et al. (2010), Figs. 1.9–1.10.

The surface currents are, to first order, the atmospheric wind belts from the previous page painted onto the water. Trade winds from the east in the tropics, westerlies in mid-latitudes, and the ocean between them turns in a great closed loop: a subtropical gyre, clockwise in the northern hemisphere and anticlockwise in the southern.

Those gyres are not symmetric. Their western limbs — the Gulf Stream, the Kuroshio, the Agulhas, the Brazil Current — are narrow, deep and fast, carrying 30–150 Sv at speeds over 1 m s−1. The return flow on the eastern side is broad, shallow and sluggish. Western intensification is a consequence of the Coriolis parameter increasing with latitude, and it is why northwest Europe has the climate it does.

One sverdrup (Sv) is 106 m3 s−1 — about a hundred times the flow of the Amazon. The Gulf Stream at its peak carries roughly 150 Sv, the Atlantic overturning about 17 Sv, and the Antarctic Circumpolar Current around 135 Sv, the largest current on Earth and the only one that goes all the way round.

The observed surface-current map (Fig. 1.10, from satellite-tracked drifting buoys) is not reproduced here — it is an observational field and the lecturer shows the original. What is reproduced below is the mechanism that connects the wind to the water.

3 · The Ekman spiral

The water does not go where the wind is blowing

Wind drags on the surface. That surface layer is turned to the right by Coriolis (in the northern hemisphere), so it moves at an angle to the wind rather than with it. It then drags the layer beneath it, which is also turned to the right, and so is the layer beneath that. Each successive layer moves more slowly and points further round. The velocity vectors trace a spiral with depth, first worked out by Vagn Walfrid Ekman in 1905 after Fridtjof Nansen noticed that sea ice on the Fram expedition drifted 20–40° to the right of the wind rather than downwind.

surface current: 45° to the right of the wind   ·   depth-integrated transport: M = τ / (ρf), at 90° to the right The 90° result does not depend on the eddy viscosity, on the depth, or on any detail of how the turbulence works. It follows from the force balance alone.
M Ekman mass transport integrated over the depth of the spiral, per metre of width across the flow, kg m−1 s−1
τ wind stress exerted on the sea surface, N m−2
ρ seawater density, ≈1027 kg m−3
f Coriolis parameter, f = 2Ω sinφ, s−1

That independence is the important part. The shape of the spiral depends on a turbulent mixing coefficient nobody can measure well. The total transport does not depend on it at all — so the one quantity that matters for the ocean's large-scale circulation is also the one we can calculate confidently. It is rare and worth pointing at.

Question. Drag the eddy viscosity from one end to the other. The spiral changes shape completely and the transport does not move at all. Why not? Because the depth-integrated balance is between just two terms: the wind stress applied at the top, and the Coriolis force acting on the total transport. Friction inside the fluid moves momentum between layers but cannot change the total — internal stresses cancel in pairs when you integrate. So M = τ/(ρf) exactly, whatever the mixing does. Az decides how that transport is distributed over depth, which matters for biology and for the surface temperature, and not at all for the large-scale circulation.

Question. Take the latitude down towards 5°. The transport becomes enormous. Is that physical? No — and the formula tells you why it fails. M ∝ 1/f, and f → 0 at the equator, so the expression diverges. The underlying assumption — that Coriolis balances the stress — stops being true when Coriolis is negligible, exactly as geostrophic balance failed at the equator on the previous page. Real equatorial dynamics is a different problem, and the fact that Ekman transport changes sign across the equator is what produces equatorial upwelling.

A real ocean has an eddy viscosity that varies with depth and with the state of the surface, and observed spirals are consequently flatter and shallower than the classical one; the transport, however, is well verified.

4 · What Ekman transport does when it hits something

Coastal and equatorial upwelling

If the depth-integrated transport is 90° to the right of the wind, then a wind blowing along a coastline with the land on its left pushes surface water away from the shore. Water cannot be removed from a place and not replaced, so it is replaced from below. That is coastal upwelling.

The same argument works at the equator without any coast at all. The trade winds blow from the east on both sides; north of the equator the transport is to the right (northward), south of it to the left (southward). The surface water diverges away from the equator in both directions, and deep water rises to fill the gap. That is equatorial upwelling, and it is a permanent feature of the Pacific — except when it is not, which is El Niño.

Why anyone cares

  • Deep water is cold: coastal upwelling makes the sea off Peru, California, Namibia and Morocco much colder than their latitude suggests, and produces coastal fog and coastal deserts.
  • Deep water is full of nutrients, because everything that sinks decomposes down there. Upwelling zones are about 1 % of the ocean surface and support roughly half of the world's fish catch.
  • Deep water is rich in CO2, so upwelling regions outgas carbon — the equatorial Pacific is one of the largest natural CO2 sources on the planet.

And when the wind stops

  • Weak trades mean weak equatorial upwelling, so the eastern Pacific warms: El Niño. Strong trades cool it further: La Niña.
  • The warm water then changes where the convection is, which changes the winds, which changes the upwelling — a coupled ocean–atmosphere feedback, not an ocean event.
  • The Peruvian anchovy fishery, one of the largest in the world, collapses in strong El Niño years because the nutrient supply is switched off.

Question. A northerly wind blows down the coast of California, which lies on the western side of the continent. Does that produce upwelling or downwelling? What about the same wind on the coast of Portugal? California: the wind blows south, so the transport is 90° to its right, i.e. westward — offshore. Upwelling, which is why the sea at San Francisco is about 12 °C in July. Portugal is also a west-facing coast, so a northerly there does exactly the same thing, and the Portuguese and Moroccan upwelling system is one of the four great eastern-boundary upwelling regions for that reason. Reverse the wind and you get downwelling instead, piling warm surface water against the coast. The rule is not about which continent — it is about which way the wind is blowing relative to the coastline.

5 · Density, and why the ocean has a memory

Two variables, one awkward equation of state

Air has an equation of state you can write on one line. Seawater does not. Its density depends on temperature, salinity and pressure, and it is non-linear in all three — which turns out not to be an inconvenience but the source of some of the ocean's most important behaviour.

  • Cold water is denser. Roughly, and increasingly so as it gets colder: the thermal expansion coefficient of seawater at 0 °C is about a quarter of its value at 25 °C. Near freezing, temperature almost stops mattering.
  • Salty water is denser. Almost linearly, and it never stops mattering. So in polar water, where temperature has little leverage left, salinity is in charge.
  • Where does salinity come from? Evaporation concentrates salt; precipitation, river runoff and melting ice dilute it. Freezing sea ice rejects its salt into the water below, which is one of the few processes on Earth that makes very dense water quickly.

Two independent controls on one quantity is what allows more than one stable state. If density depended on temperature alone, the overturning circulation could only do one thing. Because temperature and salinity can compete — cold and fresh against warm and salty — the same circulation can have two configurations, and can switch between them. That is the origin of the abrupt changes in Lecture 7, part 4.

Cabbeling: mixing two waters makes something denser than either average. Because the density contours are curved, the midpoint of a straight line drawn between two parcels on this diagram lies on a denser contour than the average of their two densities. Take two parcels of exactly equal density — press the first preset — mix them, and the result sinks below both of them. Nothing was added and no heat was lost. This is a real process and it contributes to deep-water formation in the Southern Ocean and the Labrador Sea. It exists only because the equation of state is non-linear, and it is the cleanest possible demonstration that the non-linearity is not a numerical nuisance.

Question. In the Arctic, surface water is often near −1.8 °C and quite fresh. Cool it further and almost nothing happens to its density. Why, and what makes deep water form there instead? Because the thermal expansion coefficient collapses near freezing — you can see it on the diagram as the density contours becoming nearly horizontal at low temperature. Cooling buys you almost no extra density, and the water cannot get colder than its freezing point anyway. What does work is salt: when sea ice forms it excludes most of its salt, which is rejected as dense brine into the water below. Brine rejection under growing sea ice, together with evaporation in the Nordic Seas, is what makes the water dense enough to sink. This is why where the sea ice sits and the deep circulation are coupled, and it is in the interaction matrix on page 2.

Question. How can ocean water have a temperature below 0 °C? Because salt lowers the freezing point. At a salinity of 34.7 the freezing temperature is about −1.9 °C — the dashed line on the diagram, computed from the standard relation. Antarctic Bottom Water leaves the shelf at close to that temperature and keeps it, because there is nothing down there to warm it. So the coldest water in the ocean is well below zero, is entirely liquid, and stays that way for a thousand years.

6 · The overturning circulation

Where the ocean actually sinks

Goosse et al. (2010), Fig. 1.12, after Rahmstorf (2002) and Kuhlbrodt et al. (2007). Not reproduced here — show the original.

Almost the entire ocean is stably stratified, and deep water forms in only two small regions on the whole planet: the Nordic and Labrador Seas in the North Atlantic, and a few polynyas on the Antarctic shelf. Surface water there becomes dense enough to sink to the bottom, spreads equatorward at depth, and returns — slowly, diffusely, and by routes that are still argued about — to the surface elsewhere. A parcel entering the deep North Atlantic will typically not see daylight again for about a thousand years.

Put those four on the diagram above and the structure of the deep ocean falls out. AABW ends up beneath NADW, AAIW sits above both as a salinity minimum, and the Mediterranean water — warm, but so salty that it is denser than the Atlantic water at its own level — sinks until it finds its density and then spreads sideways at about 1000 m. Nobody decided this ordering; the equation of state did. One caveat, and it is the subject of the question below: the AABW–NADW ordering is not resolvable on a surface-referenced diagram, and needs pressure to be included before it comes out right.

Question, and the demonstration above gives an honest and awkward answer. AABW forms at about −0.6 °C and 34.65; NADW at about 3 °C and 34.95. AABW is fresher, yet it lies underneath NADW everywhere they meet. Press the second preset and see whether the diagram actually shows that. It barely does. At surface reference the two come out at σt = 27.848 and 27.845 — a difference of three thousandths of a kilogram per cubic metre, which is three parts in ten million of the density. The cooling and the freshening very nearly cancel. That near-cancellation is the point, and it is why σt is the wrong tool for deep water. Seawater's thermal expansion coefficient increases with pressure, so under 400 atmospheres the colder water gains proportionally more density than the warmer one, and AABW pulls clearly ahead. Oceanographers therefore compare deep water using potential density referenced to 4000 dbar (σ4) rather than to the surface. The diagram above omits pressure entirely, which is stated in its note — and this question is the case where that omission actually changes the answer. The general lesson survives intact: the whole vertical structure of the world ocean rests on density differences of order a hundredth of a kilogram per cubic metre, which is why a modest freshening of the North Atlantic is taken seriously.

The surface layer, and its seasonal cycle

Goosse et al. (2010), Figs. 1.14–1.15.

The top of the ocean is a well-mixed layer of nearly uniform temperature, with a sharp thermocline at its base. Its depth is set by a competition, and the competition runs on an annual cycle:

Stabilises — makes the layer shallow

  • Surface heating in spring and summer
  • Precipitation and river runoff, freshening the top
  • Ice melt, which is both fresh and cold but overwhelmingly fresh

Destabilises — makes it deep

  • Surface cooling in autumn and winter
  • Evaporation, which concentrates salt
  • Ice formation, and the brine it rejects
  • Wind stirring, which mixes mechanically regardless of density

The consequence is a mixed layer of 20–40 m in late summer and 100–200 m in late winter at mid-latitudes, and far more than that in the deep-convection regions. That is a factor of five in heat capacity within one year, which is why the effective τ of the ocean surface on page 2 is a seasonal quantity rather than a constant. It is also why spring blooms happen when they do: phytoplankton need to be held in the light, and they cannot be until the layer shallows.

What to carry forward

  • The wind drives the surface. Ekman transport is 90° to the right of the wind in the north, and its magnitude τ/(ρf) does not depend on any turbulence parameter.
  • Where that transport diverges, deep water rises: coastal and equatorial upwelling, cold, nutrient-rich, and responsible for a disproportionate share of the world's biology.
  • Density depends on temperature and salinity, non-linearly. Near freezing, salinity is in charge.
  • Deep water forms in two small regions and takes about a thousand years to return. That is the ocean's memory, and the climate system's.
  • The mixed layer breathes annually, and its depth sets both the heat capacity and the light available to life.
