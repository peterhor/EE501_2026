The biosphere

Seven orders of magnitude lighter than the ocean, and it runs the carbon cycle. The biosphere is the component with the worst ratio of mass to influence in the entire system — and the one this course exists to connect the physics to. It is not a passenger. It sets most of the land surface properties from the previous page, and it decides where a large fraction of the carbon goes.

1 · Tiny in mass, decisive in function

Stock is not flux

Compare the mass column with what each component actually does. This is the clearest case in the system where the small number is the misleading one.

ComponentMassWhat it does with it
Hydrosphere1.4 × 1021 kg Stores heat and carbon for centuries.
Atmosphere5.1 × 1018 kg Moves heat and water across a hemisphere in days.
Biosphere~2 × 1015 kg C Exchanges about a seventh of the atmosphere's entire carbon content with it every single year, and sets the albedo, roughness and evapotranspiration of the land it grows on.
— of which marine biota~3 Pg C Three petagrams, turning over in days, running the biological pump that keeps atmospheric CO2 roughly 200 ppm lower than it would otherwise be.

Marine life is three petagrams and runs a global cycle. Land vegetation holds about half as much carbon as the atmosphere and exchanges roughly a seventh of the atmosphere's content with it annually — which is why the Keeling curve has visible teeth. A component's importance is set by its flux and by what it controls, not by what it weighs.

Where the biosphere already appeared in this lecture. It is the reason the boreal albedo question has no single answer; it supplies the transpiration that sets the water partition; and it is one of the five rows in the interaction matrix. This page asks what governs it, rather than what it does to everything else.

2 · What limits growth, and where

Two ceilings again — and the lower one wins

The land surface page split precipitation by asking which was scarcer, water or energy. Productivity obeys the same logic, and this is not a coincidence: plants evaporate water in order to acquire carbon, so the constraint on one is the constraint on the other.

The Miami model (Lieth, 1975) is the simplest useful statement of it. It predicts net primary production from two numbers — mean annual temperature and annual precipitation — by computing a ceiling from each and taking the smaller:

NPPT = 3000 / (1 + e1.315 − 0.119 T)      NPPP = 3000 (1 − e−0.000664 P) T in °C, P in mm yr−1, NPP in g dry matter m−2 yr−1
NPP net primary production — what the vegetation builds and keeps, after its own respiration
NPPT the ceiling temperature alone would allow
NPPP the ceiling water alone would allow
T mean annual temperature, °C
P annual precipitation, mm yr−1
3000 the empirical global maximum, g m−2 yr−1
NPP = min( NPPT , NPPP ) Liebig's law of the minimum: growth is set by the scarcest requirement, not by the average of them

It is an empirical fit, and it is deliberately crude. Two predictors, three parameters, no soil, no species, no CO2, no nutrients, no seasonality. It nonetheless reproduces the broad global pattern of productivity, which tells you something worth knowing: at the largest scale, climate really is the first-order control on the biosphere. Everything else is a correction to that — an important correction, and still a correction.

Question. Press Sahara and then Arctic tundra. Both are unproductive. Does it follow that warming will affect them the same way? No, and the model tells you why in one glance. The Sahara is water-limited — its temperature ceiling is very high and irrelevant, so warming alone changes nothing about its productivity and only a change in rainfall would. The tundra is temperature-limited — it has water it cannot use, so warming lifts the binding constraint directly and productivity rises fast. Same low NPP, opposite sensitivity. This is exactly why high latitudes are greening while drylands are not, and it is the single most useful thing this two-parameter model will tell you.

Question. Press Savannah (25 °C, 700 mm). Now raise the temperature and watch what happens to the prediction. Why is the model's answer misleading here? The prediction barely moves, because the site is water-limited and the temperature ceiling is not binding. But in reality warming increases evaporative demand, so the same 700 mm supports less growth than it did — the water ceiling should fall as temperature rises, and in the Miami model it does not, because the two predictors are treated as independent. That is the model's biggest structural flaw, and it is why serious productivity models use a water balance (the Budyko logic from the previous page) rather than raw precipitation. Knowing where a model is wrong is worth more than knowing what it outputs.

Computed from the Miami model exactly as printed above (Lieth, H., 1975, Modeling the primary productivity of the world, in Lieth & Whittaker, eds., Primary Productivity of the Biosphere, Springer). It is an empirical regression on two predictors, not a mechanistic model: no soil, no nutrients, no species composition, no CO2 fertilisation, no seasonality, and — as the second question above draws out — no coupling between the temperature and water terms. Outputs are grams of dry matter; multiply by roughly 0.45 for grams of carbon. Use it for the pattern and the limiting factor, never for a site-specific number.

3 · Why vegetation zones look the way they do

Two variables predict most of it

Look at any map of world vegetation and the pattern is dominated by bands running east–west, not north–south. That is because the first-order control is temperature, set by latitude through insolation. The second-order control is precipitation, set by the circulation from part 4 — and precipitation is what breaks the bands up, because it varies strongly with longitude.

The Köppen–Geiger classification formalises exactly this: it is a decision tree operating on twelve monthly temperatures and twelve monthly precipitation totals, and nothing else. No vegetation data go into it, and yet it reproduces the world's biomes remarkably well — which is itself the evidence that temperature and precipitation are the controls.

Set an annual climatology and the classifier runs the published Köppen–Geiger criteria on it, exactly as Peel, Finlayson and McMahon (2007) state them. Watch which threshold you are closest to — that is where a climate zone will move first.

Question (the breakout discussion from the lecture). Are vegetation and climate zones more dependent on latitude or on longitude? Are there exceptions? And which two climate parameters do most of the work? Latitude first, because insolation and therefore temperature are set by it — that is why the zones form bands. The exceptions are all about circulation and ocean. Western Europe is several classes milder than eastern North America at the same latitude, because of the North Atlantic Current and the prevailing westerlies. The Atacama and the Namib are deserts on the coast, because of cold upwelling water offshore — the Ekman argument from page 5. Mountains create their own zones vertically, compressing into two kilometres what latitude spreads over four thousand. And the great continental interiors are dry simply because they are far from any ocean. The two parameters are temperature and precipitation, which is not a coincidence — they are the two things the classifier is given, and it works.

Question. The classifier and the productivity model take the same two inputs and give different outputs. What does each one add that the other does not? The classifier is categorical and seasonal: it uses twelve months, so it can tell a Mediterranean winter-wet climate from a monsoon summer-wet one with identical annual totals — and those two support completely different vegetation. The Miami model is continuous and annual: it throws the seasonality away but gives a number, and it names the limiting factor. Neither is complete. Between them they capture the two things a first-order description of the biosphere needs: what kind of place is this, and how much can grow here and why not more.

Computed, from published criteria. The classifier implements the Köppen–Geiger decision tree as set out by Peel, Finlayson and McMahon (2007), including the seasonal precipitation threshold and the summer/winter half-year definitions. The monthly climatology it is fed is synthetic — a sinusoidal temperature cycle and a precipitation cycle generated from the four numbers you set. The preset buttons use approximate, rounded normals for those places, adequate for classification practice and not a data source; look up the real station normals if you need them. A real classification uses twelve observed monthly means, and a place close to a threshold can change class depending on which reference period is used.

Two numbers to hold together. Land vegetation is 450 Pg against an atmosphere of 890 — so the entire living land biosphere is worth about half the air's carbon. And the soil beneath it holds three to five times more than the plants growing on it. Most of the land's carbon is dead, in the ground, and slow. That is why land-use change is such a large term in the carbon budget: cutting a forest releases the trees quickly and then keeps releasing the soil for decades.

5 · The feedbacks the biosphere runs

Both signs are present, and that is the summary

Each of these is a two-way coupling in the interaction matrix on page 2. The sign is given here; the physics is Lecture 6.

MechanismSignHow it works
Snow masking by tall canopypositive Warmer → treeline advances → darker winter surface → warmer. The boreal albedo trap from the previous page.
CO2 fertilisationnegative More CO2 → faster growth → more uptake → less CO2. Saturates, and is limited by nitrogen and water.
Soil respirationpositive Warmer → faster decomposition → more CO2 released → warmer.
Vegetation–precipitation couplingpositive More vegetation → more evapotranspiration and lower albedo → more rainfall → more vegetation. The Sahara mechanism.
Evaporative coolingnegative Warmer and wetter → more transpiration → more latent heat flux → cooler surface.
Fire and disturbanceboth Releases carbon (positive) and resets albedo (either sign, depending on what regrows).

The biosphere is not a thermostat and it is not an amplifier — it is a set of competing mechanisms whose net effect depends on region, on timescale and on how far the system has already moved. Where a positive feedback happens to dominate strongly enough, the response stops being proportional to the forcing and becomes a threshold. That is what PC Lab 1 and PC Lab 3 are both about, and you will run both this week.

Question. Of the six rows above, which are already visible in observations, and which are still mostly model results? Observed: CO2 fertilisation, in the sense that the land sink has grown roughly in step with emissions and satellites show widespread greening; treeline advance and Arctic shrubification; and fire, unambiguously. Partly observed: soil respiration — the temperature sensitivity is well measured in the laboratory and at plot scale, but its global integral is poorly constrained. Mostly model: the vegetation–precipitation coupling at large scale, because you cannot run the experiment. Being able to say which category a claim falls into is a large part of reading this literature, and it is the same habit the provenance appendix is training for figures.

6 · The whole lecture in one place

What to take away from Lecture 1

  • The climate system has five components — atmosphere, hydrosphere, cryosphere, land surface, biosphere — and climate is a description of the whole thing, not just the air.
  • Each component has its own timescale, from days for the atmosphere to millennia for the deep ocean and the ice sheets, and mostly it is set by heat capacity.
  • Every component interacts with every other, usually through the atmosphere, and the couplings are not symmetric.
  • The general circulations of the atmosphere and ocean exist to move heat from where there is a surplus to where there is a deficit, and rotation is what gives them their structure.
  • Recurring themes, all of which return in later lectures: the interface between atmosphere and ocean; the arrangement of land and sea; differences in heat capacity; topography; seasonality in the biosphere and cryosphere; albedo; and the carbon cycle.
  • And the question deliberately left open: where do human activities belong in this diagram — as a sixth component, or as a forcing from outside?
