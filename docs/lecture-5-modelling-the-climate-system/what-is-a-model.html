Discretising the planet

A climate model is a set of equations plus a way of solving them. The equations come next; this page is about the way of solving them — chopping the atmosphere and ocean into boxes, stepping forward in time, and deciding what to do about everything that is smaller than a box.

The definition, and the two halves of it

Goosse et al., ch. 3. Worth reading twice, because the second half is the part that is easy to skip and is where nearly all the practical difficulty lives.

“A climate model is based on a mathematical representation of the climate system derived from physical, biological and chemical principles, and on the way the resulting equations are solved.”

Two models can share the first half exactly — the same conservation laws, written the same way — and still disagree, because they discretise differently, run at different resolution, and make different choices about the processes their grid cannot see. That is why an ensemble of models is not a redundant exercise, and it is the reason page 5 of this set exists.

These equations cannot be solved with pen and paper. There is no analytical solution to the equations of fluid motion on a rotating sphere with moisture, radiation and a lower boundary made of mountains, forests and sea ice. So they are solved numerically: the continuous fields are replaced by values at a finite number of points, the derivatives by differences between neighbouring points, and the evolution by a march of finite time steps.

One cell, one time step

What actually happens inside the model

Every time step, for every cell: exchange with the six neighbours (that is the dynamics, and it comes straight from the equations on page 3), then apply everything that happens within the cell and is too small to see (that is the physics, and it is parameterised).

The loop is that simple, and that is the whole trick. Compute the tendencies, add them up, advance every variable by one Δt, repeat. A century-long simulation at a 20-minute time step is about 2.6 million passes through that loop, for every one of several million cells.

The left half of the picture is exact physics, approximated numerically. The right half is approximate physics, applied exactly. Model uncertainty lives overwhelmingly on the right.

What this grid resolves. A feature needs roughly four grid points across it before a model can do anything sensible with it — two points is a wave the numerics cannot represent, not a mountain. So the working rule is resolved if Δx ≤ L/4. Everything else has to be parameterised: represented by a formula that predicts its large-scale effect from the large-scale variables the model does carry.

    Why the cost grows so fast

    This is the single reason climate modelling is a supercomputing problem rather than a laptop problem.

    cost  ∝  Nx Ny Nz × (1/Δt)  ∝  (1/Δx)2 × Nz × (1/Δx)  =  Nz Δx−3
    cost total computational work of a simulation of fixed length
    Nx, Ny number of grid points in the two horizontal directions
    Nz number of vertical levels
    Δt time step, s — it must shrink with Δx to satisfy the CFL condition
    Δx horizontal grid spacing, m

    The last factor is the one people forget. You cannot refine the grid and keep the time step: the CFL condition requires that nothing travels more than one grid box in one step, so Δt must shrink with Δx. Halving the grid spacing therefore costs eight times more, not four — and if you add vertical levels as well, more still.

    Computer power doubled roughly every 1.2 years over the period covered by the IPCC reports. Eight times more work is three doublings, so about 3 × 1.2 ≈ 3.6 years of hardware progress buys one halving of the grid spacing. That single number explains the whole history below: four IPCC reports, sixteen years, grid spacing down by a factor of about four.

    Ocean components are usually finer than the atmosphere by a factor of two to four, because the length scale that has to be resolved there — the Rossby radius of deformation, which sets the size of an ocean eddy — is tens of kilometres rather than a thousand.

    Question. The Earth Simulator (Japan, 2003) held the world’s fastest-computer title and was built substantially for climate work. Using the scaling above: if it could run a 100 km, 40-level model at an acceptable speed, and computer power had kept doubling every 1.2 years ever since, what grid spacing should be routine today — and is it? Twenty-two years is about eighteen doublings, a factor of 260 000 in raw power, which is six halvings of Δx: roughly 1.5 km. Routine CMIP6 runs are at 100 km. The gap is real and worth understanding: parallel efficiency, memory bandwidth, storage for the output, the number of years and ensemble members you need, and the end of processor frequency scaling around 2005. Resolution is only one of the things you can spend a supercomputer on, and often not the most valuable one.

    Parameterisation

    Replacing a process by its effect

    Goosse’s definition: a method of replacing processes that are too small-scale or complex to be physically represented in the model by a simplified process. A parameterisation does not simulate the process. It predicts what the process would have done to the grid-box mean.

    effect on the resolved state  =  f ( resolved state, tunable parameters )
    effect on the resolved state the tendency the unresolved process imposes on the grid-box mean — heating, drying, momentum drag
    f the parameterisation itself: a diagnostic function, not a simulation of the process
    resolved state the variables the model actually carries in that grid box — temperature, humidity, wind
    tunable parameters coefficients with no directly measurable value, fixed by calibration

    The right-hand column is where model differences come from. Those parameters are not measured constants. Several have no direct observational counterpart at all, because the quantity they describe only exists once you have agreed to average over a grid box. Choosing their values is calibration, and page 5 is about how to do that honestly.

    Question. Two modelling groups solve the same seven equations, at the same resolution, with the same forcing, and get warming rates that differ by 40 %. Where does the difference come from? Overwhelmingly from the cloud and convection parameterisations, through the cloud feedback. Cloud feedback has been the largest single contributor to the spread in climate sensitivity in every model generation from FAR to AR6 — which is to say, the biggest uncertainty in climate projection comes from the half of the model that is not solved from first principles.

    Two kinds of input from outside the model

    A model does not generate its own world. Some things are handed to it. The distinction between these two categories matters because it decides what a simulation is allowed to answer.

    Boundary conditions — generally fixed for the whole run. They define the stage.

    • Land–sea distribution and orography
    • Ocean bathymetry
    • Soil type and rooting depth
    • Earth’s radius, rotation rate, gravity

    External forcings — prescribed but time-varying. They drive the change you are trying to simulate.

    • Solar irradiance and orbital parameters
    • Volcanic aerosol loading
    • Greenhouse gas concentrations, or emissions
    • Anthropogenic aerosols and land-use change
    The line between the two categories moves as models improve, and that movement is the whole story of model development. Sea surface temperature was a boundary condition in the 1970s and is a prognostic variable now. Vegetation cover was a fixed map, then became interactive. CO2 concentration is a forcing in a CMIP concentration-driven run and a prognostic variable in an emission-driven Earth system model. Ice sheets are still largely a boundary condition, and are the next to move. When you read a model result, ask first which side of this line each quantity sat on — a model cannot be wrong about something it was told.

    Question. PC Lab 3 produces an abrupt vegetation collapse in the Sahara. Could a model with vegetation as a boundary condition produce that result at all? No. The collapse is a feedback between vegetation and precipitation, so vegetation has to be prognostic and coupled to the atmosphere. Prescribing it removes the very loop being studied — the model would run perfectly happily and tell you nothing.
