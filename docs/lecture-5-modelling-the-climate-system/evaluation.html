Does the model work?

Three words get used interchangeably and mean quite different things: verification, validation and calibration. Getting them apart is the difference between testing a model and tuning it until it agrees with you.

Three questions, three answers

Each row answers a different question. Only the first has a right answer.

 The questionHow you would fail itWhat you do about it
Verification Does the code solve the equations it claims to solve? The numerical scheme is unstable, does not conserve mass, or converges to the wrong answer as the grid is refined. Analytical test cases, convergence tests, conservation checks. This is mathematics, and it can be settled definitively.
Validation Are the results close enough to reality for the purpose at hand? The equations are solved perfectly and the climate is still wrong, because the equations, the resolution or the parameterisations are inadequate. Comparison against observations and reanalyses, past and present. Never complete — passing every test you have thought of is not the same as being right.
Calibration What values should the free parameters take? Not a test at all. It is an adjustment, and it can be used to hide a failed validation. Adjust parameters within their physically plausible range, against a small number of large-scale targets, and then declare what you did.
Calibration is legitimate, and it is also the easiest thing in modelling to abuse. It is legitimate because some parameters have no measured value — the entrainment rate of a cumulus plume is not a quantity anyone has ever observed. It is abused when it is used to compensate for a deficiency elsewhere: a model that gets the global mean temperature right because two large errors were tuned to cancel will get the response to a perturbation wrong, and no amount of agreement with the present day will reveal that. The rule of thumb that survives: tune against far fewer quantities than you intend to evaluate, and never tune against the thing you are trying to predict.

Question. A model is tuned so that its top-of-atmosphere radiation balance is within 0.1 W m−2 of observations. Is it then fair to present its simulated global mean temperature as evidence that the model is good? Only if the temperature was not itself part of what was tuned — and it usually is, indirectly, because the radiation balance is adjusted through cloud parameters that strongly control temperature. The transparent presentation states the tuning targets explicitly and then evaluates against something else: the seasonal cycle, the response to a volcanic eruption, the last glacial maximum, variability at interannual timescales. Those are not tuned, so agreement there means something.

Putting a number on it

The simplest metrics, and what each one hides

Every performance metric is a summary, and every summary throws information away. Knowing which information is the skill.

bias = (1/n) Σk (Tmod,k − Tobs,k)     RMSE = √[ (1/n) Σk (Tmod,k − Tobs,k)2 ]

RMSE root-mean-square error — errors squared so they cannot cancel, K

Bias can be zero when everything is wrong. A model too warm in the tropics and equally too cold at the poles has no bias at all. This is not a hypothetical — it is the normal situation, and it is why the RMSE, which squares the errors so they cannot cancel, is reported alongside it. Neither number tells you where the error is, which is why the standard figures in an IPCC chapter are maps rather than tables.

Question. Set the shared structural error to zero and drag the ensemble size up. The multi-model mean gets arbitrarily good. Now set it to 2 K and try again. What has changed, and what does that say about the practice of using more and more models? Independent errors cancel as 1/√N, so with no shared error the ensemble mean converges on the truth. Shared error does not cancel at all — averaging fifty copies of the same mistake gives you the mistake. Beyond a modest ensemble size the multi-model mean stops improving and sits on the floor set by whatever all the models get wrong together: usually the same missing process, or the same parameterisation lineage, since many models share components and code. This is why an ensemble spread is not an uncertainty estimate. It measures how much the models disagree, not how wrong they all are.

Question (from the lecture slides). AR5 shows two maps side by side: the difference between the multi-model mean and the reanalysis, and the mean of the individual models’ absolute errors. Why show both, and which one is bigger? The second is always bigger — it is the “typical member” figure above, and it is the fair measure of how good one model is. The first is the “multi-model mean” figure, and it is what you get if you use the ensemble as a single best estimate. Showing only the first flatters the models; showing only the second hides the fact that the ensemble mean really is more skilful. The gap between the two maps is the amount of error that cancels — and the parts of the map where the two are equal are where every model is wrong in the same direction.

Standard tests

Model intercomparison projects

If every group ran whatever experiment it liked, no two results could be compared. A MIP defines the experiments precisely — same forcing, same period, same output format — so that the differences between models are differences between models.

ExperimentSetupWhat it tests
piControlPre-industrial forcing, held constant, for 500–1000 years Drift and internal variability. Everything else is measured as a departure from this.

CMIP is why the IPCC can say anything quantitative at all. CMIP5 underpinned AR5, CMIP6 underpins AR6, and the archives are open, so the figures in those reports can be reproduced by anyone. The design is also the reason the shared error in the demonstration above is not a hypothetical: the participating models share components, share parameterisation schemes and share a scientific lineage, so their errors are correlated by construction.

Where models do well, and where they do not

A rough guide to how much to trust which variable, from the CMIP5 and CMIP6 evaluations. Read it as orders of magnitude.

The ordering is not accidental. Surface temperature is constrained by an energy balance that the model solves from first principles, so it is hard to get very wrong. Precipitation is produced almost entirely by parameterised convection, so it is hard to get right — and the classic failures, a double ITCZ in the tropical Pacific and too much drizzle and too little heavy rain, have survived three generations of models. Sea ice sits at the far end because it depends on the ocean, the atmosphere and its own dynamics, and because a small temperature error near freezing translates into a large area error.

Question. For an ecological study of species range shifts, which of these errors matters most, and what does that imply about how you should use model output? Almost always precipitation and its seasonality, because water availability sets range limits over most of the world, and that is exactly the field models are worst at. The implication is practical: use model anomalies rather than absolute values — add the simulated change to an observed present-day climatology, rather than using the simulated climate directly. That is the delta method in the next demonstration, and it is the standard practice in impact studies for precisely this reason.

The anomaly, or delta, method

Xcorrected(t) bias-corrected value of the variable at time t

Written this way the logic is obvious: keep the model’s anomaly, throw away its absolute value, and graft the anomaly onto the observed mean over a reference period. It is the most-used correction in the whole of impact modelling, and it assumes only that the bias is constant in time.

Which is exactly what a changing climate is likely to break. Suppose the model is 2 K too warm in the reference period because it loses its snow cover a month too early. In a warmer future the real world also loses its snow, so the mechanism producing the bias weakens, and the true correction should shrink. Applying the reference-period offset unchanged then over-corrects. There is no way to test for this from the reference period alone — the assumption is untestable within the data used to make it, which is the recurring shape of every problem on this page.

Question. The drift correction subtracts a trend measured in the model’s own control run, in which nothing is being forced. Why is that legitimate, when subtracting a trend generally is not? Because in a control run the forcing is constant by design, so any long-term trend is unambiguously an artefact — the model has not finished spinning up, or it does not conserve energy exactly. There is nothing physical for that trend to be. In a forced run you cannot make the same argument, because the trend is what you are trying to measure. The distinction is the whole reason piControl is a required CMIP experiment.

The points to leave with

Six sentences

  • Climate models of different complexity exist — EBMs, EMICs and GCMs — and they are complementary, not competing.
  • An EBM is built on the energy balance of the Earth and can be written on one line; it is enough to explain the greenhouse effect, and not enough to project regional rainfall.
  • A GCM describes the interactions between the components of the Earth system in three dimensions, and is limited by resolution and by the parameterisations that stand in for everything smaller than a grid box.
  • EMICs sit between the two, and exist because some questions need ten thousand years of simulation.
  • Models must be evaluated against observations of the past and present — and validation is never complete, because passing the tests you thought of is not the same as being right.
  • Several techniques exist to correct model biases, all of them resting on assumptions that cannot be tested with the data used to build them.

And the thread running through all five pages: a climate model is a piece of physics you can interrogate. Its value is not that it is right — it is not — but that it is explicit. Every assumption in it is written down somewhere and can be changed, and the consequence of changing it can be measured. That is what makes the difference between a model and an opinion.
