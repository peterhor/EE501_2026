Components, coupling and scale

The atmosphere is one component of four, and its equations are the ones you have just seen. This page adds the ocean, the sea ice and the land surface, shows how they are bolted together, and then faces the basic problem that a 100 km grid box is not a place anybody lives.

Four components and a coupler

Select a component to see what it exchanges, how fast it responds, and how much heat it can store. The last column is the one that explains most of the behaviour of the coupled system.

Component

The coupler is not a formality. Each component runs on its own grid and its own time step — an atmosphere at 20 minutes and 100 km, an ocean at 1 hour and 25 km — so the coupler has to interpolate between grids and average over time while conserving heat, water and momentum exactly. Fail at that and the model leaks energy, which after a thousand years of simulation is a drift of several degrees. Early coupled models leaked so badly that they were kept in place by an artificial flux adjustment: a fixed correction added at the interface to stop the climate sliding away. Those adjustments were quietly abandoned during the 2000s as conservation improved — and their disappearance is one of the more concrete signs that models got better.

The ocean

The same equations, three changes

Goosse puts it in one sentence: the equations for the ocean are similar to the ones for the atmosphere. Which is true, and the differences are where all the interesting behaviour comes from.

What has to be parameterised, and why it is harder than in the atmosphere. The ocean eddies that do most of the lateral heat transport are 10–100 km across — the Rossby radius is far smaller in water than in air — so a 1° ocean model does not resolve any of them. Convection is worse: deep water forms in plumes a few hundred metres wide, and models represent it by simply re-sorting the water column whenever it becomes statically unstable. Add to that the overflows across sills at Denmark Strait and Gibraltar, tidal mixing, and mixing along sloping density surfaces, and the ocean component carries at least as much parameterisation uncertainty as the atmosphere.

Sea ice

Two halves that must both be there

Goosse’s division: the physics of sea ice separates into the thermodynamic growth and decay of the ice, and the large-scale dynamics of a material that moves, cracks and piles up.

Thermodynamics — the vertical problem

  • Heat conducted up through ice and snow, growth at the base
  • Surface melt when the energy balance turns positive
  • Bottom melt from ocean heat
  • Snow insulating the ice from the cold air above it
  • Brine, and the fact that sea ice has no single melting point

Dynamics — the horizontal problem

  • Drift under wind stress and ocean currents
  • Internal stress: ice resists compression, not tension
  • Ridging and rafting under convergence
  • Leads opening under divergence — and the very large heat loss through them
  • Export through Fram Strait, which removes about 10 % of the Arctic ice each year

Why both halves matter for the answer. Thermodynamics alone would give an Arctic that thickens smoothly to an equilibrium. Dynamics turns 2 m of level ice into a landscape of 5 m ridges and open leads, and a lead in mid-winter loses heat to the atmosphere a hundred times faster than the ice beside it. A model with only the thermodynamics gets the mean thickness roughly right and the heat loss badly wrong.

Grow some sea ice

The thermodynamic half, integrated over six years. Heat is conducted up through the ice and the snow on top of it, and the ice grows at its base at whatever rate that conduction can carry the latent heat away; in summer it melts from above and below. Question. Compare Arctic today with No snow at all. Removing 20 cm of snow roughly doubles the equilibrium thickness. Yet snow is white and reflects sunlight, so removing it should warm the surface and thin the ice. Which effect wins in the real Arctic, and why is that a hard question for a model? In winter, insulation wins outright — there is no sunlight to reflect, and the snow simply keeps the ice warm and stops it growing. In summer the albedo effect wins, because the snow delays the onset of melt ponds. So the answer depends on the seasonal timing of snowfall, which is a precipitation field from the atmospheric component and one of the least reliable things a model produces at high latitudes. The competition between two well-understood processes is settled by a poorly-constrained third.

Question. Push the mean temperature up to about −7 °C. The ice stops surviving the summer. Notice how sharply that happens as you drag the slider — and remember that this model has no ice–albedo feedback in it, because the air temperature is prescribed rather than computed. What would adding that feedback do to the sharpness of the transition? Sharpen it further, and possibly make it irreversible over some range: once the ice goes, the ocean absorbs far more sunlight, which warms the air, which prevents the ice returning. This is the same structure as the vegetation collapse in PC Lab 3 and the ice line in PC Lab 2 — a threshold produced by a positive feedback, not by anything abrupt in the forcing.

The land surface

From a bucket to a biosphere

The land component has to return four things to the atmosphere at every time step: reflected shortwave, emitted longwave, sensible heat and latent heat. Everything in it exists to get those four numbers right.

The bucket is the oldest land surface model and it is still the right way to think. One store of water with a capacity set by rooting depth and soil texture; rain goes in; evaporation comes out at a rate that falls as the bucket empties; anything above capacity leaves as runoff. You have already run this equation — it is the soil water balance on the water balance page, and the water-limited versus energy-limited distinction it produces is exactly what decides whether a model’s summer over southern Europe is a heatwave or not.

What a modern land surface model adds on top. Each addition exists because the bucket got one of the four fluxes wrong somewhere:

  • Multiple soil layers with heat and water diffusion, so that the surface can dry while the root zone stays wet — and so that the soil temperature wave of Lecture 3 is represented rather than assumed.
  • A canopy with its own energy balance, intercepting rain and radiation, so that the surface the atmosphere sees is leaves rather than soil.
  • Stomatal conductance responding to light, humidity, soil water and CO2 — which is what couples the water cycle to the carbon cycle, and why rising CO2 changes evaporation even with no change in rainfall.
  • Snow with its own albedo, depth and ageing — the single largest land-surface control on high-latitude climate.
  • Vegetation dynamics and carbon pools, turning the land from a fixed map into a component that responds and feeds back. That step is what turns a GCM into an Earth system model.

Question. A bucket model and a full land surface model are given the same rainfall and the same net radiation for a European summer. Where would you expect them to disagree most? In the timing of the switch from energy-limited to water-limited evaporation, and therefore in the peak temperature during a drought. The bucket dries uniformly; a layered model with roots keeps drawing water from depth for weeks longer, which delays and softens the heatwave. Getting this wrong is a known cause of models over-predicting summer temperature extremes in mid-latitudes.

The simplest possible statistical downscaling: fit a straight line between what the model produces at the grid scale and what a station actually recorded, over a period where you have both, then apply that line to the model’s future output. It is cheap, it works well for temperature, and it can be applied to every station you have — a regional model gives you one field at 12 km, a regression gives you every valley for which somebody kept records.

And it rests on an assumption that cannot be tested. asd and bsd are fitted in today’s climate and then used in a different one. If the relationship between the large scale and the local scale changes — and it will, whenever the mechanism linking them changes, as it does when a valley stops having snow cover, or when the wind direction that brings rain shifts — then the fitted coefficients are wrong in exactly the situation you built them for. A regional climate model has no such problem, because it solves the physics rather than fitting a relationship. It just costs a hundred times more, covers one domain, and inherits every error of the model driving it.

Question. A regional model at 12 km is nested inside a GCM at 200 km. The GCM has the storm track 400 km too far south. What does the regional model do about it? Nothing. It takes the GCM’s fields at the edges of its domain as boundary conditions, so it faithfully reproduces the wrong storm track at magnificent resolution. Downscaling adds detail; it cannot add skill that the driving model did not have. This is why the evaluation on page 5 is done on the global model first.
