The model hierarchy

EBM, EMIC, GCM. These are not three attempts at the same thing, one of which is best. They are three different trades between how much physics and how many years, and every one of them is the right tool for some question and the wrong tool for most others.

The trade that defines the hierarchy

You have a fixed computer. You can spend it on detail or on duration. You cannot spend it twice.

Read the diagram along the diagonal. Every step up in complexity costs you roughly two orders of magnitude in the number of years you can simulate, or in the number of ensemble members you can run. A palaeoclimate question spanning a glacial cycle and a question about next decade’s regional rainfall are simply not answerable by the same model, and no amount of computer time changes that.

Complexity is not accuracy. A simple model that contains the one process that matters for your question will beat a complex one whose extra processes you cannot check. What complexity buys is the ability to ask more kinds of question — and the obligation to evaluate far more of the answer.

The bottom rung, built properly

From a sum to a model

The zero-dimensional energy balance is the smallest thing that deserves the name climate model. You have already done its arithmetic in Lecture 2; what makes it a model rather than a sum is the left-hand side.

Equilibrium — what Lecture 2 gave you
πR2S0(1 − αp) = 4πR2σTe4   →   Te = [ S0(1 − αp) / 4σ ]1/4 = 255 K   S0 = 1361 W m−2, αp = 0.3

The Stefan–Boltzmann page derives the right-hand side and the greenhouse-effect page explains why the surface sits 33 K above Te. Nothing on this page repeats that. What it adds is the two things that turn it into something you can run:

The model
C · dTs/dt  =  S0(1 − αp)/4  −  τa σ Ts4   [W m−2]
TermMeaningWhy it changes everything
CEffective heat capacity of the system, J m−2 K−1 Introduces time. Without it the planet responds instantly to every change; with it, the answer depends on how long you wait, and on what is thermally connected.
τaInfrared transmissivity of the atmosphere — the fraction of the surface’s emission that escapes to space Introduces the greenhouse effect as a parameter you can perturb. Adding CO2 is, in this model, exactly a small reduction in τa.

Set the left side to zero and you recover the equilibrium with a greenhouse term: Ts = [ S0(1 − αp) / (4 τa σ) ]1/4. τa = 1 gives the bare 255 K; τa ≈ 0.61 gives the observed 288 K. That one number is standing in for the entire radiative transfer scheme of a GCM.

Question. Set the perturbation to Double CO2 and read the final warming. The model gives about 1.1 K. Every GCM gives 2.5–4.5 K for the same forcing. Is one of them wrong? Neither. This model has exactly one feedback — the Planck response, the fact that a warmer surface radiates more. It has no water-vapour feedback, no ice-albedo feedback (αp is a fixed number you set by hand), no lapse-rate feedback and no clouds. Adding those roughly triples the answer. The gap between 1.1 K and 3 K is the subject of the next lecture, and it is the reason the hierarchy has more rungs than this one.

Question. Switch the heat capacity from 50 m mixed layer to Whole ocean without changing anything else. The equilibrium temperature does not move at all, but the simulation changes completely. Why, and what does that mean for a modelling centre about to start a run? Heat capacity cannot change where you end up, only how long you take to get there — it appears in dT/dt, not in the equilibrium condition. But the response time goes from about 2 years to about 150, so a run that would have spun up in a decade now needs many centuries of computer time before the answer means anything. This is why coupled models are spun up for a thousand years or more before the experiment proper begins, and why any remaining imbalance shows up as the model drift that page 5 has to correct for.

Adding dimensions

One dimension: latitude, and a transport term

Split the planet into latitude bands and the balance no longer closes locally — the tropics absorb more than they emit and the poles do the reverse, exactly as the geography of the budget showed. What closes it is transport between the bands, and in a one-dimensional EBM that transport is the whole of the atmospheric and oceanic circulation, compressed into a single coefficient.

Ci dTi/dt  =  (Si/4)(1 − αi)  −  (A + B Ti)  +  D ( T̄ − Ti )    band i

Two things arrive with that equation and neither is available in zero dimensions. First, the ice line. Make αi depend on Ti — white below freezing, dark above — and the model acquires the ice–albedo feedback, multiple stable states and a threshold beyond which it collapses into a snowball. Second, transport as a competitor. Strong transport smears the gradient and stabilises the ice line; weak transport lets it run away. The single parameter D decides which.

The upper rungs

EMICs — Earth models of intermediate complexity

Goosse’s defining property: an EMIC always includes a representation of the Earth’s geography. It knows where the continents are. What it gives up is resolution and, usually, a full three-dimensional atmosphere.

Typically simplified

  • Atmosphere reduced to a statistical-dynamical or quasi-geostrophic form
  • Coarse resolution, often 5–10°
  • Weather not simulated, only its statistics
  • Fewer vertical levels, simpler clouds

Usually kept, and often better than in a GCM

  • Full ocean circulation and sea ice
  • Interactive carbon cycle and vegetation
  • Ice sheets on their own long timescale
  • Thousands of years of integration, and large ensembles

What they are for. Questions where the answer takes millennia to emerge or where you need hundreds of runs: glacial cycles, the last deglaciation, the stability of the Atlantic overturning circulation, long-term commitment to sea-level rise, and systematic exploration of parameter uncertainty. A GCM cannot be asked those questions at all. LOVECLIM and CLIMBER are the standard examples.

GCMs and Earth system models

The most precise and complex description of the climate system: three-dimensional, resolving the general circulation, with the components of page 4 coupled to one another and exchanging fluxes every few hours.

An AOGCM couples atmosphere, ocean, sea ice and land surface, and is driven by prescribed greenhouse gas concentrations. An ESM adds the biogeochemistry — the carbon cycle, dynamic vegetation, atmospheric chemistry, sometimes ice sheets — and can therefore be driven by emissions instead, computing the concentration itself. That distinction is exactly the boundary condition versus prognostic variable line from page 1, and it decides which questions the model may be asked.

Each new row is a new way to be wrong, and a new question you can ask. Coupling the carbon cycle in the early 2000s immediately produced a result no fixed-concentration model could have given: the land and ocean sinks weaken as the climate warms, so a given emissions pathway leads to a higher concentration than the offline calculation assumed. That is a feedback, and it was invisible until the component existed.

Which model for which question

The practical form of “the types are complementary”. Read it as a checklist before choosing a tool, and as a warning when you read someone else’s result.

QuestionRight toolWhy the others fail
Why is the Earth 33 K warmer than its emission temperature?0-D EBM Anything larger obscures the answer. One equation is the explanation.
Can a planet have two stable climates for one solar constant?1-D EBM Needs a latitudinal ice line; 0-D has no geography to put one on.
What happened to the ocean circulation over the last glacial cycle?EMIC 100 000 years of integration. A GCM would take decades of wall-clock time.
How much will summer rainfall change over southern Norway by 2070?GCM + RCM Needs resolved orography and storm tracks. An EMIC has neither.
How much CO2 can we emit and stay under 2 °C?ESM The carbon cycle must respond to the climate, not be prescribed.
How large is the uncertainty from convection parameters?EMIC or EBM ensemble Needs hundreds to thousands of runs. Affordable only low on the ladder.

Question. A newspaper reports that “a simple model shows the Gulf Stream could collapse” and dismisses it as too crude to believe. What would you need to know before agreeing? Whether the collapse mechanism — the salinity–density feedback on the overturning circulation — is actually represented in the model, and whether the simplifications made are ones that affect it. A simple model containing the mechanism is far better evidence than a complex model run for too few years to show it. Complexity is not the criterion; relevance is.
