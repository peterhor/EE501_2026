The governing equations

Seven equations, seven unknowns. This is the physics that page 1 discretised onto a grid — the exact half of a climate model, before any parameterisation. Open each one in turn: what it conserves, what every term does, and what happens to the numbers when you change the situation.

Why exactly seven

A system of coupled differential equations has a unique solution only when the number of independent equations equals the number of unknowns. Six and the model is under-determined; eight and they contradict each other. The count is the first thing to understand and the easiest to check.

None of these are climate equations. They are conservation laws that would be as true in a bathtub: momentum, mass, water, energy, plus one algebraic closure. Climate is what emerges when you solve them on a rotating sphere that is heated unevenly — which is why the same seven equations serve a three-day forecast and a three-century projection.

      Where these seven go next

      These are the equations for the atmosphere. The ocean solves an almost identical set — salinity in place of humidity, and a far nastier equation of state — and the sea ice and land surface solve their own. Page 4 puts all four together and shows what the coupler has to move between them.

      Everything smaller than the grid box these equations are solved on never appears in them at all, and has to be parameterised instead. That is page 1, and it is where the uncertainty lives. Whether the whole apparatus produces anything trustworthy is page 5.

Saturation vapour pressure from Bolton’s approximation; the moist adiabatic lapse rate from the standard saturated expression; the standard atmosphere follows the ICAO profile. The condensation level uses the 125 m per kelvin of dew-point depression rule of thumb, which is accurate to a few per cent for well-mixed boundary layers and no better.
