Reference values and the model

  • As provided: vegetation feedback coefficient DB = 150 mm/yr, rainfall thresholds PC1 = 270 and PC2 = 370 mm/yr, vegetation timescale τ = 5 yr, background rainfall at 6500 yr BP Pd0 = 300 mm/yr (version 1) or 150 mm/yr (versions 2 and 3), and maximum stochastic forcing (the largest random rainfall anomaly) Stoch = 0 (version 1) or 300 mm/yr (versions 2 and 3). The comparison runs of E2 to E4 raise Pd0; each exercise says by how much. Selecting an exercise in the lab restores the settings that exercise needs.

The whole lab rests on one small model, in five equations stepped one year at a time from 6500 to 3500 yr BP (the lab's Recap shows the same, with the terms coloured to match its loop diagram):

Pd(t) = Pd0 · (1 − (t + 6500)/3000)(1)

The orbital forcing. Pd(t) is the background rainfall in year t, the rain the region would get with no vegetation; t is the model year in years relative to the present, from −6500 to −3500; Pd0 is the background rainfall at the start. It declines in a straight line to zero at 3500 yr BP. For the astronomical cycles behind that decline, see the video Milankovitch cycles. The size of the change: summer (June to August) insolation at 20°N is 451 W m⁻² today; 10 to 11 thousand years ago, when perihelion fell in northern summer, it was about 8 percent higher, and the humid period began at 14.8 ka and ended at 5.5 ka at the same value, 470 W m⁻², 4 percent above today (deMenocal et al. 2000). Pd(t) stands for the rainfall response to that decline.

PE(V, t) = Pd(t) + DB · V(2)

The feedback. PE is the equilibrium rainfall, the rain the region receives on average at a vegetation cover V (a fraction from 0, desert, to 1, full grass cover); DB is the vegetation feedback coefficient, the rainfall a full cover adds, 150 mm/yr.

P(t) = max [ PE(t − 1) + PN(t), 0 ](3)

The rainfall of one year is last year's equilibrium rainfall plus this year's random anomaly PN, drawn uniformly between 0 and the maximum stochastic forcing Stoch (version 2), replaced by its 10-year running mean (version 3), or zero (version 1). Because the anomaly has mean Stoch/2, versions 2 and 3 halve Pd0 so that the long-term mean rainfall is the same in all three.

VE(P) = 0 for P < PC1;  (P − PC1)/DC for PC1 ≤ P ≤ PC2;  1 for P > PC2(4)

The cover the rainfall can sustain. VE is the equilibrium cover a rainfall P sustains in the long run; PC1 and PC2 are the lower and upper rainfall thresholds, 270 and 370 mm/yr, and DC = PC2 − PC1 = 100 mm/yr the width of the ramp between them. Two stable states exist only when DB > DC (Liu et al. 2006).

V(t) = V(t − 1) + [ VE(t − 1) − V(t − 1) ] / τ(5)

The vegetation follows with a lag: each year the cover V closes a fraction 1/τ of the gap to its equilibrium cover VE; τ is the e-folding time of the vegetation, 5 yr. The run starts at V = 1 and P = Pd0 + DB.

Three versions. Version 1 has no random anomaly (Stoch = 0, Pd0 = 300 mm/yr) and shows what the feedback does on its own. Version 2 adds an anomaly drawn afresh every year (Stoch = 300, Pd0 = 150 mm/yr): fast, year-to-year variability. Version 3 uses the 10-year running mean of the same draws: slow, decadal variability. All three share one sequence of random draws and the same DB, thresholds and τ.

IntroThe green Sahara

Between roughly 10 000 and 5 500 years ago the Sahara was a grassland with lakes, rivers, hippos and people. The evidence is in the ground and on the sea floor: lake levels far above today's across North Africa (Kohfeld and Harrison 2000), remains of hippos and crocodiles in what is now hyper-arid desert (Drake et al. 2010), human settlement of the Sahara between about 10.5 and 5.5 thousand years ago (Kuper and Kröpelin 2006), and, off the West African coast, a flux of desert dust that jumps within a few centuries at about 5.5 thousand years ago, when the grassland went (deMenocal et al. 2000). That abrupt end to a slow change is the observation this lab's model exists to explain.

The rain came from the West African monsoon. In summer the land heats faster than the Atlantic, low pressure over the Sahara draws moist air in from the Gulf of Guinea, and the rain belt moves north; in winter the flow reverses and the region is dry. More summer insolation strengthens that land–sea contrast and pushes the rain further north, so the monsoon follows the orbital forcing: more Northern Hemisphere summer insolation in the early Holocene, declining slowly over the following millennia (equation (1) above).

The conceptual model of Liu et al. (2006) has two variables, annual rainfall P and vegetation cover V, and one feedback between them. Three versions differ only in how the year-to-year rainfall variability is treated.
