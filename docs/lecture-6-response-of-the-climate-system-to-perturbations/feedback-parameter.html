The feedback parameter

One equation collapses every feedback in the climate system into a single number, and that number turns a forcing in W m−2 into a temperature in kelvin. This page builds it out of five terms and shows why the last one ruins everything.

The linearisation, and what it buys

The honest answer to “how much will it warm?” requires a full model. This is the approximation that lets you reason about the answer without one.

Apply a forcing ΔQ. The system starts to warm. As it does, dozens of things change — humidity, cloud, ice cover, lapse rate — and each of them alters the radiative fluxes at the tropopause. The bet we make is that the net effect of all of them can be written as proportional to the global mean surface temperature change and nothing else:

ΔR  =  ΔQ  +  λf ΔTs
ΔR = imbalance in the radiative budget [W m−2], downward positive  ·  λf = climate feedback parameter [W m−2 K−1]
ΔR imbalance left in the radiative budget, W m−2, downward positive
ΔQ radiative forcing applied to the system, W m−2
λf climate feedback parameter — the net radiative response per degree, W m−2 K−1 (negative for a stable climate)
ΔTs change in global-mean surface temperature, K
  • ΔQ is what you did to the system — the whole of page 2 in one number.
  • λfΔTs is what the system does back. All of it, every feedback, condensed into one slope.
  • ΔR is what is left over — the energy currently accumulating in the system. It is not a bookkeeping term: it is measurable, it is about 0.9 W m−2 today, and almost all of it is going into the ocean.

Set ΔR = 0 and you have equilibrium, which is the whole point of writing it this way:

ΔTs = − ΔQ / λf    at equilibrium, where the accumulation has stopped

The quantity (−1/λf) is the climate sensitivity: kelvin per watt per square metre. Everything else on this page is an argument about its value.

Why λf must be negative

Not a convention, and not an assumption — a condition for the planet to have a climate at all.

Read it as a restoring force. Each line starts at ΔR = ΔQ when the temperature has not yet moved, and the system warms as long as ΔR > 0. If λf is negative the line slopes down, crosses zero, and the warming stops there. The steeper the slope, the sooner it crosses, and the smaller the warming.

If λf were positive, the line would slope up and away: warming would increase the imbalance, which would increase the warming. Nothing would stop it until some process outside this linearisation intervened. That is a runaway, it is what happened to Venus, and the fact that Earth's temperature has stayed within a few tens of kelvin for four billion years is a strong empirical claim that λf < 0 across a wide range of states.

The sign trap. λf is negative overall, but individual feedback terms λi can be either sign. A positive λi makes λf less negative, which makes −1/λf larger, which means more warming: an amplifying feedback. A negative λi damps. So “positive feedback” and “positive λi” agree with each other — but both push λf in the direction of instability, which is where the sign confusion comes from.

Building λf out of parts

The decomposition

λf is a total derivative. Split it by the variable each process acts through, and it becomes a sum you can estimate term by term.

λf = ∂R/∂Ts = ∑i (∂R/∂xi)(∂xi/∂Ts)  =  λ0 + λL + λw + λc + λα
R net radiative flux at the tropopause, W m−2
Ts global-mean surface temperature, K
xi the i-th climate variable a feedback acts through — humidity, lapse rate, cloud, albedo
λ0 Planck (blackbody) feedback, W m−2 K−1
λL lapse-rate feedback, W m−2 K−1
λw water-vapour feedback, W m−2 K−1
λc cloud feedback, W m−2 K−1
λα surface-albedo feedback, W m−2 K−1

Each term is a product of two things you can think about separately: how much does variable x change per kelvin of warming, and how much does the radiation budget care about x. Second-order terms — feedbacks on feedbacks — are neglected, which is fine for a few kelvin and progressively less fine beyond that.

What is deliberately left out. This decomposition covers the processes that act on the tropopause budget through physics on a timescale of years to decades. Changes in ocean and atmospheric circulation are not in it. Neither are the carbon-cycle and vegetation feedbacks of page 6, nor ice sheets, which are far too slow. When you read a number for “climate sensitivity”, check which of these it includes — the answer changes by a factor of two between the narrow and broad definitions.

λ0: the one you can derive on the back of an envelope

The blackbody or Planck response — the fact that a warmer planet radiates more. It is the only feedback in the list that needs no modelling at all.

Start from the top-of-atmosphere balance you already know from Lecture 2, differentiate it with respect to temperature, and assume the whole troposphere warms uniformly so that ΔTs = ΔTe:

ΔR = (1 − α) S0/4 − σTe4   →   λ0 = ∂R/∂T = −4σTe3
with Te = 255 K: λ0 = −3.8 W m−2 K−1. Detailed radiative calculations give −3.2.
α planetary albedo, held fixed here (dimensionless)
S0 solar constant, 1361 W m−2
σ Stefan–Boltzmann constant, 5.67×10−8 W m−2 K−4
Te effective emission temperature, 255 K

The difference between −3.8 and −3.2 is not an error. The envelope calculation treats the planet as a single blackbody at one temperature; the real one emits from a range of altitudes and temperatures, some of it through the atmospheric window straight from the surface, and the geographic pattern of warming is not uniform. Models get −3.2 and that is the number used from here on.

If λ0 were the only feedback: ΔTs,0 = −ΔQ/λ0 = 3.7 / 3.2 = 1.16 K for a CO2 doubling
ΔTs,0 the reference (Planck-only) warming, K

This is the 1.1 K from Lecture 5. The zero-dimensional model on that page had exactly one feedback — this one — because αp and τa were fixed numbers you set by hand. Every kelvin of the difference between 1.16 and the 3 K that models give is contributed by the four remaining terms.

Question. Start from Planck only and add water vapour. The warming goes from 1.16 K to about 2.6 K — an increase of 1.5 K from one feedback worth +1.8 W m−2 K−1. Now, from the full five-term estimate, add another +0.4 to the cloud term. The warming jumps by nearly a kelvin, from an addition less than a quarter the size. Why is the second addition worth so much more? Because ΔT = −ΔQ/λf is a hyperbola, not a line. Equal steps in λf produce increasingly large steps in ΔT as λf approaches zero. The consequence, visible on the right-hand plot, is that a symmetric uncertainty in λf maps into a strongly asymmetric uncertainty in the warming. This is why every assessed sensitivity range has a long tail on the high side, and it is not a statistical artefact — it is the shape of the function.

Question. Load A glacial climate. The albedo feedback more than doubles and the sensitivity rises. Does that mean the “true” climate sensitivity of the Earth is larger than we think? No — it means there is no single true value. Feedback strengths depend on the mean state they operate in. A world with ice sheets down to 45°N has far more ice to lose per kelvin than one with ice only in Greenland, so λα is larger. This is one of the four caveats below, and it is the reason palaeoclimate estimates of sensitivity cannot simply be transplanted onto the next century.

The feedback factor, and the reference every feedback is measured against

An equivalent way of writing the same thing, and the one that makes the amplification explicit.

ΔTs = 1 1 + (λL+λw+λc+λα)/λ0  ·  ΔTs,0  =  ff · ΔTs,0
ff climate feedback factor — the amplification of the reference warming (dimensionless)
λL, λw, λc, λα lapse-rate, water-vapour, cloud and surface-albedo feedbacks, W m−2 K−1

ff is the climate feedback factor: the factor by which the real system's response exceeds the bare blackbody response. With the standard values it is about 2.5 — feedbacks roughly two and a half times the warming that physics alone would give. If ff > 1 the feedbacks amplify; if 0 < ff < 1 they damp.

TermλiSpread What it is, and where it is explained
λ0 Planck −3.2small A warmer surface radiates more. Derived above; the only one that needs no model.
λw water vapour +1.8±0.1† Clausius–Clapeyron at roughly constant relative humidity. Page 4.
λL lapse rate −0.8±0.1† The upper tropical troposphere warms more than the surface, so the planet radiates more efficiently. Page 4.
λc cloud +0.68±0.4 Changes in cloud amount, height and brightness. The dominant uncertainty, by a wide margin.
λα surface albedo +0.26±0.08 Retreating snow and ice darken the surface. Small globally, about half the response at high latitudes.
λf total−1.26 ±0.42 Giving 2.9 K for a doubling, with a range of roughly 2.2–4.4 K.

† The dagger matters. ±0.1 is the spread on the sum λw + λL, not on either one separately. The two are strongly anti-correlated: a model that warms the upper troposphere strongly gets a large negative lapse-rate feedback and a large positive water-vapour feedback, because it is putting extra moisture exactly where the atmosphere is driest and most sensitive to it. The errors cancel. This is the single most useful thing to know about feedback uncertainty, and it is why the pair is nearly always quoted together.

The limits of the framework

Four things this one number cannot do

Goosse states them plainly, and they are worth taking seriously before you quote a sensitivity at anyone.

 LimitationConsequence
1No spatial information ΔTs is a global mean. It says nothing about where the warming falls, how rainfall redistributes, or what happens to storms. Ecology happens at the local scale, and a global mean will not get you there.
2Forcings are not equally efficient The same ΔQ from black carbon and from CO2 does not produce the same ΔTs, because a forcing concentrated at high latitudes or in one hemisphere engages different feedbacks. Sensitivity is defined against CO2 for a reason.
3Feedbacks depend on the mean state λα is much larger in a glacial world with ice to lose, and much smaller in a world with none. Sensitivity is not a constant of the planet, and it drifts as the climate changes.
4Thresholds break the linearisation ΔR = ΔQ + λfΔTs assumes a smooth proportional response. Cross a threshold — a collapsing ice line, a monsoon that fails, a circulation that stops — and the change is set by the system's own dynamics rather than by the size of the forcing. That is what PC Labs 2 and 3 are about.

None of that makes the framework useless. It makes it a first term. It tells you the right order of magnitude, it tells you which feedbacks are worth arguing about, and it lets a two-line calculation be checked against a model that took a million core-hours. That is a lot for one equation.

After Goosse et al., Introduction to Climate Dynamics and Climate Modelling, ch. 4.1.2 and 4.1.3 (Eqs. 4.4–4.13, Fig. 4.8). Feedback values are those of Soden and Held (2006) as quoted by Goosse: λ0 = −3.2, λw = +1.8, λL = −0.8, λc = +0.68 ± 0.4, λα = +0.26 ± 0.08 W m−2 K−1, and the ±0.1 spread on the combined water-vapour-plus-lapse-rate term is the AR4 inter-model standard deviation. The total ±0.42 is those spreads added in quadrature, which assumes the cloud and albedo terms are independent of the others — a convenient assumption rather than a demonstrated one. Summing the best estimates gives λf = −1.26 W m−2 K−1 and 2.9 K for a 3.7 W m−2 doubling, which sits in the middle of the assessed 1.5–4.5 K range without having been tuned to. The glacial preset raises λα to +0.6 and is illustrative of state dependence, not a published estimate.
