The transient response

Everything so far has been about equilibrium — where the system ends up. Nobody lives at equilibrium. This page adds the one term that turns an answer into a trajectory, and explains why forty years of warming still does not tell you the climate sensitivity.

Adding time

The same energy balance as page 3, with the left side no longer set to zero.

At equilibrium the imbalance ΔR is zero because the system has finished adjusting. Before then it is not zero — and where does that energy go? Into heating something. Represent everything thermally connected as a single slab of heat capacity Cs, and the imbalance becomes a rate of change:

Cs dTs/dt  =  ΔQ  +  λf ΔTs
Cs in J K−1 m−2; for a slab of water of depth h, Cs = ρcwh with ρcw = 4.18×106 J m−3 K−1

For a forcing switched on at t = 0 and held constant, this has an exact solution — and it is worth seeing written out, because every term in it says something:

ΔTs(t) = −(ΔQ/λf) · (1 − e−t/τ)   with   τ = −Cs/λf
PieceWhat it says
−ΔQ/λf The equilibrium answer, unchanged. Heat capacity does not alter where you end up.
(1 − e−t/τ) The fraction of it realised so far. 63 % at t = τ, 86 % at 2τ, 95 % at 3τ.
τ = −Cs/λf The bit that catches people out. The response time depends on the sensitivity. A more sensitive climate — smaller |λf| — is not just warmer at the end, it is also slower to get there. Both effects work in the same direction, and together they are why the early years of a warming look so alike.
initial slope At t = 0, ΔTs = 0, so dTs/dt = ΔQ/Cs. The feedback parameter has dropped out entirely. The first years of warming carry no information about climate sensitivity at all.

Question. With the default settings the two curves are almost on top of each other for the first fifteen years, and one of them is heading for twice the warming of the other. What is the practical consequence? That watching the early response of the climate system does not identify the sensitivity. The initial slope is ΔQ/Cs, which contains no λf at all. This is the honest technical reason why several decades of excellent observations have not collapsed the 1.5–4.5 K range, and it should be your answer whenever someone says the uncertainty must be carelessness.

Question. Load Volcanic eruption. The forcing peaks at −3 W m−2, comparable in size to a CO2 doubling, yet the cooling is only a few tenths of a kelvin. Where did the response go? Nowhere — it never had time to develop. The forcing is gone in about three years and τ is fifteen or more, so the system realises only a small fraction of the equilibrium response before the push is withdrawn. Set the slab depth to 10 m and the cooling becomes much larger and sharper: a shallow system responds quickly to a brief forcing. This is the same argument as the eleven-year solar cycle on page 2, and it is why volcanoes make the temperature record noisy without changing its trend.

Question. Load Ramp, then hold: the forcing rises and then stays constant, which is what happens if concentrations stop rising. The temperature keeps climbing for decades afterwards. What is that warming, and can it be avoided? It is committed warming — the part of the equilibrium response already forced but not yet realised, currently in transit into the ocean. It cannot be avoided by emitting less, because it has already been paid for; only by reducing the forcing itself. The radiative imbalance in the lower panel is the measurable signature of it, and it is about 0.9 W m−2 in the real world today, almost all of it going into the ocean.

Two numbers, not one

Equilibrium climate sensitivity and transient climate response

Two definitions that get confused constantly, and mean quite different things.

Which one you want For the eventual state: sea level, ice sheets, the long commitment. For this century: it is roughly what you get by 2100 under a rising forcing.

TCR is always smaller than ECS, and the gap is set entirely by ocean heat uptake. Run the demonstration with 1 %/yr to 2× and compare the warming at year 70 with the equilibrium value: the ratio is typically 0.5–0.7. That missing half is not gone. It is in the pipeline, and the ocean is holding it.

Why one slab is not enough

The single-Cs model gets the shape right and the tail wrong, in a way that matters for anything beyond a century.

  • The ocean is not one slab. A mixed layer of 50–100 m responds in years. Below it, water is exchanged with the deep ocean over centuries. So the real response has at least two timescales: a fast one that gets you most of the way in a few decades, and a slow one that takes centuries to close the rest.
  • The consequence. Fit a single exponential to the first fifty years of a warming and you will underestimate the eventual equilibrium, because you have measured the fast timescale and assumed it is the only one. Estimates of ECS from the historical record are biased low for exactly this reason.
  • And λf itself drifts. Feedbacks depend on where the warming is, not just how much of it there is. In the first decades the warming is concentrated where the ocean mixes least — and that pattern engages different cloud feedbacks from the eventual, more uniform one. The effective λf inferred from the historical record is therefore not the λf that will govern the equilibrium. This is called the pattern effect, and it is one of the more recent additions to this subject.
Which does not make the slab model useless. It gives the right initial slope, the right equilibrium, the right qualitative dependence of response time on sensitivity, and the correct explanation of why volcanoes and solar cycles produce so little response. That is a good return on one ordinary differential equation, and it is exactly the argument for the model hierarchy in Lecture 5: use the simplest model that contains the process you are asking about.

Put those together and the arithmetic of page 3 checks out: ΔR = ΔQ + λfΔTs gives 0.9 ≈ 2.7 + λf × 1.2, so λf ≈ −1.5 W m−2 K−1, and an equilibrium warming of about 1.9 K for the forcing already applied. That is a climate sensitivity estimated from three measurements and one linear equation — and it lands inside the assessed range.

Then read the caveats and mistrust it slightly. The forcing has the aerosol uncertainty of page 2 in it, and the pattern effect above says the λf you infer this way is probably too negative. Both corrections push the answer upward. The calculation is worth doing precisely because you can see every assumption in it.
