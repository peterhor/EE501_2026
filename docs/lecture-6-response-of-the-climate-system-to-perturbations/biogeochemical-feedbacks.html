Biogeochemical and biogeophysical feedbacks

The feedbacks of page 4 all acted through radiation, in years. These act through the carbon cycle and through living things, over decades to millions of years — and for an ecologist they are the ones that involve you.

Two words that sound the same and are not

The distinction is about which pathway the vegetation acts through.

Biogeochemical — the biosphere changes the composition of the atmosphere.

  • Forests take up CO2; warming soils release it; thawing permafrost releases both CO2 and CH4.
  • The climate effect runs through a greenhouse forcing, so it is global whatever the latitude of the vegetation that caused it.

Biogeophysical — the biosphere changes the physical properties of the surface.

  • Albedo, roughness, rooting depth, how much water is transpired — all the surface parameters from Lecture 3.
  • The effect is felt most strongly where it happens, and it can have the opposite sign to the biogeochemical effect on the same patch of ground.

Some of these feedbacks operate with no climate change at all. The ocean and the land biosphere absorb carbon simply because the atmospheric concentration has risen, whether or not the planet has warmed. Separating that response from the response to warming is what the next demonstration is about, and it is the single most consequential distinction in this whole area.

Carbon and climate

Splitting the carbon response in two

Everything emitted goes somewhere. Bookkeeping first, then the two ways the sinks respond.

CE = ΔCA + ΔCO + ΔCL
emitted = stays in the air + taken up by the ocean + stored on land, all in PgC

This is the budget from Lecture 4 and nothing on this page changes it. What is new is asking why the sinks take what they take, and there are exactly two reasons:

ΔCL = βL ΔCA + γL ΔT      ΔCO = βO ΔCA + γO ΔT
ParameterNameMechanismSign
βconcentration–carbon Higher CO2 drives uptake directly: more dissolves into the sea, and plants photosynthesise faster — the CO2 fertilisation effect. This works without any warming. positive β
= damping
γclimate–carbon Warming reduces uptake: warm water holds less CO2 and stratifies, and warm soil respires faster. Both sinks weaken as the planet heats. negative γ
= amplifying

The sign convention is a trap worth pausing on. A positive β means more carbon is removed from the air, which damps the perturbation. A negative γ means less carbon is removed as it warms, which amplifies it. So a positive number describes a negative feedback and a negative number a positive one, which is exactly the sort of thing that gets misread in an exam. Read the mechanism, not the sign.

Question. Press No climate–carbon feedback, note the warming, then go back to the best estimate. The extra warming that appears came from no extra emission at all. Where did it come from? From carbon that would have been absorbed and was not. Warming makes soil respire faster and makes seawater hold less CO2, so a larger share of the same emission stays airborne, raising the concentration, raising the warming. It is a loop identical in structure to the water vapour feedback on page 4 — but it operates through the carbon cycle rather than through radiation, which is why it is not in λf and why “climate sensitivity” quoted for a fixed CO2 concentration systematically understates the response to an emission.

Question. Drag γL towards −200 PgC K−1. The airborne fraction climbs sharply and then the whole thing becomes very sensitive to small changes. What is happening, and what would it look like in the real world? The loop gain is approaching one. Beyond a certain point the land does not merely absorb less — it becomes a net source, and the system runs away within the limits of this simple model. Some models did produce a dieback of the Amazon with roughly this behaviour in the 2000s. It is not the consensus expectation, but the spread in γL across models is genuinely a factor of four, and the tail of that distribution is where this lives.

Feedbacks with a biosphere in them

Permafrost and methane

The largest single carbon store that is not currently exchanging with the atmosphere, sitting in the region that is warming fastest.

  • Permafrost soils hold roughly 1500 PgC — about twice what is in the atmosphere now, frozen and biologically unavailable.
  • Thaw makes it available to microbes. In dry, aerobic conditions the product is CO2; in waterlogged, anaerobic conditions it is CH4, which is a far stronger absorber per molecule but lasts about a decade.
  • Which of the two you get depends on drainage, and therefore on vegetation, topography and thaw rate — which is why permafrost carbon release is an ecological question, not just a thermal one.
  • The loop is positive and it is effectively irreversible on human timescales: carbon that took ten thousand years to freeze in does not refreeze if the temperature comes back down.

The methane budget itself — sources, sinks, the ten-year lifetime, the hydrate reservoir — is on the land, rock and methane page from Lecture 4, and is not repeated here. What is new on this page is only that a warming climate moves the source terms.

Tundra and taiga: the same warming, two opposite effects

The cleanest available example of biogeochemical and biogeophysical pulling in different directions on the same hectare.

The biogeophysical effect. Warming lets trees establish on what was tundra. A snow-covered forest is much darker than snow-covered tundra — the canopy sticks up through the snow and the dark needles dominate the view from space. Albedo falls from perhaps 0.75 to 0.25, more absorbed sunlight, more warming, more trees. Positive, regional, and strongest in spring, when there is both snow and strong sun.

The biogeochemical effect. The same trees are also accumulating carbon, drawing CO2 out of the atmosphere. Negative, and global.

Which wins depends on latitude, and at high latitudes the albedo effect generally does — boreal afforestation can warm the planet. The surface properties page has the albedo values behind this, and the fact that they matter so much here is a good argument for why that page exists.

This is where the course's two halves meet. Everything in this lecture up to now has been a global mean. A treeline advancing across the Finnmarksvidda is a change in the local surface energy balance, driven by ecology, feeding back on the climate that drove it. The case studies at the start of the semester — Arctic vegetation, alpine ecosystems — are describing one arm of this loop.

Vegetation and the monsoon: the Sahara

A biogeophysical feedback strong enough to have produced an abrupt, essentially irreversible change in a major region — and one you are about to simulate.

Between about 9 and 6 thousand years ago the Sahara was vegetated, with lakes and large mammals. The orbital forcing that supported it — stronger Northern Hemisphere summer insolation, and so a stronger African monsoon — declined slowly and smoothly over millennia. The vegetation did not. It collapsed over a few centuries.

  • Less vegetation → higher albedo → less absorbed solar → weaker ascent and a weaker monsoon → less rain → less vegetation. Two minus signs: a positive loop.
  • The loop is strong enough to support two stable states under the same insolation, so the system can sit in either and will not cross between them until the forcing passes a threshold.
  • Once it crosses, the change is set by the feedback, not by the forcing — which is the fourth caveat from page 3, made concrete.

PC Lab 3 is this feedback, in a model you can push yourself. Do the lab knowing what you are looking for: a slowly changing forcing, a response that refuses to be slow, and a state you cannot get back to by reversing the forcing.

The slow stabilisers

Carbonate compensation

The ocean's own thermostat on alkalinity, and the reason atmospheric CO2 has never wandered very far over tens of millions of years. Double the input from rivers and watch the sea floor deal with it.

The chemistry is on the oceanic carbon cycle page and is not repeated here. Three facts from it are all you need:

[CO32−] ≈ Alk − DIC   ·   KCaCO3 = [CO32−]sat[Ca2+]sat
carbonate falls with depth, solubility rises with pressure — so somewhere in between is the saturation horizon
Set by continental weathering. Doubling it is Goosse's thought experiment.

Question. The new steady state has burial exactly matching the new river input, and it gets there by moving the saturation horizon. Why does that constitute a negative feedback? Because the perturbation triggers a change that removes the perturbation. More river input raises alkalinity, which raises [CO32−], which deepens the saturation horizon, which exposes more sea floor to supersaturated water, which preserves more CaCO3 in the sediment, which takes the extra alkalinity back out. The loop closes on itself with an odd number of inversions. It is slow — the adjustment takes of the order of ten thousand years, because that is how long it takes to turn over the ocean's carbonate inventory — and it caps the resulting CO2 change at something like 30 ppm rather than several hundred.

Weathering, tectonics and the very long run

Two hypotheses about the same set of arrows, one stabilising and one not, and both still argued about.

The thermostat — negative. Uplift exposes fresh silicate rock; weathering consumes CO2; CO2 falls; the climate cools and dries; cooler, drier conditions weather more slowly. The perturbation is moderated. This is the standard explanation for why Earth has had liquid water for four billion years while the Sun brightened by 30 %.

The erosion hypothesis — positive. If weathering is limited not by temperature but by the supply of fresh rock, then cooling helps: glaciers grind rock and expose new surfaces, weathering rises, CO2 falls further, and it cools more. Same variables, different rate-limiting step, opposite sign.

Which is right is not settled, and Goosse says so. It is a good example of a feedback whose sign depends on which process you believe is rate-limiting — and therefore of why the loop diagrams from page 1 are an argument about physics on each link, not a piece of notation. Both operate over hundreds of thousands to millions of years, so neither is any use whatsoever on the timescale of the rest of this course. Their relevance is that they set the background state everything else happens against.

Everything, on one axis

The feedbacks of this lecture, by timescale

Sixteen loops, six orders of magnitude of time, and one useful way to hold them all at once.

Read the diagram vertically, at the timescale of your question. Ask about next summer's heatwave and only the top few bars are engaged. Ask about 2100 and you have everything down to the ocean circulation. Ask about the fate of the Greenland ice sheet and you need the millennial band, where several of the loops are irreversible. Nothing here is a single number called “climate sensitivity”; what there is, is a different effective sensitivity for every timescale you might care about, and it increases as you go down the page.

Read it horizontally and you get the other lesson. Red dominates. Most of the loops in the climate system amplify, and the reason the planet is nonetheless stable is that one negative feedback — the Planck response, the plain fact that hot things radiate — is larger than all the amplifiers put together. Climate stability rests on σT4, and on very little else.

What to take out of the lecture

  • A forcing comes from outside the system; a feedback is the system's own response acting back on itself. Which is which depends on where you draw the boundary, and you have to say.
  • Radiative forcing makes unlike perturbations comparable. The anthropogenic total since 1750 is about +2.3 W m−2, made of roughly +3.3 of greenhouse gases and about −1 of aerosols.
  • ΔR = ΔQ + λfΔTs. At equilibrium ΔTs = −ΔQ/λf, and λf must be negative for the planet to have a climate.
  • λ0 = −3.2 alone gives 1.16 K per doubling. Adding water vapour, lapse rate, cloud and albedo gives about −1.26 and 2.9 K. That is where the gap between Lecture 5's 1.1 K and the models' 3 K went.
  • Because ΔT = −ΔQ/λf is a hyperbola, symmetric uncertainty in λf gives a lopsided range in warming. Cloud supplies most of that uncertainty; water vapour and lapse rate largely cancel each other's.
  • Heat capacity does not change the destination, only the journey. The initial slope contains no sensitivity information at all, which is why decades of observation have not settled the question.
  • The carbon cycle and the biosphere add loops that the radiative framework does not contain, and almost all of them amplify. Several are irreversible.
