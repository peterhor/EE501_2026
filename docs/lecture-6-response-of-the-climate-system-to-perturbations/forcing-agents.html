Question. Switch on CO2 alone: 1.82 W m−2. Now switch on everything anthropogenic: about 2.3. But the greenhouse gases alone come to roughly 3.3. Where did a whole watt per square metre go? Aerosols, almost all of it. They have been cancelling something like a third of the greenhouse forcing for a century, and nobody chose that — it is a side-effect of burning dirty fuel. It also means the warming we have seen is the response to a forcing substantially smaller than the greenhouse forcing, which is why cleaning up air pollution, an unambiguously good thing to do, unmasks warming that was already paid for.

Question. Look at the uncertainty bars. Which single term dominates the uncertainty on the total, and why does that matter? Aerosol–cloud interactions, spanning roughly −1.2 to 0. It matters because that term is subtracted from a well-known greenhouse forcing to give the net — so the uncertainty on what has been applied to the climate is nearly as large as the uncertainty on how the climate responds. A large aerosol cooling implies a sensitive climate; a small one implies an insensitive one, and the observed warming is consistent with both.

Agent by agent

Greenhouse gases — the well-behaved case

Long-lived, well mixed, and computable from a formula.

CO2, CH4, N2O and the halocarbons stay in the atmosphere for decades to centuries, which is long enough for circulation to mix them almost uniformly. Hemispheric differences are a per cent or two. That homogeneity is exactly why a single global-mean number describes them well, and why the formulas on page 1 work at all. Everything else on this page is worse behaved.

Ozone is the exception that proves the rule. It lives for weeks, so its concentration is highest near where it is produced. Tropospheric ozone, formed photochemically downwind of traffic and industry, contributes about +0.40 W m−2. Stratospheric ozone has declined since 1750 because of chlorofluorocarbons, giving about −0.05 — small in the global mean and very large over Antarctica in spring. Two ozone changes, opposite signs, different altitudes, different chemistry, the same molecule.

Aerosols — the hard case

Small solid or liquid particles suspended in the air. They live for days, so they never mix globally, and most of their effect is not on radiation directly but on clouds.

The net is negative, but not everywhere and not for every species. Sulphate cools; black carbon warms, and by enough that it is the second largest positive anthropogenic forcing agent after CO2 on some estimates. Because both are concentrated downwind of industrial regions — Europe and North America in the twentieth century, South and East Asia now — the forcing map is nothing like uniform, and a global mean hides a lot.

Optical depth, in one line. Aerosol amount is usually reported as aerosol optical depth, a measure of how much of a beam survives the passage through the atmosphere: zero means perfectly transparent, and the transmitted fraction is e−τ. Typical clean background values are 0.05; heavily polluted air or a fresh volcanic cloud reaches 0.2–0.3.

Question. Aerosols have a lifetime of about a week. Greenhouse gases last for a century or more. What follows for policy from that one fact? That the cooling is only rented. Stop emitting sulphate today and the atmosphere is clean in a fortnight, exposing the full greenhouse forcing that has accumulated underneath it. Stop emitting CO2 today and its forcing is still there in a century. The two levers act on completely different clocks — and the fast one is the one pointing the wrong way.

Land use: from a local albedo change to a global forcing

Clearing forest for cropland changes the surface albedo of a patch of ground. Turning that into a number comparable with CO2 takes three factors, and doing it by hand once makes the published value stop being arbitrary.

ΔQ  =  − Δαs  ×  S̄surf  ×  (Aconverted / AEarth)  ×  τup

Question. Land clearance also released carbon — roughly 180 PgC since 1750, most of the cumulative land-use emission in Lecture 4's budget. So does deforestation warm the planet or cool it? Both, and which wins depends on latitude. In the boreal zone the albedo effect dominates — removing dark conifers that mask snow is a real cooling, and afforestation there can warm. In the tropics there is no snow, albedo change is small, and the carbon release plus lost evapotranspiration dominates, so clearance warms strongly. This is one of the cleanest examples in the course of a global mean concealing two opposite regional signals, and it is why “plant trees” is a latitude-dependent recommendation.

The natural forcings

Solar variability and volcanic eruptions

The two things that perturbed climate before anyone was burning anything, and the yardstick against which the anthropogenic forcing has to be judged.

The Sun. Total solar irradiance has been measured from satellites since 1978 and varies by about 1 W m−2 out of 1361 over the eleven-year sunspot cycle — a tenth of a per cent.

ΔQsolar = ΔS0 (1 − αp) / 4 ≈ 0.175 · ΔS0
ΔQsolar radiative forcing from a change in solar output, W m−2
ΔS0 change in total solar irradiance, W m−2 (about 1 out of 1361 over the sunspot cycle)
αp planetary albedo, 0.3 (dimensionless)
/ 4 the disc-to-sphere geometry factor: a disc intercepts, a sphere radiates
  • The division by four is the geometry from Lecture 2: a disc intercepts, a sphere radiates.
  • So a 1 W m−2 cycle in irradiance is only 0.18 W m−2 of forcing.
  • The estimated secular change since 1750 is about +0.05 W m−2 — smaller than the cycle, and a fortieth of the anthropogenic total.

Volcanoes. Only explosive eruptions matter, and only because they inject sulphur into the stratosphere.

  • Sulphur dioxide converts to sulphate droplets that scatter sunlight.
  • In the troposphere rain removes them within a week. Above the tropopause there is no rain, so they persist for one to three years and spread across a hemisphere.
  • Peak forcing after Pinatubo (1991) reached roughly −3 W m−2, briefly larger in magnitude than the entire anthropogenic forcing of the time.
  • And yet it cooled the planet by only about 0.4 K, for about two years. The reason is on page 5.

Read the two curves against each other. The volcanic spikes are individually the largest forcings on the plot — Krakatau and Pinatubo both reach about −3 W m−2, more than the whole anthropogenic forcing of their day. But each is gone within three years and its time integral is small, so the climate barely has time to respond before the forcing withdraws. The anthropogenic curve is never dramatic in any single year and never goes away. Turn on Sum and the point becomes obvious: volcanoes make the record bumpy, and the trend is not theirs.

Question. The eleven-year solar cycle produces a forcing of about ±0.09 W m−2 — comfortably larger than the whole secular solar trend since 1750. Why can you barely see an eleven-year cycle in the global temperature record? Because the ocean is a low-pass filter. A forcing that reverses every five and a half years does not have time to warm a 50 m mixed layer, let alone anything below it — the response is roughly 0.05 K, at the edge of detectability. The same physics explains why Pinatubo cooled the planet by far less than its instantaneous forcing implies, and it is exactly the heat-capacity argument from Lecture 5. Slow forcings get their full response; fast ones do not. Page 5 makes this quantitative.

What page 3 does with all this

You now have a number, ΔQ. It is not yet a temperature.

The net anthropogenic forcing of +2.3 W m−2 is about 0.6 of a CO2 doubling. If the planet responded like a bare blackbody, that would give about 0.7 K of warming; observed warming is already above 1 K and equilibrium warming for that forcing is something like 1.8 K. Turning ΔQ into ΔT requires exactly one more number — the feedback parameter — and that is the next page.
