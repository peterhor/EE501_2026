The physical feedbacks

Five numbers went into λf on the last page. This page is where they come from: the mechanism behind each, why the two largest partly cancel, and why the one that will not sit still is cloud.

Water vapour — the big amplifier

Clausius–Clapeyron does the work

λw = +1.8 W m−2 K−1. On its own it would make the planet 2.3 times as sensitive as a blackbody.

Two facts, and the feedback follows from them.

  • Saturation vapour pressure rises quasi-exponentially with temperature. About 7 % per kelvin near room temperature. That is the Clausius–Clapeyron relation, and it is thermodynamics, not climatology.
  • Relative humidity stays roughly constant. This one is an observation, supported by models rather than derived. It means the actual vapour content tracks the saturation value, so the atmosphere really does gain 7 % more water per kelvin.
  • Water vapour is the strongest greenhouse absorber in the atmosphere. More of it, less outgoing longwave, more warming.
Where the extra vapour matters is not where there is most of it. The radiative effect of water vapour goes roughly as the logarithm of its concentration, so adding vapour where it is already abundant — the tropical boundary layer — does very little. Adding it where the air is dry — the upper troposphere — does a great deal. This is exactly the saturation argument that made CO2 forcing logarithmic on page 1, and it is the reason the upper troposphere gets so much attention.
column water vapour ≈ W0 · 1.07ΔT    +7 % per K → +30 % for 4 K of warming

The observable consequence beyond temperature: a wetter atmosphere carries more latent heat and delivers it in heavier bursts. Global mean precipitation is limited by the energy budget and rises only about 2 % per kelvin, while the moisture available to any individual storm rises at 7. The arithmetic of those two numbers is the reason extreme rainfall intensifies faster than mean rainfall, and it is a direct consequence of this feedback. The water balance page has the budget it has to fit inside.

Question. The Arctic warms two to three times as fast as the global mean, and almost all of that warming is at the surface. That gives a positive lapse-rate feedback there. So why is the global λL negative? Area and radiative leverage. The tropics are half the planet's surface and emit far more longwave than the poles do, so the tropical top-heavy profile dominates the global mean. The Arctic contribution is real, it is positive, and it is one of the reasons polar amplification is as strong as it is — but it loses the global average. This is the same lesson as the geography of the budget in Lecture 2: a global mean is an area-weighted statement about very unequal places.

Question. Why is the uncertainty on λw + λL together (±0.1) so much smaller than on either alone? Because they are driven by the same unknown. A model that warms the upper troposphere strongly gets a strongly negative λL — and, in the very same air, a strongly positive λw, because it has just added moisture to the driest and most radiatively sensitive layer in the atmosphere. Whatever the vertical structure of warming turns out to be, the two errors have opposite signs and largely cancel. Cloud has no such partner, which is why it dominates the total uncertainty.

The anticorrelation, seen across models

Each dot is a model. Individually they scatter widely; their sum does not.

This is why feedbacks are quoted in the pair. Reporting λw = +1.8 ± 0.4 and λL = −0.8 ± 0.4 and adding the uncertainties in quadrature would give ±0.57 on the sum — nearly six times too large, because it assumes an independence that does not exist. Whenever you see quoted uncertainties added, check whether the terms are actually independent. Usually somebody has assumed it.

Cloud — the one that will not settle

What a cloud does to the budget, and what happens when it moves

Every cloud does two opposite things: it reflects sunlight (cooling) and it emits longwave from a cold top instead of a warm surface (warming). Which wins depends almost entirely on how high it is.

Question. Set the height to 13 km and the albedo to 0.60 — a deep convective tower. The two effects nearly cancel. Is that a coincidence? Not entirely. A deep tower is both very bright and very cold, so it maximises both effects at once, and in the tropics they land within a few W m−2 of each other. It is close enough to have been noticed and argued about for forty years, and it means the net effect of tropical deep convection on the budget is a small difference between two large numbers — which is exactly the arithmetic that makes a quantity hard to compute.

Question. Now the feedback, which is about change, not about the present state. Suppose warming raises cloud tops by 300 m while leaving their temperature the same, and dissolves 1 % of the marine stratocumulus. Both of those are positive feedbacks. Why? Raising a high cloud top makes it colder relative to the surface it shades, so it emits less while the surface below emits more: the longwave trapping strengthens. That is the fixed anvil temperature effect, and models agree on it. Losing low cloud removes reflection with almost no compensating longwave change, since a low cloud top is nearly as warm as the surface: the shortwave cooling weakens. Both raise λc. Models agree on the sign; they disagree on how much low cloud is lost, and that disagreement is the ±0.4 that dominates the whole sensitivity uncertainty.

Ice, soil and ocean

Cryospheric feedbacks

λα = +0.26 W m−2 K−1 globally, and roughly half the response at high latitudes.

Snow–ice albedo Ice and snow have albedos of 0.5–0.9 against 0.1 for open ocean. Melt them and the surface absorbs several times as much sunlight. Strongest over land in spring, when snow disappears quickly and the sun is already high — the seasonality matters as much as the magnitude. seasons to decades
Sea-ice insulation Sea ice has a very low thermal conductivity and separates a −30 °C atmosphere from a −1.8 °C ocean. Thin the ice and the winter heat flux from ocean to air rises sharply. Not a radiative feedback at all, but it explains why Arctic warming is largest in winter, when the albedo feedback is switched off for lack of sunlight. seasons
Ice-sheet growth Snow that survives one summer accumulates. Once an ice sheet exists it raises the planetary albedo, and its own height keeps its surface cold enough not to melt. Both effects lock in the cold that started it. millennia

This is the feedback the labs are built around. In PC Lab 2 the ice line in a latitudinal energy balance model gives multiple stable states and a threshold past which the planet freezes over and will not unfreeze when you turn the sun back up. In PC Lab 1 the albedo is biological rather than cryospheric, but the loop is the same shape and it stabilises rather than destabilises. Two labs, one feedback structure, opposite signs — which is the point.

Polar amplification is not one feedback. The albedo feedback is part of it, but so are the positive lapse-rate feedback at high latitudes, changes in poleward heat transport, and the fact that the same absolute change in outgoing longwave corresponds to a larger temperature change where it is cold, because σT4 is flatter there. Attributing Arctic amplification to “ice–albedo” alone is the most common oversimplification in this part of the course.

Soil moisture

A feedback that operates through the surface energy balance rather than the top of the atmosphere — which is exactly why it is not in the λf decomposition, and exactly why it matters for ecology.

The mechanism is the Bowen ratio from Lecture 3. Available energy at the surface must leave as either latent or sensible heat. Wet soil evaporates, spending the energy on latent heat and keeping the surface cool. Dry the soil out and the same net radiation has nowhere to go but sensible heat: the surface and the air above it heat up.

  • The loop. A dry spell reduces soil moisture → less evaporation → more sensible heat → hotter, drier air → higher evaporative demand and often less convective rainfall → drier soil. Positive, regional, and fast.
  • Where it bites. Transitional zones between wet and dry climates — the Mediterranean, the Sahel, the central Great Plains, and increasingly central Europe. In permanently wet or permanently dry regions there is nothing to switch.
  • Why an ecologist should care. This is the feedback that turns a warm summer into a heatwave. It acts on the timescale of weeks, in the growing season, at the scale a plant actually experiences — none of which a global-mean sensitivity captures.

The advective feedback in the ocean

The one feedback here that can produce an abrupt change rather than a proportional one.

The North Atlantic overturning is driven by dense water sinking at high latitudes, and density there is controlled by salinity as much as by temperature. The circulation that sinks also imports salty water from the south. So the circulation sustains the very density contrast that drives it.

  • Add freshwater — melting ice, more rainfall, more river runoff — and the surface water gets lighter and sinks less readily.
  • Weaker sinking means weaker northward transport of salty water, so the surface freshens further.
  • The loop is positive, and unlike the others on this page it has more than one stable state: a vigorous overturning and a collapsed one, with a threshold between them.

Why this breaks the framework of page 3. ΔR = ΔQ + λfΔTs assumes the response is proportional to the forcing. Here a small extra freshwater input can produce either nothing at all or a wholesale reorganisation, depending on which side of the threshold you were on. The size of the change is set by the system, not by the size of the push — which is precisely the fourth caveat from the last page, and the structure you will meet again in PC Lab 3.

The five terms, back together

Where each one comes from, in one place.

Read the whiskers, not the bars. The bars say what we think the feedbacks are. The whiskers say how much of that we can defend. On that reading the ordering changes completely: water vapour is the largest feedback and one of the best constrained; cloud is a third the size and carries four times the uncertainty. Effort spent on cloud is worth more than effort spent on anything else here, and that has been true in every IPCC assessment since 1990.
