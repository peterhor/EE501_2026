Orbital forcing and the glacial cycles

The clearest case of forced variability in the whole record, and the one that refuses to be simple. The forcing is known exactly — it is celestial mechanics, computable to arbitrary precision. The response is enormous. And the two do not resemble each other. Working out why is most of what palaeoclimate has done for a century.

Sixty-five million years of cooling first

The glacial cycles are a feature of the last two million years. They need a cold planet to happen on, and the planet had to become one.

  • Greenhouse to icehouse. Deep-sea oxygen-isotope records show a broadly declining temperature through the Cenozoic, from a world with no permanent ice at either pole to one with ice at both. The Eocene, 50 Myr ago, had continents in nearly modern positions but a climate with crocodiles in the Arctic.
  • What changed. Slow tectonic drivers: the opening of Southern Ocean gateways that let the Antarctic Circumpolar Current isolate Antarctica, the uplift of the Himalaya and the enhanced silicate weathering that goes with it, and the long decline in atmospheric CO2 that these imply. Those are the mechanisms at the far right of the timescale diagram on page 1, and the weathering thermostat of Lecture 6, page 6.
  • Two thresholds. Antarctic glaciation begins near 34 Myr, Northern Hemisphere glaciation only near 2.7 Myr. Both are threshold crossings, not gradual transitions — a hint of the non-linearity that will dominate the rest of this page.
  • Then the wobble becomes visible. Once there are ice sheets to grow and shrink, the small orbital changes that had always been there acquire a large amplifier. The orbital forcing did not switch on 2.7 Myr ago. The response did.
Restating page 1’s point about boundaries. Over 65 Myr, continental positions and CO2 are forcings. Over 100 kyr they are, respectively, a fixed boundary condition and a feedback. Nothing about the physics changed — only the question.

The forcing, exactly

Three numbers describe the orbit

And they are the only three that matter for insolation.

ParameterRangePeriodsWhat it changes
Eccentricity e0 – 0.058 ~100 and 405 kyr The shape of the orbit. Directly it changes the annual mean insolation by at most 0.17 %, about 0.5 W m−2 — negligible. Its real job is to set how strong the precession effect can be.
Obliquity ε22.0° – 24.5° 41 kyr The tilt of the axis. Changes the seasonal contrast, symmetrically in both hemispheres, and most strongly at high latitudes. Higher tilt → warmer summers and colder winters at the poles, and more annual-mean energy delivered polewards of the Arctic circle.
Climatic precession e sin ω̄ ±0.05819 and 23 kyr Where in the orbit each season falls. Changes seasonal contrast antisymmetrically between the hemispheres: when northern summer occurs at perihelion, southern summer occurs at aphelion. Its amplitude is multiplied by e, which is why the two cannot be separated.

There is no approximation in that expression, and the demonstration below evaluates it directly. Everything you are about to see follows from those three parameters and nothing else — no physics, no feedbacks, no model. This is the forcing, and it is known.

Do these four things, in this order.
  • Press Obliquity 22.0°, then Use the current setting as reference, then Obliquity 24.5°. The field you are now looking at is the pure obliquity effect: same sign in both hemispheres’ summers, growing towards the poles, near zero at the equator, and it changes the annual mean at high latitude.
  • Press Back to today, then Perihelion at NH winter solstice, set that as reference, then Perihelion at NH summer solstice. That is the pure precession effect: opposite signs in the two hemispheres, opposite signs in the two seasons, and almost no change in the annual mean anywhere.
  • Now set eccentricity to zero and repeat the precession experiment. Nothing happens. Precession without eccentricity is not a forcing at all — a circular orbit has no perihelion.
  • Finally press 127 ka and look at 65°N in June: about 546 W m−2 against today’s 478. That extra 68 W m−2 of summer sunshine is what ended the previous ice age.
Compare the scale with Lecture 6. A doubling of CO2 is 3.7 W m−2 globally and annually averaged. The orbital changes here are ten to twenty times larger locally and seasonally and essentially zero in the global annual mean. They are not a bigger version of the same forcing — they are a completely different kind of forcing, one that redistributes rather than adds. That is why they need an amplifier to produce a global signal, and why the amplifier is the interesting part.

The response, which does not look like the forcing

What the ice cores show

The shape of the last 800,000 years, and the three things about it that need explaining.

  • Long glacials, short interglacials. Roughly 90 kyr of slow cooling and ice growth, then a few thousand years of rapid collapse. A sawtooth, strongly asymmetric in time.
  • A dominant 100-kyr rhythm for the last 800 kyr — but before about 1 Myr ago the same records cycle at 41 kyr instead, with smaller amplitude. Something changed, and it was not the orbit.
  • CO2 moves with temperature, between about 180 ppm in full glacials and 280 ppm in interglacials — a range of 90 ppm, worth about 2 W m−2 of radiative forcing. Check that on Lecture 6’s calculator: it is more than half a CO2 doubling.
  • The Last Glacial Maximum, 21 kyr ago, was about 4 K colder than pre-industrial in the global mean, with the cooling strongly amplified over the northern ice sheets and almost absent in the tropical oceans.
The arithmetic that motivates the next demonstration. Take the 21 ka world: about −2.2 W m−2 from CO2, another −1 W m−2 or so from the other greenhouse gases and dust, and something like −3 W m−2 from the ice sheets’ own albedo. That is roughly −6 W m−2 for 4 K — which, run backwards through Lecture 6, page 3, is a perfectly ordinary climate sensitivity. The glacial world is not a different physics. It is the same physics with the ice and the carbon moved into the feedback column.

From orbit to ice: where the 100-kyr cycle comes from

Drive a deliberately crude ice model with the insolation you just computed, and watch a rhythm appear in the output that is not in the input.

The model is the simplest one that does the job, after Imbrie and Imbrie (1980): ice volume relaxes towards whatever the current summer insolation implies, but it melts faster than it grows. That single asymmetry is the whole mechanism.

The spectra — this is the punchline

The same two series, decomposed by period. Read the input on the left, the output on the right.

The experiment to run. Set the asymmetry b to zero. The ice volume becomes a smoothed copy of the insolation: 23 and 41 kyr in, 23 and 41 kyr out, no sawtooth, no 100-kyr peak. Now raise b towards 0.6 and watch two things happen at once — the time series develops its sawtooth shape, and a peak grows in the spectrum at a period that is not present in the forcing. A non-linear system does not merely amplify its input frequencies; it creates new ones. That is the single most important thing on this page.
And now the caveat, which is equally important. This model is too easy to believe. It produces a 100-kyr rhythm, but it does so by responding to the eccentricity envelope of precession, and the real eccentricity signal is far too weak in energy terms — that 0.5 W m−2 from the table above — to be the direct driver. This is the 100-kyr problem, and it is not solved by an asymmetry alone. Current explanations require ice-sheet physics that this model has none of: basal sliding, isostatic depression of the bedrock, dust deposition on the ice, and above all the CO2 drawdown that makes the glacial state cold enough to persist through weak insolation maxima. Models that include those, driven by orbital parameters and the observed greenhouse gases, do reproduce the observed ice volume — but they need the carbon cycle to do it.

The 90 ppm question

The single largest unresolved bookkeeping problem in palaeoclimate, and a good test of Lecture 4.

Where did 90 ppm of atmospheric CO2 go during each glaciation, and where did it come back from? The atmosphere holds too little carbon for the land to be the answer — and in any case the land lost carbon in the glacial, as forests contracted. It has to be the ocean, which holds fifty times as much. Several mechanisms contribute, no single one suffices, and they do not simply add.

MechanismDirectionWhy
Colder ocean− CO2 is more soluble in cold water. Straightforward, and worth perhaps a third of the signal.
Saltier ocean+ Water locked up in ice sheets raises salinity, which lowers solubility. Works against the above.
More efficient biological pump− Iron-bearing dust was far more abundant in the glacial atmosphere, fertilising the Southern Ocean and exporting more carbon to depth.
Southern Ocean stratification and sea-ice cover− Capping the region where deep, carbon-rich water outcrops stops it venting to the atmosphere.
Carbonate compensation− The slow sediment response amplifies whatever the faster mechanisms started. Timescale of several thousand years — see Lecture 6, page 6.
Reduced land biosphere+ Less vegetation and soil carbon on a colder, drier, partly ice-covered land surface. Also works against the observed change.

Why this belongs on this page rather than in Lecture 4. Because it converts CO2 from a forcing into a feedback. Nothing external changed the CO2; the climate changed it, and the change then fed back on the climate. Half of the glacial–interglacial temperature range is attributable to greenhouse gases in this way, and the orbital forcing is the trigger, not the energy source. The carbonate chemistry that decides how the ocean responds is Lecture 4, page 3.

The uncomfortable corollary. If the carbon cycle amplified orbital forcing in the past, it will amplify anthropogenic forcing in the future — the β and γ feedbacks of Lecture 6, page 6 are the same physics running in the other direction. The glacial cycles are the best evidence we have that those feedbacks are real and large.

What the astronomical theory does and does not claim

Worth stating precisely, because it is routinely overstated in both directions.

ClaimStatus
Orbital parameters vary with known periods Certain. Celestial mechanics, computed to high accuracy back tens of millions of years.
Those periods appear in palaeoclimate records Established, in many independent archives: ice cores, marine sediments, loess, speleothems.
Northern summer insolation paces deglaciations Strongly supported. Deglaciations begin at insolation maxima, and the timing is what dates them.
Insolation change is energetically sufficient False. The global annual mean forcing is essentially zero. Amplification by ice albedo and by the carbon cycle is required, and is most of the signal.
The response is a linear function of insolation False. Thresholds, asymmetry and the 100-kyr power that is absent from the forcing all rule it out.
Orbital forcing explains the last 800 kyr but not the earlier 41-kyr world An open problem. The orbit did not change at 1 Myr; the ice sheets, or the CO2 baseline, or the sub-glacial regolith did.

The transferable lesson. A forcing that is small in the global mean can still organise an enormous response, provided the system has an amplifier and a threshold. Keep that in mind for page 4, where the forcing gets smaller still and the response gets faster.

The 800-kyr demonstration is not exact and is labelled as idealised in the figure. Its orbital elements are a deliberately truncated synthesis — obliquity as a single 41-kyr sinusoid of range 22.25–24.25° phased to peak 9 kyr ago, perihelion precessing uniformly with a 22-kyr period from its present position, and eccentricity as the sum of a 405-kyr and a 95-kyr term spanning 0.004–0.054 — not the full Berger (1978) solution, which contains dozens of terms. It reproduces the amplitude, the periods and the character of the real variation but not the exact phasing of individual cycles, and no date read off it should be taken as a real date. The insolation is then computed from those elements with the same exact integral. The ice model is that of Imbrie and Imbrie (1980), with their asymmetric relaxation time; the default τ0 = 17 kyr and b = 0.6 are close to the values they used. It is a heuristic, not a physical ice-sheet model, and the caveat box above says what it omits; simulations that do reproduce the observed ice volume, such as Ganopolski and Calov (2011), require ice-sheet dynamics and prescribed greenhouse gases.
