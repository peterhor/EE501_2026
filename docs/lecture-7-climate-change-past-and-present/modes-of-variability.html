Modes of internal variability

Page 1 said the system wanders on its own. It does not wander at random. Left alone it keeps returning to a small number of recognisable patterns, each with a preferred timescale, because those are the patterns that can feed themselves. Five of them account for most of what the instrumental record does that no forcing asked for.

What a mode of variability is

A definition worth being fussy about, because the word suggests more than it delivers.

A mode of variability is a recurrent, large-scale spatial pattern whose amplitude varies in time. It is described by two things and only two: a pattern (a map of how much of the anomaly appears where) and an index (one number per month or year, saying how strongly the pattern is expressed and with what sign).

anomaly field(x, y, t)  ≈  index(t) × pattern(x, y)  +  everything else
the whole content of “the NAO was strongly positive in the winter of 1989”
anomaly field the departure of a field (pressure, temperature) from its climatology at each point and time
x, y the two horizontal space coordinates — longitude and latitude
t time
index(t) one number per month or year saying how strongly the pattern is expressed, and with what sign
pattern(x, y) the fixed map of how much of the anomaly appears where, normalised
everything else the residual the single mode does not explain — usually most of the variance
  • Why patterns exist at all. The system has geography — ocean basins of a certain width, mountain ranges in certain places, a rotating sphere. Only some spatial arrangements of wind and temperature reinforce themselves against that geography. Those persist and recur; everything else decays.
  • Why the word “oscillation” is a trap. Most of these are not oscillators with a period. They are broadband: a preferred timescale, an irregular occurrence. Calling something an oscillation and then hunting for its period is one of the standard ways to go wrong here.
  • Correlation map or regression map? Both are used, and they answer different questions. A correlation map shows where the relationship is reliable, regardless of size. A regression map shows how many degrees or hectopascals you get per unit of index — it has units, and it is the one to use if you want a magnitude.
Why this matters for a lecture about climate change. Everything on this page is the noise of page 1 — given a name, a mechanism and a magnitude. You cannot decide whether a 30-year trend is forced until you know what a 30-year trend from these can look like.

Watch the order of causation, because there isn’t one. Weak trades → flatter thermocline → warm east → weaker east–west temperature contrast → weaker trades. Every arrow in that chain is a consequence of the one before it, and the last one closes on the first. There is no first link. That is what “proximate cause only” meant on page 1, and this is the clearest example of it in the whole climate system.

The Bjerknes feedback, counted the same way as in Lecture 6

The loop that makes the tropical Pacific unstable, with the sign rule you already have.

Count the minus signs. Warmer east Pacific → smaller east–west SST contrast (one minus) → weaker trades (a plus: weaker contrast, weaker winds) → less upwelling and a flatter thermocline (one minus) → warmer east Pacific. Two minus signs. Even number, so the loop is positive — self-amplifying. It is exactly the rule from Lecture 6, page 1, applied to a regional circulation instead of the global energy balance.

So why doesn’t the Pacific simply lock into El Niño forever? Because the loop is fast and the ocean has a slow memory. The warm anomaly is built by draining warm water from the western Pacific into the east; while the event runs, the equatorial heat content is being discharged. Ocean waves crossing the basin bring that deficit back some months later and flip the sign. A fast positive feedback plus a slow delayed negative one is the standard recipe for an oscillator — and it is why ENSO has a preferred timescale of three to seven years rather than a period.

The reach of it. ENSO is a regional phenomenon with a nearly global signature. The displaced tropical convection changes where heat is released into the atmosphere, which changes the large-scale wave pattern, which changes weather far outside the tropics. It is also the single largest contributor to interannual variability of the global mean temperature — a strong El Niño adds of order 0.1–0.2 K to the following year’s global mean, which is most of the σ = 0.11 K you set on page 1.

Named events, which are not synthetic

The strong events of the instrumental era. Worth knowing, because they show up in every global temperature curve you will ever be shown.

Use this against the global temperature curve. 1998 and 2016 both stand out as unusually warm years in the instrumental record, and both sit on top of a very strong El Niño. Choosing 1998 as the start of a trend calculation and 2012 as its end is a well-worn way of producing a small trend from a record that has a large one — the arithmetic is correct, and the conclusion is wrong, because the calculation has one mode of internal variability at each end pointing in opposite directions. Are they really internal?

The complication, and it matters for the last two.

ENSO, the NAO and the SAM are unambiguously internal: they appear, with the right pattern and the right timescale, in control simulations with every forcing held constant. The PDO and the AMO are harder. Their timescales are 20–80 years, which is also the timescale of volcanic clustering, of the solar cycle envelope, and of twentieth-century sulphate aerosol. There is active argument about how much of the observed AMO is an internal ocean mode and how much is the North Atlantic responding to aerosol forcing.

  • Why it is hard to settle. There are perhaps two independent AMO cycles in the entire instrumental record. That is not a sample.
  • Why it matters. If part of the multidecadal North Atlantic variation is forced, then using “the AMO” as a null hypothesis for natural variability double-counts the forcing, and makes the forced signal look smaller than it is.
  • How it gets resolved. Long control runs, which give many synthetic cycles, and the palaeoclimate record, which gives many real ones. This is exactly the argument for page 5.
Hold this thought. “Internal” and “forced” are not always cleanly separable in a record of finite length. That is not a failure of the definition — it is a statement about how much information a 170-year record contains.
