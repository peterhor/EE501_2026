The Holocene and the last 2000 years

No ice sheets left to collapse, no meltwater, no Dansgaard–Oeschger events. The amplitudes drop from ten kelvin to a few tenths, and the problem changes completely: it is no longer “what caused that?” but “was that anything at all?” This is the period that tells you how large natural variability is in a world like ours — which is exactly the number page 6 needs.

The Holocene starts with a strong orbital signal and spends 10,000 years losing it

The one forcing that unambiguously changes across the Holocene, and it is the one you can compute.

The current interglacial began with northern summer insolation near its maximum: perihelion fell in the northern summer around 11 ka, and obliquity was near its peak at 9 ka. Both have been declining since. Go back to page 3 and press 6 ka: summer insolation at 60°N is some 25 W m−2 above today’s, and winter insolation is below it. The Holocene forcing is not a warming or a cooling — it is a seasonal-contrast forcing in the Northern Hemisphere that has been steadily weakening.

  • Holocene Thermal Optimum, 9–6 ka. Northern Hemisphere summer temperatures, recorded by pollen, chironomids and treelines, were warmer than today — by a degree or two in much of northern Europe, more in the Arctic. Treelines in Fennoscandia stood higher and further north than they do now.
  • Stronger northern monsoons. More summer insolation over the northern continents means a stronger land–sea heating contrast, which means a stronger monsoon. West Africa, India and China were all wetter in the early and mid-Holocene.
  • A green Sahara. The most dramatic case: lakes, rivers, grassland and human settlement across a region that is now hyper-arid. Then, within a few centuries around 5–6 ka, it ended.
  • Neoglacial cooling. As summer insolation declined, northern summers cooled, glaciers advanced, and treelines retreated. The late Holocene is a slow cooling trend in Northern Hemisphere summer — which is what makes the last 150 years so anomalous.
The Sahara is a link, not a digression. A gradual, monotonic decline in insolation produced an abrupt collapse of vegetation and rainfall. That is page 4 again — a smooth forcing, a threshold, an abrupt response — with vegetation and precipitation as the feedback pair instead of salinity and overturning. It is also PC Lab 3, which you run this afternoon, and the biogeophysical feedback of Lecture 6, page 6. Same mathematics, third appearance.

Why a “thermal optimum” is not a warm world

The same orbit, viewed through different proxies. This is the resolution of a long-running argument, and it takes ninety seconds to see.

Proxies are not thermometers. A pollen assemblage or a treeline records the conditions of the growing season at one latitude. A model produces an annual, global mean. If the forcing is seasonal and hemispheric, those two quantities need not even have the same sign — and for the mid-Holocene they do not. Set the season and latitude your proxy is sensitive to and watch the “optimum” appear and disappear.

Try all five in order. A northern summer proxy shows a large, early optimum. A northern annual proxy shows almost nothing. The global annual mean shows a change of hundredths of a watt — there is no global Holocene optimum in the forcing at all. And a northern winter proxy shows the optimum with the opposite sign. All four proxies are correct. The disagreement was never about the climate; it was about what the word “warmer” refers to.
Why this is worth twenty minutes of a lecture. It is the cleanest available demonstration that a reconstruction and a model can disagree without either being wrong. It also generalises: whenever you are shown a palaeoclimate comparison, ask what season and what area each side is reporting before you ask which one is right.

The last two thousand years

What the reconstructions show

Small amplitudes, real structure, and a great deal of argument about both.

  • Medieval Climate Anomaly, roughly 950–1250. Relatively mild conditions in the hemispheric mean — but the word anomaly replaced the older warm period deliberately, because the pattern is not uniformly warm.
  • Little Ice Age, roughly 1450–1850. Cold conditions, glacier advance in the Alps and Scandinavia, with the coldest intervals varying from region to region.
  • Amplitude. Hemispheric-mean differences between these intervals are of order a few tenths of a degree — smaller than most people expect, and comparable to the internal variability magnitudes of page 1.
  • The main result. The last thirty years were likely the warmest thirty-year period of at least the last 1400 years in the Northern Hemisphere. The word likely is doing real work: it is a calibrated statement about a reconstruction with quantified uncertainty, not a rhetorical hedge.
  • Regional asynchrony. This is the finding that matters most for the rest of the lecture. Continental-scale reconstructions share some features, but their warm and cold intervals are not synchronous. There is no single century in which every region was at its warmest.
Why asynchrony is the important word. A forced signal is, by construction, in phase everywhere — the forcing acts on the whole planet at once. Internal variability is not. So the degree of synchrony between regions is itself a measurement of how much of a signal is forced. The Medieval Climate Anomaly is asynchronous; the twentieth-century warming is not. Hold that thought for page 6, where it becomes a formal method.

The four-step experiment. Do it in this order and the whole page lands.
  • Volcanic and solar only, no internal variability. You get a cool 1450–1850 and a mild Medieval interval, of a few tenths of a degree — the right sign and roughly the right size. Natural forcing explains the pre-industrial structure.
  • Now add internal variability, hemispheric mean. The forced signal is still visible, but any individual member has cold decades that the forcing did not put there.
  • Now switch to one region. The noise triples. Individual members now have their own Little Ice Ages in the wrong centuries. This is why regional records are asynchronous, and it is a simulation of exactly that result.
  • Finally add the anthropogenic forcing. The twentieth century separates from everything that came before, in every member, in every region. The natural-forcing-only runs cannot produce it at all. That is the entire argument of page 6, made in one click.
What this is. A zero-dimensional energy balance model with prescribed forcing, not a climate model: no ocean circulation, no geography, no dynamical response, and its “regions” differ only in noise amplitude. It reproduces the magnitude and timing of the forced signal because those follow from the forcing and the sensitivity, both of which are given to it. It cannot tell you anything about spatial patterns, and the regional runs are a caricature of what a general circulation model would produce. The model hierarchy of Lecture 5 explains what you would need to do better.

Reading the ensemble the way a scientist does

Three habits that follow directly from the demonstration above.

HabitWhy
Compare the ensemble mean with the reconstruction, not one member The mean estimates the forced response; a member is the forced response plus one realisation of noise that has no reason to match the real one.
Compare the ensemble spread with the reconstruction’s wiggles This is the actual test: is the observed variability inside the range the model produces? If yes, no extra explanation is required for the wiggles.
Never match an individual event by date unless it is forced A volcanic cooling should match by date, because the eruption is in both. A cold decade in the 1690s need not.

Where the ensemble came from. Running the same model many times from slightly different initial states is the practical answer to the chaos result of page 1. It converts an unpredictable trajectory into a predictable distribution, which is the only thing that was ever on offer.

A number worth remembering. Over the last millennium, the natural forcings move the hemispheric mean by a few tenths of a kelvin over centuries. The twentieth century moves it by about a kelvin in a hundred years, and the twenty-first is projected to move it by several. Whatever the last millennium is, it is not a precedent.

Its volcanic forcing is a series of exponentially decaying pulses at the dates of the largest eruptions of the period — 1258, 1453, 1600, 1641, 1695, 1783, 1809, 1815, 1835, 1883, 1902, 1963, 1982 and 1991 — with peak forcings between −2 and −9 W m−2 scaled to the relative size of each event and a 1.5-year decay; these are representative magnitudes of the kind used in last-millennium simulations, not a published reconstruction, and the dates rather than the amplitudes are the reliable part. Solar forcing is a smooth series with minima at the Maunder (1645–1715) and Dalton (1790–1830) intervals and a total range of about 0.2 W m−2, consistent with current assessments of solar variability over this period, which are much smaller than earlier estimates. Anthropogenic forcing rises from zero in 1750 to +2.7 W m−2 today, matching the budget of Lecture 6, page 2.
