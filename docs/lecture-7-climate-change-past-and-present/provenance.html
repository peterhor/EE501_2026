Appendix: what is data and what is not

Every figure and every demonstration in the eight lectures, and where its numbers come from. Some of what you see on these pages is an exact calculation, some is a model you are running live, some is a drawing that carries a shape but no measurements — and it matters a great deal which is which. This page says, for each one.

Why this page exists

It is not a disclaimer. It is part of the teaching.

These pages are built by hand and load no external data. That is a deliberate constraint, not an oversight: a self-contained HTML page cannot fetch the EPICA ice core or HadCRUT. Where a page does plot measurements — the Mauna Loa record, the US Standard Atmosphere, Oke’s site budgets — they are a short table of published values typed into the page and cited at its foot, not the full record. Faced with that constraint, there are three honest options and one dishonest one. The dishonest one — drawing a curve that looks like a measurement and letting you assume it is — is not used anywhere in these lectures.

Computed
Evaluated live in your browser from an equation or a published model, with parameters given. Reproducible, and correct to the precision of the inputs.
Schematic
Drawn by hand to carry a shape or a sequence. Carries a warning, in the figure or beside it, and often has no numeric value axis. Never read a number off one.
Real values
Published numbers quoted in the text, used as model parameters or plotted — protocol values, assessed trends, reference profiles, budgets, event dates. Sourced in the grey note at the foot of each page.
Not reproduced
Described in prose only, because it is an observational field or record that cannot be redrawn honestly. Your lecturer has the original.

The rule, stated once. If a figure on these pages has numbers on both axes, it is one of three things: computed from an equation you are given, a plot of published values named in the grey note at the foot of its page, or a schematic. In Lectures 1, 7 and 8 a schematic carries its warning inside the figure. In Lectures 2 to 6 many do not — the warning is in the caption or in the foot note — and the tables below say where it is.

How to use this page as a student. When a figure surprises you, come here first and check what kind of object it is. A surprising result from a computed figure is telling you something about the physics. A surprising detail in a schematic is telling you something about the person who drew it. Learning to ask that question about any figure — in this course, in a paper, in a newspaper — is worth more than any single fact in the lecture.

Lecture 1 · Components of the climate system

Page 8 · The biosphere

Land-cover maps; Köppen world map Not reproduced Observational fields. Described in prose; the lecturer shows the originals.

Lecture 2 · The Earth’s energy budget

Figure and equation numbers refer to Goosse et al., chapter 2, §2.1, as they are used on the pages. Almost everything in this lecture is computed from a short list of constants printed at the foot of each page — σ, the solar constant, the planetary albedo, the radii of the Sun and the Earth. The measured quantities it uses are the US Standard Atmosphere and the observed global energy budget, and both are quoted, not recomputed.

Lecture 5 · Modelling the climate system

Figure and equation numbers refer to Goosse et al., chapter 3. This is a lecture about models, so most of its demonstrations run a small model on deliberately synthetic input — an invented landscape, a seeded ensemble, a generated station record. The arithmetic applied to that input is what a real model or evaluation does; the input is not a place and not a dataset.

What this means when you read the pages

Three habits

  • Trust the computed figures with their inputs. The insolation calculator, the regression, the Stommel branches and the spectra are as correct as the parameters given to them. Change a parameter and the figure changes correctly — that is what makes them worth playing with.
  • Read schematics for shape, never for value. If a schematic has no numbers on its vertical axis, that is on purpose. The Dansgaard–Oeschger figure tells you the events are abrupt and asymmetric; it does not tell you that event 12 was 11.4 K.
  • Take the parameter values seriously. The numbers that carry the argument — 478, 3.7, 0.89, 90 ppm, 0.11 K — are sourced in the grey note at the foot of each page. Those notes are where the citations live, and they are worth reading once.
Where a tuned model could mislead you, and where it could not. The Stommel threshold of 0.35 Sv is chosen, not measured; treat it as an illustration of structure. But the existence of two stable states and a hysteresis loop follows from the equations, not from the tuning, and would survive any parameter choice in a wide range. The same distinction applies throughout: ask whether a conclusion depends on the numbers or on the shape of the equations. The conclusions these lectures ask you to remember are all of the second kind.
And the honest limit of the whole approach. A demonstration that reproduces a published result is evidence about the method, not independent confirmation of the result. Nothing on these pages replaces the papers cited in the notes, and where a real observational figure is the point — Fig. 5.42 above all — you should look at the real one.

Scope

This appendix covers all eight lectures. It does not cover the three PC labs.

The lectures are not alike in this respect, and the difference is worth noticing. Lectures 1 to 3 describe physics that is largely closed-form — radiation, hydrostatics, Coriolis, Ekman, the equation of state, the surface energy balance — so almost everything in them is computed from stated constants, with a few reference values such as the US Standard Atmosphere and Oke’s site measurements quoted. Lecture 4 plots more observed quantities than any other, as short tables of published values, and draws smoothed representative curves where a gridded field would be needed. Lecture 5 is about models, so it runs them on deliberately synthetic input. Lecture 6 computes with published feedback and forcing values as its parameters. Lecture 7 asks about the past, where the evidence is proxy records and reconstructed fields that a self-contained page cannot honestly redraw — so it contains a good deal of labelled schematic. Lecture 8 asks about the future, where there is no data at all, only models — so almost everything in it is computed, and several of its demonstrations can be checked against published assessed numbers on screen. Different subjects, different strategies for the same constraint.

Where the pages fall short of their own rule. Five figures quote values with no source named on their page: two markers on the power ladder in Lecture 2, the CO2 seasonal amplitudes, the soil carbon pools and the ocean pump profiles in Lecture 4, and the chronology of model components in Lecture 5. The Lecture 3 explorer does not say on screen that its surface inputs are presets, and several representative curves in Lectures 4 and 6 carry their warning only in the foot note. All of these are marked in the tables above.

If you find a figure anywhere in this course that you cannot classify into one of the four kinds above, that is a defect and worth reporting.
