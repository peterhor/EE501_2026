Appendix: what is data and what is not

Every figure and every demonstration in this lecture, and where its numbers come from. Some of what you see on these pages is an exact calculation, some is a model you are running live, some is a drawing that carries a shape but no measurements — and it matters a great deal which is which. This page says, for each one.

Why this page exists

It is not a disclaimer. It is part of the teaching.

These pages are built by hand and contain no observational datasets. That is a deliberate constraint, not an oversight: a self-contained HTML page that loads no external data cannot plot the EPICA ice core or HadCRUT. Faced with that, there are three honest options and one dishonest one. The dishonest one — drawing a curve that looks like a measurement and letting you assume it is — is not used anywhere in this lecture.

Computed
Evaluated live in your browser from an equation or a published model, with parameters given. Reproducible, and correct to the precision of the inputs.
Schematic
Drawn by hand to carry a shape or a sequence. Carries a warning inside the figure and usually has no numeric value axis. Never read a number off one.
Real values
Published numbers quoted in the text or used as model parameters — protocol values, assessed trends, event dates. Sourced in the grey note at the foot of each page.
Not reproduced
Described in prose only, because it is an observational field or record that cannot be redrawn honestly. Your lecturer has the original.

The rule, stated once. If a figure on these pages has numbers on both axes, either it was computed from an equation you are given, or it is labelled inside the figure as a schematic. There is no third case.

How to use this page as a student. When a figure surprises you, come here first and check what kind of object it is. A surprising result from a computed figure is telling you something about the physics. A surprising detail in a schematic is telling you something about the person who drew it. Learning to ask that question about any figure — in this course, in a paper, in a newspaper — is worth more than any single fact in the lecture.

Stommel two-box model Computed Stommel (1961). The equilibrium branches are the exact roots of the resulting quadratic; the time integration uses a 5-year step. What this means when you read the pages

Three habits

  • Trust the computed figures with their inputs. The insolation calculator, the regression, the Stommel branches and the spectra are as correct as the parameters given to them. Change a parameter and the figure changes correctly — that is what makes them worth playing with.
  • Read schematics for shape, never for value. If a schematic has no numbers on its vertical axis, that is on purpose. The Dansgaard–Oeschger figure tells you the events are abrupt and asymmetric; it does not tell you that event 12 was 11.4 K.
  • Take the parameter values seriously. The numbers that carry the argument — 478, 3.7, 0.89, 90 ppm, 0.11 K — are sourced in the grey note at the foot of each page. Those notes are where the citations live, and they are worth reading once.
Where a tuned model could mislead you, and where it could not. The Stommel threshold of 0.35 Sv is chosen, not measured; treat it as an illustration of structure. But the existence of two stable states and a hysteresis loop follows from the equations, not from the tuning, and would survive any parameter choice in a wide range. The same distinction applies throughout: ask whether a conclusion depends on the numbers or on the shape of the equations. The conclusions this lecture asks you to remember are all of the second kind.
And the honest limit of the whole approach. A demonstration that reproduces a published result is evidence about the method, not independent confirmation of the result. Nothing on these pages replaces the papers cited in the notes, and where a real observational figure is the point — Fig. 5.42 above all — you should look at the real one.

Scope

This appendix covers Lecture 7. The interactive material for Lectures 2 to 6 carries the same kind of sourcing in the grey note at the foot of each page, and those pages contain a much higher proportion of closed-form calculation and a much lower proportion of palaeoclimate proxy material — there is very little in them that could be mistaken for a measurement.
