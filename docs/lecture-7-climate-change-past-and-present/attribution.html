Detection and attribution

Five pages of establishing what the climate does on its own, so that this page can ask the only question that needed all of it: is the last century something the climate could have done by itself? The answer is a number with an uncertainty on it, produced by a method, and the method is what this page is about.

What the instrumental record says

Before any interpretation: the observations, and the three things about them that matter.

  • The trend. A linear fit to the global mean surface temperature over 1901–2012 gives a rise of 0.89 °C across that period. Three independently constructed datasets, with different corrections for changing instruments, ship intakes and station moves, agree on it within their stated uncertainty.
  • It is not steady. There is a rapid rise to about 1940, a plateau to the mid-1970s, and a steeper rise since. Both departures from a straight line matter, and both have explanations you already have the pieces for.
  • It is almost everywhere. The warming appears in nearly every region, but not uniformly: faster over land than over ocean, and fastest at high northern latitudes.

Why land warms faster than ocean. Two reasons, both from earlier lectures. The ocean has an enormous heat capacity and mixes heat downward, so it lags — that is the transient-response argument of Lecture 6. And over land, water is limited: a surface that cannot evaporate must dispose of extra energy as sensible heat, which means a temperature rise. That is the Bowen ratio of Lecture 3, applied to a whole continent.

Why the mid-century plateau. Sulphate aerosol emissions grew rapidly in the post-war decades and their negative forcing partly offset the greenhouse forcing — see the budget in Lecture 6, page 2. Internal variability contributed as well; the Pacific and Atlantic modes of page 2 can add or remove a tenth of a degree from a decadal mean. Two candidate explanations for one feature — which is exactly the situation a formal method is needed for.

One thing that requires no statistics at all

The origin of the CO2 increase is not an inference from a model. It is a measurement, several times over.

EvidenceWhat it shows
Mass balance Roughly twice as much carbon has been emitted from fossil fuels and land-use change as has accumulated in the atmosphere. The atmospheric rise is not just consistent with emissions — it is smaller than them, because land and ocean absorb the rest. See the airborne fraction in Lecture 4.
Carbon-13 Plants discriminate against 13C, so fossil carbon is isotopically light. Atmospheric δ13C has been falling as CO2 rises — exactly what adding plant-derived carbon does, and the opposite of what ocean outgassing would do.
Carbon-14 Fossil carbon is millions of years old and contains no 14C at all. Atmospheric 14C/12C fell measurably before nuclear testing overwhelmed the signal.
Atmospheric oxygen Burning carbon consumes oxygen. Atmospheric O2 is declining in the precise ratio that combustion requires. Volcanoes and the ocean do not consume oxygen.
The ocean is acidifying If the ocean were the source of the extra CO2, its carbon content and pH would be going the other way. Both are moving as they must if the ocean is a sink — the carbonate chemistry of Lecture 4, page 3.

Read that table as a structure, not a list. Five independent lines, each of which would have to be wrong in a different way for the conclusion to fail, and each of which would have to be wrong in a direction that happens to agree with the other four. That is why the word used for this particular conclusion is unequivocal — a word that appears very rarely in the assessment reports, and never lightly.

Be careful to keep two claims apart. “The CO2 rise is anthropogenic” is settled by the table above. “The warming is anthropogenic” is a different claim, and it needs the rest of this page. Conflating them is the commonest way this argument is presented badly, in both directions.

The method

Detection and attribution are two different steps

The vocabulary is precise and it is worth learning exactly.

Detection is showing that an observed change is inconsistent with internal variability alone. It is a statement about a null hypothesis, and it names no cause. Everything on pages 1, 2 and 5 exists to make this step possible: you cannot declare something inconsistent with internal variability until you have quantified internal variability.

Attribution is showing that the observed change is consistent with a particular combination of causes, and inconsistent with plausible alternatives. Both halves are required. Consistency with one explanation proves nothing on its own; you must also fail to be consistent with the others.

T(t)  =  β1 Resp1(t)  +  β2 Resp2(t)  +  …  +  u(t)
T is the observed record; Respi is the modelled pattern of the response to forcing i — the fingerprint; βi is a scaling coefficient; u is internal variability, whose statistics come from long control simulations
T(t) the observed temperature record being explained, K
Respi(t) the modelled response pattern to forcing i — the fingerprint, in space, season and time
βi scaling coefficient fitted to the observations; β ≈ 1 means the model got the amplitude right
u(t) internal variability, whose statistics come from long unforced control simulations, K
t time

The trick, and it is a good one. The models supply the shape of each response — its pattern in space, in season, in the vertical, and in time. The observations supply the amplitude, through the fitted β. So the conclusion does not depend on the models having the right climate sensitivity: if a model warms too much, its fingerprint is too large, and the fitted β simply comes out below one. What the models must get right is the shape, which is far more robust than the amplitude.

  • β significantly greater than zero → that forcing’s response is detected in the observations.
  • β consistent with one → the model’s amplitude is consistent with the observations; no rescaling needed.
  • β significantly different from one → the response is there but the model has it too strong or too weak. Useful information, not a refutation.
  • β consistent with zero → that forcing cannot be shown to have contributed over the period analysed.
Why a fingerprint is spatial and not just a time series. Solar and greenhouse forcing both warm the surface, so their time series look similar and are hard to separate. But they do different things elsewhere: greenhouse warming cools the stratosphere while solar warming heats it; it warms nights more than days; it warms the Arctic disproportionately. Those differences are the fingerprint, and they are what makes the separation possible. The one-dimensional version below is a teaching simplification of a method that is multi-dimensional.

Three things to notice while you are doing it.
  • Press Try natural forcings only. You can match the volcanic dips precisely and you cannot produce the trend at all, at any scaling. The natural forcings have no trend to give. That is a detection result: the observations are inconsistent with natural forcing plus internal variability.
  • Now raise βGHG alone until the end of the record matches. The early twentieth century is now too warm and the mid-century plateau has vanished. Bring in βother anth and both come right. The aerosol term is what makes the shape work, and it is why the two are hard to separate: pushing one up and the other down leaves the total nearly unchanged.
  • Look at the error bars, and read them carefully. βGHG comes out well determined and far from zero — that is the detection result. βother anth has a much wider range, because its response has nearly the same shape as the greenhouse one and the data cannot fully separate them. And βnatural is also well determined, which is not a result about the warming: it is well determined because the volcanic dips are sharp and unmistakable. Multiply βnatural by the trend of the natural response and you get essentially nothing. A forcing can be confidently detected and still contribute nothing to the trend.
What is real here and what is not. The regression, the least-squares solution, the uncertainty ranges and the degeneracy between the greenhouse and aerosol terms are all real and are computed live from the series you see. The series themselves are produced by the same zero-dimensional energy balance model as page 5, driven by representative forcing histories, and the “observations” are one of those responses plus synthetic internal variability — so this demonstrates the method, not the published result. A real detection and attribution study uses spatial fields from many models, and control simulations thousands of years long to characterise u(t).

How to get any trend you like

The counter-demonstration, and it is worth two minutes. Drag the two ends of the window across a record that has a known, constant underlying trend.

The underlying forcing history never changes.
Try 1998 to 2012 and see what you can claim.
The lesson, stated so it survives contact with an argument. Every trend you can produce from this record is arithmetically correct. The record has one true forced trend throughout, and short windows recover almost anything. The defence is not to distrust the arithmetic; it is to ask whether the window was chosen before or after looking at the data, and to attach an uncertainty that accounts for the internal variability of page 1. Note that the ends of the most-quoted such window, 1998 and 2012, are a very strong El Niño and a La Niña — look them up on page 2.

What the assessment actually concludes, and what the words mean

Calibrated language, used consistently, and often misread.

StatementWhat kind of claim it is
The anthropogenic origin of the CO2 rise is unequivocal A statement of fact, from the five independent lines of evidence above. Not a probability.
Observations are inconsistent with internal variability alone on decadal to centennial timescales A detection result. The null hypothesis of “the climate did this by itself” is rejected.
Anthropogenic forcing is required to explain the observed warming An attribution result: consistent with the anthropogenic fingerprint, inconsistent with every alternative combination tested.
The greenhouse-gas contribution is clearly detected, and the best estimate of the human contribution to recent warming is close to the whole of it The fitted β, with its range — exactly the quantity you produced in the demonstration.

On the word likely. In this literature it is calibrated: it means a probability greater than about two-thirds, very likely greater than 90 %, extremely likely greater than 95 %. It is not hedging. A statement without such a qualifier is generally a stronger claim, not a weaker one.

The limits, which are also part of the result. Attribution is strongest for the global mean and for large-scale patterns, weaker for regional trends, and weakest for individual events — because the signal-to-noise argument of page 1 works against you at every step down in scale. Attribution of a single flood or heatwave is a real and growing field, but it makes a different and more careful claim: not “this event was caused by climate change” but “an event like this became this much more likely”.

The lecture in one argument

Six pages, one chain of reasoning. It is worth being able to reproduce this out loud.

  • The climate varies without being pushed, and the amount depends strongly on the scale you look at. — page 1
  • That variability has structure, in a handful of named modes with known patterns, magnitudes and timescales. — page 2
  • It also responds to forcing, and the cleanest case — orbital — shows the response is amplified, non-linear, and unlike the forcing. — page 3
  • It contains thresholds, so the response can be abrupt even when the forcing is not. — page 4
  • In a world like ours the natural amplitudes are small — a few tenths of a degree over centuries, and regionally asynchronous. — page 5
  • Therefore the twentieth-century change can be tested against all of that, quantitatively, and it fails to be explicable without an anthropogenic term. — this page

Notice what the argument does not rest on. Not on any single model being right; not on the temperature record alone; not on the assumption that the past is a guide to the future. It rests on having measured how large natural variability is, and on the forced responses having distinguishable shapes. That is why the palaeoclimate half of this lecture is not background material — it is the denominator of the final calculation.

The calibrated-uncertainty vocabulary is that of the IPCC assessment reports. Both demonstrations on this page are synthetic, and deliberately so. The three fingerprints are responses of the same zero-dimensional energy balance model used on page 5 — λf = −1.26 W m−2 K−1, a 200 m slab — driven separately by a greenhouse forcing rising to +3.2 W m−2, an other-anthropogenic forcing falling to about −1.1 W m−2, and a natural forcing containing the eruptions of 1883, 1902, 1963, 1982 and 1991 together with a small solar variation; these magnitudes match the budget of Lecture 6, page 2. The “observations” are the sum of those three with unit coefficients plus first-order autoregressive noise of standard deviation 0.11 K, the global-mean internal variability of page 1. The regression, the ordinary least-squares solution and the 2σ uncertainties derived from the residual variance and the normal equations are computed live and are real; the degeneracy you will find between the greenhouse and aerosol coefficients is a real property of that design matrix and a real difficulty in the published studies. Note that the ordinary least-squares standard errors shown assume independent residuals, whereas internal variability is autocorrelated, so the plotted ranges are somewhat narrower than a proper treatment would give; published studies use the covariance structure of long control simulations instead, and their ranges are correspondingly wider. Because the true coefficients are 1 by construction, this demonstrates how the method behaves rather than reproducing any published value.
