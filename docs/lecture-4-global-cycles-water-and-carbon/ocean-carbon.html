The oceanic carbon cycle

The ocean holds more than forty times as much carbon as the atmosphere. Not because CO2 is especially soluble — it is not — but because as soon as it dissolves it reacts, and stops being CO2. That single chemical fact is the reason the ocean has absorbed about a quarter of everything we have emitted.

Getting carbon across the surface

The air–sea flux

Gas exchange is driven by a difference in partial pressure, not concentration. The water and the air each have a pCO2, and carbon moves down the gradient.

Three things set pWCO2, and they fight each other. Temperature — solubility falls as water warms, so warm water pushes CO2 out. Biology — photosynthesis strips CO2 out of the surface, pulling pWCO2 down. Circulation — upwelling brings carbon-rich deep water to the surface, pushing it up. Which one wins depends entirely on where you are.

The equatorial ocean is a CO2 source and the mid-latitudes are sinks. Solubility alone would give you the mid-latitude part of that and the tropical part too — warm water holds less — but it would not give you anything like this magnitude at the equator. What does is circulation: equatorial upwelling brings water to the surface loaded with the carbon the biological pump sent down elsewhere, and it outgasses. The strongest sinks are at 40–60° in both hemispheres, where cold surface water is subducting and taking its dissolved carbon down with it.

Question. The solubility of CO2 falls as water warms. Sketch what that means for the ocean sink over the coming century, and then explain why the observed sink has nevertheless been growing rather than shrinking. Which term in Eq. 2.41 is winning?

Why the ocean can hold so much

Dissolve, then react

Carbon dioxide is only moderately soluble. What makes seawater a carbon sponge is that dissolved CO2 immediately reacts with water and with the carbonate already there, converting itself into forms that no longer exert any partial pressure.

Read Eq. 2.47 carefully, because it is the whole trick. Add CO2 to seawater and it does not simply sit there as dissolved gas. It consumes a carbonate ion and produces two bicarbonate ions. The dissolved gas — the only form that exerts a partial pressure and can escape back to the atmosphere — is almost entirely converted into something that cannot. Of the carbon you add to seawater, only a few per cent stays as H2CO3; the rest is buffered away into bicarbonate. That is why seawater takes up roughly fifty times more carbon than its physical solubility alone would suggest — and the solver below will tell you the exact percentage for any water you care to specify.

And notice the price. Every step to the right releases H+, and every added CO2 molecule consumes a carbonate ion. So the very reaction that lets the ocean absorb our emissions makes it more acidic and depletes exactly the ion that corals, coccolithophores and pteropods need to build their shells. Ocean acidification is not a side effect of the ocean carbon sink. It is the ocean carbon sink.

Alk  =  [HCO3−] + 2[CO32−] + [B(OH)4−] + [OH−] − [H+] + …
Alk total alkalinity — the excess of bases over acids, mol kg−1
[HCO3−] bicarbonate concentration, mol kg−1
[CO32−] carbonate concentration; it counts twice because it carries two charges
[B(OH)4−] borate concentration, the largest of the minor contributions
[OH−] hydroxide concentration
[H+] proton concentration, subtracted because it is an acid
… further minor bases (phosphate, silicate) omitted here

Alkalinity is the excess of bases over acids, and it is the second number you need. DIC alone does not determine the chemistry: the same DIC at a different alkalinity gives a completely different pH and a completely different pCO2. Alkalinity is conservative — it does not change when CO2 dissolves, because the proton released is exactly balanced by the bicarbonate produced. It does change when calcium carbonate forms or dissolves, which is why the carbonate pump matters so much.

The three pumps

Why the deep ocean is richer in carbon than the surface

If the ocean were stirred until it was uniform, its surface would be in equilibrium with a much higher atmospheric CO2 than it actually is. Three processes keep the surface depleted and the deep ocean loaded. Click a pump to see what it does to DIC and to alkalinity.

The carbonate pump works against the other two. That is the counter-intuitive result worth spending a minute on. Building a shell takes one carbon atom out of the surface water — which should help — but it also removes a carbonate ion, and Eq. 2.48 counts carbonate twice. Net alkalinity falls by two for every one unit of DIC, and by Eq. 2.47 that pushes the equilibrium back towards H2CO3, raising pCO2. Marine calcifiers are, on balance, a source of atmospheric CO2.

Together the three pumps make DIC about 15 % higher at depth than at the surface, and that vertical gradient is worth a great deal of atmospheric CO2. Model experiments that switch the biological pump off entirely raise the pre-industrial atmosphere from 280 ppm to somewhere around 450–500; running it at maximum efficiency, so that every nutrient reaching the surface is used, pulls it down towards 150. The glacial–interglacial swing of 180 to 280 ppm sits comfortably inside that range, which is why the ocean is the first place to look for its explanation.

Question. Deep water eventually upwells and reaches the surface again carrying its 15 % extra DIC. Why does that not simply undo the pumps and release all the carbon back to the atmosphere?
