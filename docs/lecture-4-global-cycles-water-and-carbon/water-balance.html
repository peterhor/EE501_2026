The global water balance

Nearly all of the Earth’s water sits in one reservoir and hardly moves. Nearly all of the movement happens in a reservoir that holds almost nothing. Getting that pairing straight — size against turnover — is most of what the hydrological cycle has to teach.

One equation, applied to any box you like

Every reservoir in the cycle obeys the same statement. Change what you draw the box around and you get a different, useful special case.

Water balance of a reservoir
dS/dt  =  inputs − outputs   [103 km3 yr−1]
S amount of water stored in the reservoir, 103 km3
t time, yr
inputs all fluxes entering the box — precipitation, inflow, melt, 103 km3 yr−1
outputs all fluxes leaving it — evaporation, runoff, drainage, 103 km3 yr−1

Draw the box around the whole planet and dS/dt = 0 to a very good approximation: global evaporation must equal global precipitation, 486×103 km3 yr−1 of each. Draw it around the land only and you get P − ET = R, the runoff equation. Draw it around the ocean only and you get E − P = R, the same 40 going the other way. Draw it around one field and you get the soil water balance further down this page.

The cycle closes only because the atmosphere carries water inland. Land loses less to the air than it receives from it. The books balance because 40×103 km3 yr−1 of vapour is transported off the ocean and across the coastline every year, and exactly that much comes back down the rivers.

Where the water actually is

The same reservoirs on a logarithmic axis, because they span six orders of magnitude and a linear bar chart would show one bar. Percentages are of the global total.

Almost exactly 97 % of the water in this table is salt water in the ocean. Of the small fresh remainder, about seven tenths is locked up as ice and most of the rest is groundwater. Everything that is both fresh and readily reachable — soil moisture, lakes, rivers, the atmosphere — adds up to well under one part in a thousand of the planet’s water. That single sentence is why water availability, not water quantity, is what constrains ecosystems and societies.

Residence time

Size divided by throughput

τ  =  S / Fout   [yr] = [103 km3] ÷ [103 km3 yr−1]
τ residence time — how long the average water molecule stays in the reservoir, yr
S amount of water stored in the reservoir, 103 km3
Fout throughput — the flux leaving it at steady state, 103 km3 yr−1

The residence time of a reservoir at steady state is how long the average water molecule stays in it. It is the single most useful number in the whole cycle, because it tells you how fast that reservoir can respond to a change — and the answers here run from nine days to nine thousand years.

ReservoirVolume% of all water ThroughflowτWhat the number means

Read the two extremes together. The atmosphere holds 0.001 % of the water and turns it over about forty times a year; the ocean holds 97 % and turns it over once every three millennia. So the atmosphere is where the water cycle responds to climate — within days — and the ocean is where it remembers. Everything in between, from soil moisture at about a year to ice at several thousand, sets the timescale on which a particular ecosystem feels a drought.

Question. Atmospheric water vapour rises by about 7 % per kelvin of warming (Clausius–Clapeyron), but global precipitation rises by only 2–3 % per kelvin. Using τ = S/Fout, what must therefore happen to the residence time of water in the atmosphere — and what does that imply for the distance vapour travels between evaporating and falling?

Water balance on land

The soil water balance, one year at a time

Shrink the control volume to a single soil column and the same equation becomes a working model. Precipitation comes in; evapotranspiration, surface runoff and deep drainage go out; what is left over changes the storage. This is the box in Fig. 2.23, integrated forward day by day.

dS/dt  =  P − ET − Rsurface − Ddrainage   [mm day−1]

t time, days
P precipitation reaching the ground, mm day−1
ET evapotranspiration — evaporation plus plant transpiration, mm day−1
Rsurface surface runoff, mm day−1

What the model is. A single bucket, daily time step, 365 days, spun up to a repeating annual cycle. Actual ET is potential ET throttled by a linear stress function once storage falls below half of capacity; drainage is proportional to the cube of relative wetness; anything above capacity leaves as surface runoff the same day. Rainfall is generated from the preset’s seasonal shape with a fixed random seed, so the same settings always give the same year. It is a teaching model, not a land surface scheme — but every term in it appears in the real ones.

Question. Put the model in the Mediterranean preset and note the annual actual ET. Now double the holding capacity without changing anything else. Precipitation has not changed, and neither has the atmospheric demand — so why does evapotranspiration go up, and which term did that water come out of?

Water balance at the ocean surface

Evaporation minus precipitation, and the fingerprint it leaves

E − P over the ocean is nowhere near zero — it is strongly positive under the subtropical highs and strongly negative under the ITCZ and the storm tracks. Because evaporation removes fresh water and leaves the salt behind, the pattern is written into the sea surface salinity. Zonal means; after Trenberth et al. (2007), the basis of Fig. 2.24.

Show

Why this matters beyond the water cycle. E − P is a freshwater flux, and a freshwater flux is a buoyancy flux. It sets surface salinity, salinity contributes to density alongside temperature, and density differences drive the overturning circulation. The hydrological cycle is therefore one of the controls on ocean circulation, not merely a passenger on it — which is the thread picked up again in the abrupt-change lab.

Question. Between the 1950s and the 2000s the salty subtropical surface waters got saltier and the fresh high-latitude waters got fresher, with very little change in the global mean salinity. What does that pattern say about E and P separately — and why is the ocean a better rain gauge than the rain gauges?
