The global carbon budget

The carbon cycle is an enormous flywheel: about 200 petagrams of carbon a year moving between the atmosphere, the ocean and the land, in both directions, and very nearly cancelling. Human beings add around eleven. Everything that has happened to the climate since 1850 is the story of what happens when a balanced cycle is given a small, one-way nudge.

What the atmosphere is doing

The wiggle is the land biosphere breathing

Peak-to-trough amplitude of the CO2 seasonal cycle at background stations, against latitude, with the fraction of each latitude band that is vegetated land drawn behind it.

The amplitude follows the land, not the latitude. At Barrow, at 71°N, CO2 swings by about 16 ppm over the year; at the South Pole it swings by barely one. There is no more sunlight in the northern hemisphere than the southern — but there is roughly twice as much land, and almost all of the world’s boreal and temperate forest. Photosynthesis draws the atmosphere down from May, respiration and decomposition give it back from September, and the whole northern hemisphere records it.

And the amplitude is growing. At high northern latitudes it is roughly a third larger than it was in the 1960s. Something has changed about how much carbon the northern land surface takes up and releases each year — longer growing seasons, CO2 fertilisation, warming-driven respiration — and the atmosphere is measuring it for us, free of charge.

Question. The seasonal minimum happens in September, not in June when insolation peaks. Why is the atmosphere still drawing down in July and August, and what does the lag tell you about the difference between the rate of photosynthesis and its cumulative effect?

The reservoirs

Stocks, and the fluxes between them

Carbon stocks in PgC on a logarithmic axis; the annual exchange fluxes beneath them on a linear one. After Ciais et al. (2013), the source of Fig. 2.26. 1 PgC = 1 GtC = 1015 g of carbon = 3.67 Gt CO2.

The atmosphere is one of the smallest reservoirs in the picture — smaller than the soil, smaller than the vegetation plus soil taken together, and about one part in forty of the ocean. That is exactly why it is the one that changes. Move 1 % of the ocean’s carbon into the air and the atmosphere doubles. Move 1 % of the atmosphere’s carbon into the ocean and the ocean does not notice.

Now look at the scale of the perturbation. Photosynthesis and respiration on land exchange about 120 PgC each way every year. The ocean exchanges about 80 each way. Human emissions are about 11. We are not overwhelming the carbon cycle — we are biasing it by a few per cent, one way, for two centuries running. A cycle that cancels to within 1 % is a cycle in which a 5 % bias accumulates relentlessly.

The airborne fraction, and why it is the number to watch

AF  =  Gatmosphere / (Efossil + Eland-use)

Roughly 45 % of everything emitted has stayed in the air; the ocean and the land have quietly absorbed the other 55 %, for free, every decade since measurements began. The airborne fraction has been remarkably stable, which means the sinks have so far grown roughly in proportion to the emissions — they take up more because there is more CO2 pressing on them. Nothing guarantees that continues. Both sinks are expected to weaken as a fraction of emissions: the ocean because the buffering chemistry of Part 3 gets less effective as it fills, the land because warming increases respiration and fire.

Why carbon is not water

Page 1 argued that residence time tells you how fast a reservoir responds. For carbon that reasoning fails, and the way it fails is the single most important asymmetry in the whole subject.

Molecular residence time
~4 yr
829 PgC ÷ ~200 PgC yr−1 of gross exchange
Perturbation, first stage
~10–100 yr
mixing into the ocean surface and the land biosphere
Perturbation, second stage
~1000 yr
mixing into the deep ocean; CaCO3 dissolution
Last ~20 % of a pulse
>105 yr
removed only by silicate weathering

An individual CO2 molecule leaves the atmosphere in about four years. It is taken up by a leaf or dissolves into the sea. But the quantity of carbon in the atmosphere does not fall on that timescale, because for every molecule that leaves, another comes back out. The gross fluxes are two-way; only the imbalance matters. So the relevant number is not the residence time of a molecule but the adjustment time of the perturbation — how long it takes the whole coupled system to redistribute an added pulse of carbon and settle into a new equilibrium.

That is why a molecule of water vapour and a molecule of CO2 behave so differently as greenhouse gases. Water vapour rains out in nine days, so its concentration is set by temperature and it can only act as a feedback. CO2 does not rain out at all, so its concentration is set by emissions, and it acts as a forcing. Same physics of absorption; completely different role in the climate system, and the difference is entirely one of residence time.
