Land, rock and methane

Three reservoirs on three utterly different clocks. The land biosphere turns its carbon over in years to centuries; the rocks in hundreds of thousands; and methane, the odd one out, in about nine. Each of those numbers decides what kind of climate problem the reservoir presents.

The terrestrial carbon cycle

From 123 down to 2, one subtraction at a time

Land plants take up more carbon than all the phytoplankton in the ocean. Almost all of it goes straight back. The chain of definitions below is how ecologists keep track of what is left at each stage — and the terms are not interchangeable, which is why they are worth learning in order. PgC yr−1.

TermPgC yr−1DefinitionWhat removes the next slice
GPP123Gross primary production — total photosynthetic carbon fixationAutotrophic respiration: the plant burns roughly half of it to stay alive
NPP63Net primary production — what is actually built into plant tissueHeterotrophic respiration: decomposers eating litter and soil organic matter
NEP8Net ecosystem production — the balance of production and decompositionDisturbance: fire, harvest, erosion, dissolved losses to rivers
NBP2.3Net biome production — what the land actually keepsNothing. This is the terrestrial sink in the budget on page 2.

The land sink is a residual of two enormous, nearly equal numbers. 123 in, roughly 121 out. Two per cent of the gross flux survives to become a sink. That is a precarious way to run a planetary service — a few per cent change in either respiration or production, and the sink turns into a source without anything dramatic having happened. This is the mechanism behind almost every worrying carbon-cycle feedback: warming raises respiration faster than it raises production.

Where and when the land takes carbon up

Zonal total net primary production through the year. Colour is production per degree of latitude, so it already includes how much land there is at each latitude — this is the signal the atmosphere actually sees. The equivalent of the satellite map in Fig. 2.28, opened out across all twelve months instead of just June.

Two things to notice. The tropics are a broad, bright, permanent band — high production all year, and almost no seasonality, so they contribute enormously to GPP and almost nothing to the seasonal CO2 wiggle. The northern mid- and high latitudes are the opposite: a fierce, short pulse from May to September and effectively nothing for the rest of the year. That pulse is what carves the sawtooth in the Keeling curve. The southern hemisphere has its own pulse six months out of phase, but so much less land that it barely registers.

And this is why land-cover change is a climate issue twice over. It changes the albedo, roughness and evapotranspiration of the surface — the whole of the local energy balance — and it changes which of these bands is taking carbon out of the atmosphere.

Soil: the slow half of the land carbon cycle

Roughly three times as much carbon is stored in soils as in living vegetation, and much more than in the atmosphere. It is not one pool but several, with turnover times spanning four orders of magnitude.

The slow pools are what make soil carbon dangerous. Litter turns over in a year, so it is in near-equilibrium with the climate at all times. Peat and permafrost carbon has been accumulating for thousands of years because cold and waterlogging stop decomposition, not because the carbon is chemically stable. Warm it or drain it and decomposition resumes — releasing carbon that took millennia to store over decades. The permafrost pool alone is about twice the size of the entire atmosphere.

Fire

Decomposition is not the only way carbon leaves the biosphere. Fires return around 2.2 PgC yr−1 to the atmosphere — comparable to the entire land sink. Mean annual emissions by region, Tg C yr−1, after Randerson et al. (2018) — the update to Fig. 2.29.

Half of the world’s fire carbon comes from African savanna, and almost none of it matters for the carbon budget. Savanna burns most years and regrows within one; over a fire cycle the emissions and the regrowth cancel, so it is a large gross flux and a near-zero net one. What does matter is fire that is not followed by regrowth: tropical deforestation fires, and above all peat fires in equatorial Asia, where the carbon burnt has been accumulating for thousands of years. The 1997 Indonesian peat fires released somewhere between 0.8 and 2.6 PgC in a single season — up to a quarter of global fossil fuel emissions that year, from one event.

Boreal fire is the one to watch. It is a small term today and it is growing, it burns deep organic soil rather than just the canopy, and the char and soot it deposits on snow and ice change the albedo — a carbon-cycle process reaching directly into the energy balance.

Geological reservoirs

The long-term inorganic carbon cycle

Everything so far has moved carbon between reservoirs that exchange in years to millennia. There is one more loop, three orders of magnitude slower, and it is the loop that ultimately sets how much carbon is in the ocean and atmosphere at all. Three reactions, Eq. 2.49–2.51 in the lecture.

1 · Sedimentation   Ca2+ + CO32−  →  CaCO3↓   shells buried in shallow seas become limestone
2 · Metamorphism   CaCO3 + SiO2  →  CaSiO3 + CO2↑   limestone subducted and cooked; CO2 returns via volcanoes
3 · Weathering   CaSiO3 + H2CO3  →  CaCO3 + SiO2 + H2O   uplifted silicate rock consumes carbonic acid from rain
Ca2+ dissolved calcium ion, delivered to the sea by rivers
CO32− dissolved carbonate ion
CaCO3 calcium carbonate — shells and, once buried, limestone; ↓ marks removal to the sediment
SiO2 silica (quartz)
CaSiO3 calcium silicate, the metamorphic rock produced at depth
CO2 carbon dioxide; ↑ marks its return to the atmosphere through volcanoes
H2CO3 carbonic acid, formed when rainwater dissolves atmospheric CO2
H2O water

The three reactions are a closed loop, and they very nearly cancel. Each moves only about 0.1–0.3 PgC yr−1 — a hundredth of what fossil fuel burning moves, and utterly negligible on any human timescale. But run them for ten million years and they have cycled the entire ocean–atmosphere carbon inventory several times over. On that timescale they are not negligible; they are the only thing that matters.

And the weathering step is temperature-dependent, which turns the loop into a thermostat. Chemical weathering goes faster when it is warm and when it rains more. So if CO2 rises and the planet warms, weathering speeds up, consumes more CO2, and pulls it back down. If the planet cools, weathering slows and volcanic CO2 accumulates until it warms again. It is a negative feedback with a response time of a few hundred thousand years — far too slow to help us, and quite possibly the reason the Earth has kept liquid water for four billion years.

The methane cycle

A different kind of greenhouse gas

Methane is present at about two parts per million — two hundred times less than CO2 — and still accounts for around a fifth of the anthropogenic greenhouse forcing. Two properties make it behave completely differently: it absorbs far more strongly per molecule, and it is destroyed.

The sink    CH4 + OH·  →  CH3· + H2O    and onward to CO2 through several steps
CH4 methane
OH· the hydroxyl radical — the atmosphere's main oxidising agent, produced by sunlight
CH3· methyl radical, which oxidises onward to CO2 in several further steps

Carbon dioxide has no chemical sink in the atmosphere. It can only be removed by being transferred somewhere else — dissolved in the ocean, built into a plant — and the ocean and the biosphere can and do give it back. Methane is different: the hydroxyl radical destroys it, converting it into water and, eventually, CO2. Roughly 90 % of the methane sink is this one reaction. Because the loss rate is proportional to the amount present, methane has a genuine lifetime — about nine years — and therefore a genuine steady state.

dB/dt  =  S − B/τ    at steady state, B* = Sτ
B burden — the total amount of methane in the atmosphere, Tg
t time, yr
S source strength — total methane emissions, Tg yr−1
τ atmospheric lifetime, about 9 yr for methane
B* steady-state burden, reached when the source and the sink balance, Tg

That one line is the entire difference between the two gases. Hold methane emissions constant and the concentration stops rising and settles at Sτ. Hold CO2 emissions constant and the concentration keeps climbing indefinitely. Stabilising methane requires constant emissions; stabilising CO2 requires zero emissions. That asymmetry is the single most important thing to take away from this section.

Notice what is missing from the anthropogenic list: combustion. Almost every anthropogenic methane source is either a leak or an anaerobic process — a stomach, a paddy, a landfill, a bog. Methane is what carbon becomes when it decomposes without oxygen. That is a genuinely useful organising idea: it tells you immediately why draining a wetland reduces methane and increases CO2, and why flooding one does the reverse.

The feedback that worries people. Wetlands are the biggest source and they are temperature-sensitive; permafrost thaw creates new wetlands and exposes old carbon; and warming may reduce the OH sink. All three point the same way. None of them is in the box model above, because the model treats natural sources as a number you set rather than something the climate decides.

Pulling the four parts together

What to take away

  • The same equation runs both cycles. dS/dt = inputs − outputs, applied to whatever box you choose to draw. Everything else is bookkeeping and chemistry.
  • Reservoir size and residence time are independent, and both matter. The ocean holds almost all the water and responds to nothing; the atmosphere holds almost none and responds to everything.
  • Fresh water is scarce and unevenly distributed, and the distribution is set by atmospheric circulation, not by how much water there is. E − P leaves its fingerprint in ocean salinity.
  • The carbon cycle is a near-perfect cancellation of huge fluxes. The human perturbation is small against the gross exchange and overwhelming against the residual.
  • About 45 % of emitted carbon stays in the air. The ocean and the land absorb the rest, and both are expected to become less efficient.
  • Carbonate chemistry, not solubility, is what lets the ocean hold carbon — and the price of that service is acidification and a falling carbonate ion concentration.
  • Three pumps keep the deep ocean carbon-rich, and one of them — the carbonate pump — works in the opposite direction to the other two.
  • Sedimentation, metamorphism and weathering close the budget over millions of years, and give the planet a slow thermostat.
  • Methane has a sink, so it has a steady state. Stabilising methane needs constant emissions; stabilising CO2 needs zero emissions.
