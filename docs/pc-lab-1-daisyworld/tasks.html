IntroA world of daisies

A cloudless grey planet, seeded with light and dark daisies, orbits a slowly brightening sun. Dark daisies warm their surroundings; light daisies cool them. Work through the exercises below and answer each question in your report.

E1Growth rate vs. temperature

Daisies grow fastest at a preferred temperature and not at all when it is too cold or too hot. The Growth-curve tab plots the parabolic growth rate β against local temperature Tₗ (eq. 3 of the paper). The dots mark the current local temperatures of each daisy type.

  1. What does the curve tell you about the relation between daisy growth and temperature?
  2. What is the optimal temperature for growth?
  3. What are the lower and upper temperature limits for growth?

E2A brightening sun

In the model the sun slowly brightens: luminosity L rises by 0.025 every time step. The Dynamics-vs-time tab shows this forcing as luminosity against time.

  1. What does this steady increase in luminosity represent physically?

E3A bare planet

Switch off both daisy populations to get a lifeless planet whose temperature is set purely by luminosity and the fixed bare-ground albedo. Sample Te at several luminosities and build the Te-vs-L curve yourself.

  1. For which luminosity values would the planet be habitable for daisies (use your viable temperature range from E1)?
  2. What is the global average temperature Te at L = 1.0?

E4Black daisies only

Now allow only black (dark, low-albedo) daisies. Sample the planet across luminosity and compare your Te-vs-L points with the bare planet of E3. Watch the black-daisy area too.

  1. How have the temperature conditions changed, e.g. at L = 1.0?
  2. What caused the difference compared with the bare planet (E3)?
  3. At which luminosity does the black-daisy area peak, and what is that peak area?
  4. Compare Te with the local black-daisy temperature Tb — why do they differ?

E5White daisies only

Reset and allow only white (bright, high-albedo) daisies. Sample temperature and the white-daisy area across luminosity.

  1. Compared with E3 and E4, what happens to the temperature?
  2. How does the area covered by daisies change with luminosity?
  3. How do you explain the differences with the bare planet (E3) and the black-daisy world (E4)?

E6Both daisies together

Let both species compete. Sample Te across the full luminosity range and see how the global temperature responds. Local temperatures Tw and Tb appear when you reveal.

  1. What happens to the global temperature when both black and white daisies are present?
  2. Which conditions are most favourable for black daisies?
  3. How do Tb and Tw differ from Te, and why?

E7Stronger albedo contrast

Increase the contrast between the daisies — start from white albedo 0.85, black albedo 0.15 — and repeat the two-species run of E6. Then experiment with the albedo sliders.

  1. What has changed for the temperatures Te, Tw and Tb?
  2. What has changed for the areas of black and white daisies?
  3. How do you explain the difference with E6?

E8A lower death rate

Return the albedos to reference values and lower the death rate γ from 0.3 to 0.2. Sample the daisy areas across luminosity and compare with the reference two-species run (E6).

  1. What has changed in the daisy areas?
  2. How do you explain the difference?

E9Daisyworld & global change

Step back and interpret the model. The sun really does brighten with age — about 10% every 1.1 billion years — while Earth has stayed habitable for billions of years.

  1. If our Sun brightens ~10% per 1.1 Gyr, roughly how much time does one model step (ΔL = 0.025) represent?
  2. What is the relevance of the Daisyworld model for studies of present-day global change?
