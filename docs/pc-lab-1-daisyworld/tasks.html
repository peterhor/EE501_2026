Preparation & submission

  • Work the exercises in order — several ask you to compare against a curve you produced earlier, and the lab draws your earlier reference runs behind the current one.
  • Each exercise lists the figures to hand in under its questions. The lab draws the data, but every word on the figure is yours: the Prepare figure for the report button asks you for the figure title, both axis labels with their units, and a caption, and exports only what you typed. Nothing is filled in for you, and these are marked — a caption that does not state the parameter values behind the run (A_w, A_b, A_g, γ, and which species were switched on) is an incomplete figure. Keep the files in a /Daisyworld/figures folder, named by exercise number (e.g. Fig1_1).
  • You can also export your own recorded points as CSV. The model curve itself is not downloadable — the figures you hand in must rest on data you sampled yourself, so sample properly: at least 12 points, spread across the whole luminosity range, and more closely wherever the curve turns.
  • Every prediction you draw stays on the revealed figure. Your sketches are part of what you hand in, so they must be your own work.
  • This lab is part 1 of 3 of your report. Submit as a single PDF through the Canvas assignment named Daisyworld. This is an individual assignment and a plagiarism check applies — include your own name and "PC-LAB1-Daisyworld" in the file name. Deadline: see the Canvas assignment.

Reference parameter values

  • White-daisy albedo A_w = 0.75, black-daisy albedo A_b = 0.25, bare-ground albedo A_g = 0.50, death rate γ = 0.30, solar constant S = 1000 W m⁻². Selecting an exercise in the lab restores whatever settings that exercise needs, so you never have to reset by hand.
  • The main plot follows the brightening path: one model step per +0.025 in L, with the daisy populations carried over from the previous step, exactly as the spreadsheet does. The populations therefore lag behind their equilibrium — the Equilibrium at fixed L tab shows how far, and the gap is itself worth a comment in E9.

IntroA world of daisies

A cloudless grey planet, seeded with light and dark daisies, orbits a slowly brightening sun. Each exercise runs in three steps: predict what will happen and draw the curve you expect, collect your own data points from the model, then reveal the model curve and compare.

E1Growth rate vs. temperature

Daisies grow at a rate β set by the local temperature Tₗ of their own patch, through eq. (3) of Watson & Lovelock (1983). Open the Growth-curve tab and predict the shape of β(Tₗ) before revealing it.

  1. What does the curve tell you about the relation between daisy growth and temperature?
  2. What is the optimal temperature for growth? Show how you obtain it from eq. (3).
  3. What are the lower and upper temperature limits for growth? Show the algebra — do not just read them off the plot.

Figure: Fig1_1 — growth rate β against local temperature Tₗ

E2A brightening sun

The sun in this model does not stay fixed. Open the Luminosity-vs-time tab and predict how L behaves over the run before revealing it.

  1. What does this steady increase in luminosity represent physically?
  2. At which luminosity L does the absorbed solar energy match present-day Earth, whose solar constant is 1361 W m⁻²?

Figure: Fig2_1 — solar luminosity L against time step t

E3A bare planet

Both daisy populations are switched off, leaving a lifeless planet whose temperature depends only on luminosity and the fixed bare-ground albedo A_g. Predict the shape of Te(L), then sample Te across the luminosity range and build the curve from your own points.

  1. Over which range of luminosity would the planet be habitable for daisies? Use the viable temperature range you derived in E1.
  2. What is the global average temperature Te at L = 1.0?

Figure: Fig3_1 — Te against L for the bare planet

E4Black daisies only

Only black daisies are allowed to grow. Predict how Te will compare with the bare planet of E3, then sample both Te and the black-daisy area a_b across luminosity. Your E3 curve is drawn behind for comparison once you reveal.

  1. How have the temperature conditions changed, e.g. at L = 1.0?
  2. What caused the difference compared with the bare planet (E3)?
  3. At which luminosity does the black-daisy area peak, and what is that peak area?
  4. Compare Te with the local black-daisy temperature Tb — why do they differ?

Figure: Fig4_1 — Te and the black-daisy area a_b against L, with the bare planet of E3 behind

E5White daisies only

Only white daisies are allowed to grow. Predict how Te will compare with the bare planet, then sample Te and the white-daisy area a_w across luminosity.

  1. Compared with E3 and E4, what happens to the temperature?
  2. How does the area covered by daisies change with luminosity?
  3. How do you explain the differences with the bare planet (E3) and the black-daisy world (E4)?

Figure: Fig5_1 — Te and the white-daisy area a_w against L, with the bare planet of E3 behind

E6Both daisies together

Both species now compete for the same bare ground. Predict how Te behaves across the whole luminosity range, then sample it. The bare planet of E3 is drawn behind for comparison once you reveal.

  1. What happens to the global temperature when both black and white daisies are present?
  2. Which conditions are most favourable for black daisies?
  3. How do Tb and Tw differ from Te, and why?

Figure: Fig6_1 — Te, Tw, Tb and both daisy areas against L, with the bare planet of E3 behind

E7Stronger albedo contrast

The two albedos now start further apart: A_w = 0.85 and A_b = 0.15, against the reference 0.75 / 0.25. Repeat the two-species run of E6, then experiment with the albedo sliders. Your E6 reference run is drawn behind for comparison.

  1. What has changed for the temperatures Te, Tw and Tb?
  2. What has changed for the areas of black and white daisies?
  3. How do you explain the difference with E6?

E8A lower death rate

The albedos are back at their reference values and the death rate is lowered from γ = 0.3 to γ = 0.2. Sample the daisy areas across luminosity and compare with your reference two-species run from E6, drawn behind.

  1. What has changed in the daisy areas?
  2. How do you explain the difference?

E9Daisyworld & global change

A synthesis view: the reference two-species run of E6 with the bare planet of E3 drawn behind it. Our own Sun really does brighten with age — by roughly 10% of its present output every 1.1 billion years — while the Earth has stayed habitable for billions of years.

  1. Take the Sun's brightening as ~10% of its present luminosity per 1.1 Gyr, and one model step as ΔL = 0.025. Roughly how much time does one model step represent?
  2. What is the relevance of the Daisyworld model for studies of present-day global change?
