Forcing and feedback

Two words used loosely everywhere else, and precisely here. A forcing is something done to the climate system from outside. A feedback is what the system does about it — and the rest of this lecture is an accounting of the second in response to the first.

The distinction, and why it is not just terminology

Both are “something that changes the temperature”. The difference is whether the change happens to the system or within it.

Forcing — an external impact on a characteristic of the climate system.

  • Its cause lies outside the boundary you have drawn around the system.
  • It is imposed: the system cannot alter it by responding.
  • Example. The Sun dims, the planet cools. Nothing the planet does changes how brightly the Sun shines.

Feedback — a response of the system to a forcing which, in return, changes the effect of that forcing.

  • Its cause lies inside the boundary: it is triggered by the response itself.
  • It is a loop, so it acts on whatever forcing is applied, of whatever kind.
  • Example. Warming raises evaporation; more water vapour traps more infrared; that warms further.
Where you draw the boundary decides which is which. In a study of glacial cycles, ice sheets are a response to orbital forcing and a powerful feedback. In a study of glacial atmospheric circulation, the same ice sheets are a fixed boundary condition whose albedo is a forcing. Neither is wrong. This is exactly the boundary-condition-versus-forcing distinction from Lecture 5, and it is why you must say what you are treating as the system before you say what is forcing it.

Making perturbations comparable

Radiative forcing — a common currency

A volcano, a coal plant and a wheat field perturb the climate through completely unrelated physics. To compare them you need one number they all produce.

The trick is to stop asking “what does this do to the climate?” — which needs a full model — and ask instead “what does this do to the radiative budget, before the climate has had a chance to respond?” That question has an answer you can compute with radiative transfer alone, which is why every perturbation in this lecture gets quoted in the same units.

Radiative forcing ΔQ — the net change in the radiative budget at the tropopause, with stratospheric temperatures allowed to reach a new radiative equilibrium while surface and tropospheric temperatures are held at their unperturbed values
[W m−2], global and annual mean, downward positive
Clause in the definitionWhy it is there
at the tropopause Because the troposphere and surface are tightly coupled by convection and behave as one body. The tropopause is the surface across which that body exchanges energy with everything above it.
stratosphere allowed to adjust The stratosphere re-equilibrates radiatively in about a month; the troposphere takes a decade or more. On the timescale a forcing is meant to describe, stratospheric adjustment has already happened, so it belongs on the forcing side of the ledger rather than the response side.
surface and troposphere fixed This is what makes it a forcing and not a response. Let the surface warm and you have begun to include feedbacks, and the number stops being comparable between agents.
global annual mean Convenient, and reasonable for a well-mixed gas. It is a much weaker description of something concentrated in one season and one region — which is precisely the trouble with aerosols and land use on page 2.
RF and ERF. Recent assessments also quote an effective radiative forcing, in which everything except the global mean surface temperature is allowed to adjust — clouds, water vapour, circulation, the lot. For CO2 the two are nearly identical. For aerosols they are very different, because most of an aerosol's effect runs through clouds, and holding clouds fixed throws it away. Where the two differ, ERF is the better predictor of the eventual temperature change; the bar chart on page 2 shows both.

Question. Set CO2 to 556 ppm — twice pre-industrial — and read the forcing. Now set 1112 ppm, four times pre-industrial. Why is the second doubling worth exactly as much as the first, when it takes twice as much carbon? Because the forcing is logarithmic. The core of the 15 µm band is already saturated, so adding CO2 can only widen its wings, and the width grows like the logarithm of the amount. Each doubling adds about 3.7 W m−2 — the single most reused constant in this lecture. The good news is that the forcing does not run away with concentration; the bad news is that reversing it means halving the excess, not trimming it.

Question. A molecule of CH4 is a far more effective absorber than a molecule of CO2, yet its bar is much shorter. Why? Concentration and band position. Methane is present at about two parts per million against CO2's 420, and its band sits where the atmosphere is nowhere near saturated — which is why the dependence is a square root rather than a logarithm: it grows faster per molecule, from a tiny base. Per kilogram emitted, methane is far worse; per kilogram in the air there is very little of it, and it is gone in a decade. Lecture 4 has the budget that keeps it there.

Reading a feedback

The signal-flow graph, and the one rule for reading it

Every feedback in this lecture is drawn the same way. Learn the convention once here and the eight diagrams that follow take ten seconds each instead of a minute.

  • A node is a variable that can go up or down. An arrow means “influences”.
  • A + on an arrow: an increase at the tail produces an increase at the head.
  • A − on an arrow: an increase at the tail produces a decrease at the head.
  • The sign of the loop is the product of the signs around it. Positive → the loop amplifies the original change. Negative → it damps it.
  • The circled sign in the middle states the answer, so you can check yourself.

Two counting rules worth saying out loud. An even number of minus signs makes a positive loop; an odd number makes a negative one. And “positive” does not mean “good”, nor “negative” “bad” — a positive feedback amplifies cooling exactly as readily as warming. It means destabilising, and nothing more.

Question. Load Snow–ice albedo and flip the sign on the surface albedo → absorbed solar link from − to +. The loop turns negative. What world would that be, and why is it not ours? One in which a brighter surface absorbed more sunlight, which is a contradiction in terms. The exercise is worth doing anyway, because it shows that the sign of a feedback is not a property of the diagram — it is a claim about physics on every single link, and each link can be measured and argued about separately. That is exactly how disagreements about cloud feedback are actually conducted.
