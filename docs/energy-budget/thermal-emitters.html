Four things that glow — and how little of it you can see

A heater element, a wood fire, your own skin and the Sun are all doing the same thing: radiating because they are warm. Temperature alone decides where on the spectrum the energy comes out and how much there is.

Read the three panels together. Going from the heater to the flame raises the temperature by only about 30 %, which barely moves λmax — both stay deep in the infrared. But it multiplies the total power by ~3.5 (Wien is 1/T, Stefan–Boltzmann is T⁴), and it multiplies the visible output by ~50, because the visible band sits far out on the steep short-wavelength tail. That is why a fire looks so much brighter and yellower than a heater that is not much cooler.

And the glow is almost nothing. For the heater, roughly 1 watt in 110 000 is light you can see; for the flame, 1 in 2 000–10 000. The warmth you feel across the room is the other 99.99 % — invisible infrared, sitting in the same part of the chart as the heat radiating off your own skin. Only the Sun, at 5772 K, gets its peak into the visible at all.
