Where the surplus goes

Part 3 of 4 of the global view. The tropics gain 50 W m−2 a year and the poles lose 100, and neither is running away. There are exactly two places the difference can go: into storage, or sideways. This page does both, and finds that which one dominates depends entirely on the timescale you ask about. Goosse et al. (2010) §2.1.5, Figs. 2.15–2.18.

1 · Two terms, and a timescale that picks between them

The budget for a column of the climate system

∂Es/∂t = RTOA − Ftransport rate of change of stored energy = net radiation at the top − net export sideways

Three quantities, and any two of them determine the third. What makes this equation useful is that the two terms on the right dominate on completely different timescales, so you can nearly always neglect one of them — provided you know which.

Days to seasons
storage wins
The ocean charges through summer and discharges through winter. Transport barely changes over a season, so almost the whole radiative swing goes into ∂Es/∂t.
Annual mean and longer
transport wins
Averaged over a year, storage integrates to nearly zero. Whatever the radiation budget does not balance locally must be exported — there is nowhere else for it to go.
Both halves have a modern twist. The annual-mean storage term is not exactly zero today: the planet runs a persistent top-of-atmosphere surplus of roughly +0.6 W m−2, and about 90 % of it is going into the ocean. Global warming is, in the most literal sense, the storage term failing to close. Small as it is, integrated over the ocean's mass it is the dominant fact about the current climate.

2 · Storage: it is all heat capacity

How much energy does it take to warm something by one kelvin?

The heat capacity of a column, per square metre of surface, is C = ρ cp h — density times specific heat times the depth actually involved in the exchange. Two things vary, and they multiply:

Units: ρcp in J m−3 K−1, C in J m−2 K−1. The two numbers to remember from this table are the ratio ocean : land ≈ 100 : 1, and the fact that the entire atmosphere holds less heat than the top 2.5 m of the ocean. Everything about maritime versus continental climate follows from those two facts.

Ask yourself Why is the ocean's depth "50 m" rather than 3800 m?
Answer
Because only the wind-stirred mixed layer participates on seasonal timescales. Below it the thermocline is a strong density barrier and heat crosses it slowly. The relevant depth is set by the timescale you are asking about: about 50 m for the seasonal cycle, a few hundred metres for decades, and the full 3800 m for millennia. This is the same idea as the damping depth in Heat in the ground, applied to a turbulent fluid instead of a conducting solid — and it is why the ocean's response to warming has both a fast component and a very slow one.

3 · The seasonal cycle as a test of heat capacity

Drive a column with a sine wave and see what comes out

Take one column, force it with the seasonal swing in net radiation, and let it shed heat in proportion to how warm it has become. That is two terms:

C · dT/dt = A sin(ωt) − λT ω = 2π per year; A is the seasonal amplitude of the forcing; λ is how strongly the surface sheds heat per kelvin

It has an exact periodic solution, and both parts of it are worth having:

amplitude = A / √(λ² + (Cω)²)   ·   lag = arctan(Cω / λ) a bigger heat capacity does two things at once: it damps the swing and delays it

Compare with the real map. The July-minus-January surface temperature difference exceeds 40 K over the Siberian and central Canadian interiors, and is under 6 K over the mid-latitude North Pacific — at the same latitude. Nothing about the radiation differs between those places to any comparable degree. The whole contrast is the depth of the heat-storage medium underneath. Two orders of magnitude of heat capacity, one order of magnitude of temperature swing.
Ask yourself (from the slides) What causes the large difference between the continental interior and the North Pacific in the July–January map?
Answer
Heat capacity, through two compounding effects. Water has roughly twice the volumetric heat capacity of soil — and, far more importantly, the ocean mixes, so the incoming energy is distributed through 50 m of water instead of concentrating in the top metre of ground. Around a hundredfold more heat capacity, so around a hundredfold less temperature swing for the same forcing. The phase lag comes along with it: coastal stations reach their warmest in August rather than July, and their coldest in February rather than January. There is a second contributor worth naming — evaporation. The ocean can convert absorbed energy into latent heat without warming at all, which land can only do while it still has water.

4 · Transport: integrating the imbalance

The annual-mean picture, straight from Part 2

On the annual mean, storage nearly vanishes and the column budget collapses to RTOA = Ftransport. Integrate the net radiation from the South Pole up to some latitude φ and you have the total energy that must cross that circle of latitude, northward:

F(φ) = 2πa² ∫−π/2φ RTOA(φ′) cosφ′ dφ′ a = 6371 km; the cos φ is the shrinking circumference of a latitude circle

This is not a new measurement. It is the same zonal curve from Part 2, integrated. And that is the point worth making in class: the entire poleward heat transport of the climate system is a derived quantity — nobody measures 5 petawatts crossing 35° N. It is inferred from satellite radiation data by exactly this integral.

Read the closure error, do not hide it. Nothing can be transported across the pole, so F(90°) must be zero. Ours is not, because the tabulated radiation curves average to +2.1 W m−2 rather than to zero — and that, spread over the planet's 5.1 × 1014 m², is the 1.1 PW you see at the pole. Press Residual removed to subtract the global mean before integrating and watch the curve come back to zero. Real observational estimates face exactly this problem: satellite radiation budgets are not accurate enough in absolute terms to close, so they are adjusted the same way, and the size of the adjustment is a fair measure of how well anyone actually knows these numbers.

For scale: 1 PW = 1015 W. Total human primary energy use is about 0.019 PW. The climate system moves roughly three hundred times that across a single circle of latitude, continuously.

5 · Who carries it — atmosphere or ocean?

The split, and why it changes with latitude

The integral gives the total, but not the carrier. Observational estimates split it roughly like this: the ocean dominates in the deep tropics, peaking near 15–20° N at about 1.7 PW; the atmosphere takes over polewards of about 25° and does almost all of the work in mid-latitudes, where the storm tracks carry warm moist air poleward and cold dry air equatorward.

Ask yourself (from the slides) This is the global-mean picture. How does it look for the Atlantic basin alone?
Answer
Quite different, and it is the single most consequential exception in the climate system. In the Pacific and Indian Oceans the heat transport is roughly antisymmetric — poleward in both directions from the equator, as you would expect. The Atlantic transports heat northward at every latitude, even at 30° S, because the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation carries warm surface water north and returns cold deep water south. The Atlantic therefore steals heat from the South Atlantic and delivers it to the North Atlantic, on the order of 1 PW. That is why northern Europe is anomalously warm, why the Atlantic ITCZ sits north of the equator, and why a slowdown of the AMOC is a question about where heat goes rather than how much there is.

6 · The regional signature of transport

Subtract the zonal mean and what is left is circulation

A clean way to see transport in observed temperatures: take the annual mean temperature at a place and subtract the average over its entire circle of latitude. Whatever remains cannot be insolation — every point on that circle receives the same annual sunlight. It is heat capacity and heat transport, and nothing else.

All five of these places sit within four degrees of latitude of one another and receive, to within a percent or two, the same annual sunlight. They span just over 17 kelvin.

Ask yourself (from the slides) What causes the strong positive values over the North Pacific and especially the North Atlantic?
Answer
Three things, working together. (1) Ocean heat transport: the North Atlantic Current delivers warm water far into the Norwegian Sea, and the AMOC keeps it coming. (2) Heat storage: the ocean releases in winter what it stored in summer, so the winter half of the annual mean is enormously milder than it would otherwise be. (3) The comparison itself is unfair — the zonal mean at 60° N is dragged down by Siberia, central Canada and Greenland, which are cold for the mirror-image reason. So the Atlantic anomaly is partly the Atlantic being warm and partly everything else at that latitude being cold. Both halves of that are the same physics seen from two sides.

The sentence to leave with. The radiation budget of Part 2 sets up an imbalance the planet cannot tolerate. Storage buffers it on short timescales, transport removes it on long ones, and the two together are why Earth has a climate rather than just a temperature. What remains is to check that all the arrows really do add up — which is Part 4.
