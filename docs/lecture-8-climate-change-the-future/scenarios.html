Scenarios and the forcing chain

Everything in Lectures 2 to 7 was, in principle, a question with an answer. This lecture is different. The largest single uncertainty about the climate of 2100 is not a gap in the physics — it is that nobody has decided yet. So we stop predicting and start doing something else: we assume a future, and work out what it implies. This page is about what exactly gets assumed, and about the chain that turns an assumption into a temperature.

Why the future needs a scenario

The one thing a climate model cannot compute is the thing that matters most.

A climate model takes a forcing and returns a response. Give it ΔQ and it gives you ΔT — that is exactly the method of Lecture 6. But for the twenty-first century the forcing is dominated by a term whose value depends on what eight billion people choose to do. No equation contains that.

So the community does the only defensible thing available: it states the assumption out loud, and attaches it to every number that follows. A scenario is that stated assumption — a self-consistent story about population, energy, technology and land use, carried through to the emissions it implies. It is not a forecast, it carries no probability, and a projection made under it is a conditional statement: if this, then that.

Question. If scenarios carry no probability, in what sense can a projection be “wrong”? Only in the physics, never in the assumption. A projection is falsified if the climate response to a forcing that did occur turns out to be different from what the model said. It is not falsified because the emissions took a different path — that was never a claim. This distinction is routinely lost in public argument, where “the models were wrong” almost always means “the scenario was not followed”. Testing the physics separately from the scenario is exactly what Lecture 7’s attribution method does, and it is why the historical period is the place to do it.

The chain, and where each link is made

Four links, four different kinds of model, four different kinds of uncertainty. Confusing them is the single commonest error in reading this material.

Read it left to right and notice how the discipline changes at every arrow. The first link is economics and demography; the second is biogeochemistry (Lecture 4); the third is radiative transfer (Lecture 2); the fourth is the whole of Lecture 6. A climate model, strictly speaking, is only the last link.

Two ways to run the chain, and they are not equivalent. A concentration-driven experiment starts at link three: the modelling groups are handed a CO2 concentration and asked to simulate the response. A emission-driven experiment starts at link two: the model is handed emissions and has to work out the concentration itself, using its own carbon cycle. Almost all of the best-known CMIP figures are concentration-driven, because it removes carbon-cycle spread and isolates the physics — but that means the quoted spread understates the true uncertainty. Page 6 returns to this.

RCPs, SSPs, and what the numbers in the names mean

Two generations of scenario, and your lecturer’s figures use both.

The Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs), used through IPCC AR5 and in most of the textbook figures, are named for the total radiative forcing in 2100 in W m−2. RCP8.5 means 8.5 W m−2 — and since a CO2 doubling is 3.7, that is a little over two doublings. They were deliberately built to span the plausible range rather than to be equally likely, and they carry no socio-economic story: several different worlds can produce the same pathway.

The Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs), used in AR6 and CMIP6, fix that. The name has two numbers: SSPx–y, where x identifies one of five narratives about how the world develops and y is again the 2100 forcing. So SSP1–2.6 and SSP2–2.6 reach the same forcing by different routes, and SSP5–8.5 is a fossil-fuelled world, not just a hot one.

Question. RCP8.5 and SSP5–8.5 both reach 8.5 W m−2. Does that mean they give the same climate? Almost, for the global mean — but not regionally. The 8.5 is a total, and it can be assembled from different ingredients. Two pathways with the same net forcing but different aerosol loading have different regional patterns, because aerosol forcing is spatially concentrated near its sources while CO2 forcing is not. This is the same lesson as Lecture 6, page 2: a global-mean W m−2 is a summary, and summaries lose the geography.

The chain, end to end

Choose an emission pathway. The page then runs a real carbon cycle and a real energy balance model to give you a concentration, a forcing and a temperature. Everything below the emission curve is computed here, in your browser, from equations you have already met.

This is not a picture of a projection. It is a projection — a small one, but a real one, with three components you can name. Carbon is carried by the impulse-response model of Joos et al. (2013): a pulse of CO2 released into the atmosphere decays as a sum of four exponentials, one of which never decays at all. Forcing is ΔF = 5.35 ln(C/C0), the formula from Lecture 6, page 1 — where ΔF is the radiative forcing in W m−2, C is the atmospheric CO2 concentration in ppm, C0 is its pre-industrial value of 278 ppm, and 5.35 is the empirical coefficient in W m−2. Temperature comes from the two-layer energy balance model — the same slab-ocean equation as Lecture 6, page 5 and PC Lab 2, with a deep ocean attached underneath so it can store heat.

What this model is and is not. The carbon cycle here is linear: a pulse of CO2 decays the same way whatever the state of the climate. That is a real simplification, and it is the wrong one in exactly one direction — a warmer ocean and a stressed biosphere take up less carbon, so the true concentration for a given emission pathway is higher than this model says. Page 6 is about that missing feedback. The temperature model has no geography, no seasons and no internal variability; page 2 adds the variability back and page 3 adds the geography.
Question. Set the peak year to 2020 and net zero to 2050 — the SSP1–1.9 shape. The temperature curve flattens but does not come down. Emissions are zero. Why is it not cooling? Because zero emissions means zero additional CO2, not zero CO2. Look at the middle panel: the concentration stops rising and then declines only slowly, as the ocean and land keep drawing down the excess. So the forcing stays high, and the system, which was still catching up to it, continues to warm slightly — the committed warming of Lecture 6, page 5. What is remarkable is that the two effects nearly cancel: the declining forcing pulls down almost exactly as hard as the catching-up ocean pushes up. That near-cancellation has a name — the zero-emissions commitment — and it is the reason “net zero stops the warming” is approximately true. Page 7 takes it apart.
Question. Now hold the pathway fixed and drag climate sensitivity from 2.5 to 4.0 — the AR6 likely range. Compare the size of that change with the difference between SSP2–4.5 and SSP5–8.5. They are comparable, and which one dominates depends on when you look. Near the middle of the century the sensitivity range and the scenario range are of similar width; by 2100 the scenario range is clearly larger. That crossover is the whole subject of page 2, where we take the same three sources of spread — internal variability, model physics, scenario — and measure them against each other properly.

The forcing that goes away: aerosols

One of the more uncomfortable results in the whole subject, and it is in the scenario data rather than the climate model.

Every RCP and every SSP has sulphur dioxide emissions falling through the twenty-first century, in some cases steeply. This is not a climate assumption; it is an air quality one. SO2 causes acid rain and kills people through particulate pollution, so every plausible development story cleans it up, and the fossil-fuelled ones clean it up fastest because they can afford to.

But sulphate aerosol is a negative forcing — Lecture 6, page 2 put it near −1.1 W m−2, the single largest uncertainty in the present-day forcing budget. Removing it is a positive forcing. So the cleaner air of the coming decades will, on its own, add warming. Set the non-CO2 slider above to +2.0 in the demonstration and you can see the size of it.

This is not an argument for dirty air. Aerosols have a residence time of days; CO2 has a residence time you can read off the middle panel above. Using one to mask the other means committing to it forever, and stopping for any reason — a recession, a war, a policy — unmasks the full warming within about a decade. The asymmetry of the two lifetimes is the entire reason this is a trap rather than a strategy. It is also, in miniature, the argument against solar geoengineering.

How plausible is a scenario?

Scenarios carry no probability — but they are not therefore all equally worth quoting.

RCP8.5 and SSP5–8.5 were designed as high-end pathways: roughly the 90th percentile of the no-policy literature at the time they were written, with coal use in 2100 several times today’s. They are frequently, and wrongly, labelled “business as usual”. Hausfather and Peters (2020) argued in Nature that this label should be retired: the collapse in the cost of renewables and the policies already enacted have made a coal-dominated 2100 substantially less likely than it looked in 2005.

Where does that leave the range you should have in your head? Current policies, extrapolated, point at roughly 2.5–3 °C by 2100 — near SSP2–4.5. Pledges, if fully delivered, take that down towards 2 °C. That is a considerable achievement compared with the 4 °C world of a decade ago, and nowhere near the stated target. Both halves of that sentence are true, and you should be suspicious of any account that gives you only one of them.

Question. If SSP5–8.5 is unlikely, why does the IPCC still run it? Three reasons, and they are all good ones. First, it is a clean experiment: the signal-to-noise ratio is large, so it is the best scenario for diagnosing a mechanism, which is why most process studies use it. Second, it brackets the possibility of strong carbon-cycle feedbacks — a world with moderate emissions and a badly behaved land sink can end up on a high-forcing pathway without a coal boom. Third, risk is not the same as expectation: a 5 % chance of 5 °C is worth planning for, and you cannot plan for what you have not simulated. The error is not running it. The error is calling it “business as usual”.

Try it yourself. The interactive policy model at En-ROADS (Climate Interactive and MIT Sloan) lets you build a pathway from actual policy levers — carbon price, electrification, deforestation, methane — rather than from the abstract peak-and-decline shape used above. The two models will not agree in detail. Finding out which lever moves your temperature most, and why, is the point.
