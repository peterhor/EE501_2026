Projections, predictions and uncertainty

Two words that are used interchangeably in the newspapers and mean quite different things here. One is an initial-value problem, like a weather forecast; the other is a boundary-value problem, like asking what a room will settle to when you change the thermostat. They fail for different reasons, they are verified differently, and the crossover between them — somewhere around a decade — is where most of the confusion about climate models lives.

The distinction, and why it is not pedantry

The same model, the same scenario, run two different ways, answering two different questions.

A projection asks: what is the response to the forcing? The goal is the forced part of the signal and nothing else. Members are initialised from a long equilibrium control run, so each carries an arbitrary phase of internal variability, and the ensemble mean averages that phase away. This is a boundary-condition problem: what matters is the forcing applied at the boundary, not where you started.

Verification is statistical. You never ask whether the model put a cold decade in the right place, because it never claimed to — the point Lecture 7, page 1 made about ensembles.

A prediction asks for more: the response to the forcing and the part of internal variability that is currently predictable. Members are initialised from observations — in practice from an ocean state assimilated from Argo floats and satellite data — so they all start in the phase the real world is actually in. This is a mixed initial- and boundary-condition problem.

Verification is date-by-date, like a weather forecast, and it can fail. It usually does, after about a decade.

Why the ocean is what you initialise. The atmosphere forgets its initial state in about two weeks — that is the Lorenz result from Lecture 7. There would be no such thing as a decadal prediction if the atmosphere were all there was. What makes a few years of skill possible is that the ocean has a memory measured in years to decades: heat anomalies in the subpolar North Atlantic, the phase of the AMO, the state of the Pacific. Predict those and you predict a slice of the surface temperature that goes with them.

Question. A decadal prediction system is verified against observations for 1961–2010 and beats the uninitialised runs for lead times up to five years. Does that make it a better climate model? No — it is the same model, doing a different job. Beating the uninitialised runs at short lead times demonstrates that initialisation adds information, which is a statement about the predictability of internal variability, not about the model’s response to forcing. A model can have excellent decadal skill and a badly wrong climate sensitivity, or vice versa. The two are verified against different things, which is precisely why the vocabulary matters.

Where predictability goes

One realisation of a “real world”. Two ensembles chasing it — one told where the ocean started, one not. Watch the advantage disappear.

Internal variability here has two parts, because the real one does: a fast component with a memory of about a year, standing for the atmosphere and the weather, and a slow component with a memory of about a decade, standing for the ocean. Only the slow part is worth initialising. Drag the initialisation quality from 0 to 1 and you move continuously from a projection to a prediction — they are the two ends of one dial, not two different things.

Question. Set the initialisation quality to 0. The skill line does not go to zero; it settles at some positive value. What is left? The forced response. Even with no knowledge whatever of the initial state, an ensemble that has the right forcing correlates with the observations, because both are warming. At long lead times all of the skill is of this kind — which is the formal statement of what a projection is. Notice also what this implies for verification: a decadal prediction system must be judged against the uninitialised runs, not against zero, or it takes credit for the greenhouse effect.

The demonstration this page is built around

Three sources of uncertainty, measured against each other

Internal variability, model spread and scenario choice. Their relative importance changes with lead time, with the region, and with the question — and almost every argument about climate projections is really an argument about which of the three is in charge.

The decomposition is Hawkins and Sutton (2009), and the arithmetic is simple enough to do here in full. Take the projected warming at some future year, and ask where its spread comes from.

Vtotal(t)  =  Vinternal  +  Vmodel(t)  +  Vscenario(t)
Vinternal = σ2 of the averaging period  ·  Vmodel = (fm · mean response)2  ·  Vscenario = variance of the responses across scenarios
Vtotal(t) total variance of the projected warming at lead time t, K2
Vinternal variance from internal variability — σ2 of the averaging period; it does not grow with lead time
Vmodel(t) variance from model disagreement, (fm · mean response)2
fm fractional spread across models (dimensionless)
Vscenario(t) variance of the responses across emission scenarios, K2
t lead time — how far into the future the projection reaches

Each term behaves differently with lead time and that is the whole story. Internal variability does not grow at all — it is a property of the system, the same in 2030 as in 2090. Model uncertainty grows in proportion to the response, because models disagree by a roughly fixed fraction. Scenario uncertainty starts at zero — every pathway has the same emissions today — and grows fastest of all, because the pathways fan apart.

Question. Click One grid box, then set the averaging period to 1 year, and read the 2035 panel. Now go back to Global, annual with a 20-year mean. Why is the answer so different, when it is the same climate and the same models? Because internal variability is the only term that depends on how much you average, and it is enormous for a small area over a short time. This is the signal-to-noise scaling of Lecture 7, page 1, arriving from the other direction: there the question was how long before you can detect a trend; here it is how much of your projected spread is noise you can never remove. Same σ, same √n, opposite use. And the practical consequence is uncomfortable: for a local decision on a 15-year horizon, choosing a better model or a better scenario buys you almost nothing.
Question. Click Policy already decided. Scenario uncertainty vanishes entirely. Is that cheating? No — it is the correct calculation for a different question. Scenario uncertainty is not a property of the climate system at all; it is a statement about what you have not yet decided. If you are designing a drainage system for a city that has committed to a pathway, or if you are asking a conditional question (“given SSP2–4.5, what happens?”), then the scenario term is exactly zero and only physics remains. What is not legitimate is quoting the narrow range that results as though it were the uncertainty in the future climate.

What you do with a model you do not fully trust

The practical end of Lecture 5, now that we are asking models for numbers people will act on.

  • Use anomalies, not absolutes. A model whose Norwegian winter is 3 K too cold can still get the change right, because the bias largely cancels. Almost every figure in this lecture is a difference between two periods for exactly this reason.
  • Use the multi-model mean, but know why it works. It usually beats any individual model, because errors that are independent between models partially cancel. It does not help against errors they share — and shared errors are common, since models share parameterisations, ancestry and even code. An ensemble of opportunity is not a random sample.
  • Weight by performance only with care. A model that reproduces today’s climate well is not guaranteed to project well; it may be right for the wrong reasons, or tuned to be right. The emergent-constraint literature tries to find observable quantities that actually correlate with the response, and it is harder than it looks.
  • Bias-correct with your eyes open. Adjusting model output to match observed statistics (Lecture 5, page 5) makes output usable for impact models, and it silently assumes the bias is stationary under climate change. It often is not.
  • Report the spread, always. A single number from a single model is not a projection. It is an anecdote.
And the limit. None of these techniques removes the first band in the figure above. For a small region on a short horizon, the irreducible part of the uncertainty is internal variability, and no improvement in models or in policy clarity will touch it. Saying so is not defeatism; it is the difference between a usable projection and a misleading one.
