The geography of warming

Nobody lives in the global mean. The maps of projected temperature change have two features so robust that they appear in every model, in every scenario, and in the observations already: land warms more than ocean, by about half again, and the Arctic warms more than everywhere, by a factor of two to four. Neither is a modelling artefact. Both follow from physics you can do on this page.

Two patterns, and a warning about how to read the maps

The multi-model mean maps in your lecturer’s slides carry hatching and stippling. Those marks are not decoration and they are the first thing to look at.

A projected change map shows the multi-model mean warming over some future period relative to a reference. The conventional marking, used in the IPCC figures, is:

  • Stippling — the change is larger than twice the standard deviation of internal variability and at least 90 % of models agree on its sign. A robust signal.
  • Hatching — the change is smaller than one standard deviation of internal variability. In other words: whatever the colour says, it is within the noise.
  • Neither — somewhere in between; a real signal, but not one you would bet on locally.

That is exactly the signal-to-noise arithmetic of Lecture 7, page 1 and the first band of the uncertainty partition on page 2, applied grid box by grid box. On the temperature maps almost nothing is hatched, because the warming signal is large compared with temperature variability. On the precipitation maps, huge areas are hatched. Comparing the two figures side by side teaches more than either alone.

Question. Two maps of the same quantity, RCP2.6 and RCP8.5. The RCP2.6 map has far more hatching. Are the RCP2.6 models less reliable? No. Internal variability is the same in both — it is a property of the climate system, not of the scenario. What differs is the signal: RCP2.6 has a smaller forced change, so more of the map falls below the one-standard-deviation bar. The hatching is telling you the warming is small, which is the good news, not that the model is bad. This is the same confusion the fractional-variance plot on page 2 invites, and it is worth naming out loud.

Why land warms faster than ocean

Not thermal inertia. The land–sea contrast is about 1.5 in equilibrium too, when there is no inertia left to appeal to. The real answer is about moisture, and it is a calculation you can do in one line.

The wrong explanation first, because everyone reaches for it. Land has a small heat capacity, ocean has a large one, so land responds faster. That is true, and it explains part of the contrast in a transient run. But run the same model to equilibrium, wait a thousand years until nothing is lagging anything, and the contrast is still there, still around 1.5. Heat capacity cannot explain a steady state.

The right explanation. Over the deep tropics and much of the mid-latitudes the free troposphere is close to horizontally uniform — the atmosphere cannot sustain large temperature gradients aloft, because they get flattened out by waves and by the overturning circulation. So a warming aloft is shared. But the surface is connected to the free troposphere by moist convection and boundary-layer mixing, which carry moist static energy, not temperature:

h  =  cpT  +  Lvq  +  gz     →     Δhland  =  Δhocean
with q = RH · qs(T) and RH held fixed, this gives    ΔTland / ΔTocean = [cp + Lv·RHo·(dqs/dT)o] ÷ [cp + Lv·RHl·(dqs/dT)l]

Read the formula before you touch the sliders. Warming a moist surface takes a lot of energy because most of it goes into evaporation rather than into temperature. Warming a dry surface takes less, because there is less water to evaporate. If the two surfaces must share the same energy increase, the dry one ends up hotter. Land is dry. That is the whole argument.

Question. Set both relative humidities to 80 % and both temperatures to 25 °C. The ratio goes to exactly 1. Now raise only the ocean temperature to 30 °C. The ratio goes above 1 again, with identical humidities. Where did that come from? From the curvature of the Clausius–Clapeyron relation. qs grows exponentially with temperature, so dqs/dT is itself larger at higher temperature. A warmer surface therefore spends even more of any energy increase on evaporation, and warms even less. So there are two ingredients in the land–sea contrast: land is drier, and land — away from the tropics — is often colder. Both push the same way. You will meet that exponential again on page 4, where it is the central quantity.
Question. The contrast is largest in dry mid-latitude continental interiors and in regions where soil moisture is projected to fall. Connect that to Lecture 3. Through the Bowen ratio. A surface with plenty of water partitions its available energy into latent heat — a low Bowen ratio — and stays cool. Dry out the soil and the same net radiation has nowhere to go but sensible heat, the Bowen ratio rises, and the surface heats. Here that shows up as a lower effective RH over land, and it is a feedback: warming dries the soil, drying raises the contrast, the higher contrast warms the land further. This is why projected summer warming over southern Europe is so much larger than the global mean, and why page 4 and page 5 keep coming back to soil moisture.

Why the Arctic warms fastest

Ask a class why, and everyone says ice–albedo. It is a real contributor, and it is not the largest one.

Start with a piece of arithmetic that has nothing to do with ice at all. A surface at temperature T sheds energy at σT4, so the extra radiation released per extra kelvin is 4σT3 — the Planck feedback of Lecture 6, page 3. That quantity is smaller where it is colder. A cold place is worse at getting rid of extra energy, so the same forcing warms it more — before any feedback is invoked.

Now add the rest, roughly in order of how much they contribute. The ordering below follows the feedback decomposition of Pithan and Mauritsen (2014), and the first entry surprises most people.

MechanismHow it worksSign and size
Lapse-rate feedback The Arctic atmosphere is strongly stably stratified in winter: a temperature inversion, with warm air over cold surface. Extra energy added near the surface stays near the surface instead of being convected aloft and radiated away. In the tropics the opposite happens — warming is amplified aloft and radiates efficiently, a negative feedback. The largest single contributor to the contrast between polar and tropical warming.
Planck (temperature) feedback The 4σT3 argument above. Weaker damping where it is cold. Second largest. Computed in the panel above.
Surface albedo Sea ice and snow retreat, exposing dark ocean and ground. The classic ice–albedo feedback of PC Lab 2 and Lecture 6, page 4. Third. Important, seasonal, and it is not the whole story.
Ocean heat uptake and mixing Where sea ice retreats, the ocean gains a lid in winter it did not have: heat absorbed in summer is released to the atmosphere in autumn instead of being insulated from it. Autumn is when Arctic amplification is largest. Positive, strongly seasonal.
Poleward energy transport A moister atmosphere carries more latent heat poleward even if the mass circulation does not change. Dry static transport tends to weaken as the equator-to-pole gradient relaxes; moist transport strengthens. Net positive, partly self-cancelling.
Clouds More low cloud over open water in autumn and winter traps longwave radiation with little compensating shortwave effect, because there is no sun. Positive but uncertain, as usual.
Antarctica is different, and the difference is instructive. The Southern Ocean warms slowly and Antarctic sea ice has not retreated the way Arctic sea ice has. The reason is not that the physics differs — it is that the Southern Ocean has a deep mixed layer and upwells cold water from below, so heat added at the surface is carried away downward instead of accumulating. Polar amplification is an Arctic phenomenon on the timescale of a century; the Southern Hemisphere version arrives later. Two hemispheres, same physics, different ocean.
Question. If the lapse-rate feedback is the largest term, why do the popular explanations always say ice–albedo? Partly because it is visible: you can photograph the ice going and you cannot photograph a temperature inversion. Partly because it was identified first, back in the energy-balance-model work of the 1960s. And partly because the decomposition is recent and subtle — the lapse-rate and Planck feedbacks are “temperature” feedbacks and are easily mistaken for part of the baseline response rather than as terms that vary with latitude. The practical consequence matters: a model can get Arctic amplification approximately right with too little sea-ice loss, if its polar inversion is too strong. Getting the number right is not the same as getting the mechanism right.

Pattern scaling: what you can and cannot get away with

The standard approximation behind most regional impact studies, and the reason it usually works.

To a surprisingly good approximation, the shape of the warming pattern does not depend on the scenario — only its amplitude does. Write it as ΔT(x, t) ≈ P(x) · ΔTglobal(t) — where ΔT(x, t) is the warming at location x and time t in K, ΔTglobal(t) is the global-mean warming at that time in K, and P(x) is a fixed map of local warming per degree of global warming, dimensionless. Then one map plus one global time series gives you every scenario and every year, which is why so much of the impacts literature works this way, and why an assessment can talk about “a 1.5 °C world” and “a 2 °C world” without naming a scenario.

The land–sea contrast and the polar amplification you have just derived are P, in summary form: P ≈ 1.4–1.6 over land, 0.8–0.9 over ocean, 2–4 in the Arctic. The derivations above explain why P should be roughly scenario-independent: neither argument mentions the forcing at all.

Where it breaks. Wherever the response is not proportional to the forcing.

  • Aerosols. Regionally concentrated and on a different time course from CO2, so scenarios with different aerosol paths have measurably different patterns.
  • Anything that can run out. Sea ice that has already gone cannot retreat further, so Arctic amplification saturates in high scenarios. Soil moisture likewise.
  • Circulation shifts and thresholds. An AMOC weakening (page 7) produces a North Atlantic cold patch that is not proportional to anything.
  • Precipitation. Pattern scaling is much less reliable for precipitation than for temperature, because the dynamic part of the response — page 4 — does not scale.
