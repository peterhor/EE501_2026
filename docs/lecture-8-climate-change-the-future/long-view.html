Commitment, sea level and abrupt change

The year 2100 is an arbitrary place to stop, and stopping there systematically flatters the situation. Surface temperature stabilises at net zero; almost nothing else does. This last page runs the clock forward — centuries, then millennia, then longer — and then asks the question the whole course has been circling: are there thresholds, and how would we know before we crossed one?

Four clocks, running at once

Each part of the system has its own response time, and they differ by five orders of magnitude. Almost every misunderstanding about the long-term future comes from applying one clock’s intuition to another’s process.

TimescaleWhat is still adjustingConsequence
DecadesThe atmosphere and the upper ocean. Surface temperature reaches most of its transient response. This is what a projection to 2100 shows you, and it is the smallest part of the story.
CenturiesThe deep ocean absorbs heat and CO2; the ocean equilibrates with the atmosphere. Thermal expansion continues; CO2 falls to roughly a quarter of the pulse; glaciers finish responding.
MillenniaCarbonate compensation — sea-floor CaCO3 dissolves, restoring the ocean’s buffering capacity. Ice sheets adjust. CO2 falls further, towards about a tenth of the pulse. Greenland and West Antarctica respond fully.
105 yearsSilicate weathering of continental rock — the ultimate CO2 thermostat of Lecture 4, page 4. The last of the anthropogenic carbon is removed. Longer than the whole of human history by an order of magnitude.

The two that matter for the twenty-first century decision are the first two. But the third and fourth are what makes the emission of fossil carbon different in kind from other kinds of pollution: the sulphate aerosol of page 1 is gone in a week, and a meaningful fraction of the CO2 emitted this decade is still in the air when the next glacial cycle should have started.

Question. Switch between the two carbon models. The Joos pulse response, which page 1 and page 6 both use, flattens out and never comes down. Is that a bug? No — it is a fit being used outside its range, which is a different and more interesting failure. The Joos impulse response was calibrated against models run for 1000 years, and over that window a constant term is an excellent representation of everything slower. Extrapolate it to 105 years and that constant term becomes a claim that 22 % of the carbon stays in the air forever, which is wrong: carbonate compensation removes most of it over five to ten thousand years and silicate weathering finishes the job over hundreds of thousands. The second model splits the constant term into those two processes.

The general lesson is worth more than the specific one. Every parameterisation has a range of validity, and it will not tell you when you have left it — it will just keep returning numbers. That is the same warning as Lecture 5, page 1 gave about parameterisation in general, and it is worth being suspicious of every extrapolated curve you meet, including the ones on this site.
Question. Set the pulse to 5000 PgC — roughly the total conventional fossil fuel resource. Compare the shape of the curve with the 1000 PgC case. The fractional curve is identical, because this model is linear. In a real carbon cycle it is not: a bigger pulse acidifies the ocean more, consumes more of the carbonate buffer, and therefore leaves a larger fraction airborne for longer. Archer and Brovkin’s CLIMBER-2 calculation, which is the source of the textbook figure, has exactly that non-linearity, and it is why the 5000 PgC case there has a fatter tail rather than just a taller one. Our model gets the timescales approximately right and the size dependence wrong — and it is worth knowing which of those two you are reading off a figure.

Sea level: the slowest thing that matters, and the least reversible

Four contributions with four different physics, and only one of them is fast.

Thermal expansion is the one you can calculate. Sea water expands as it warms, and the ocean’s heat uptake is exactly the deep-ocean term of the two-layer model used on pages 1 and 6. The conversion is one empirical number — the expansion efficiency of heat, about 0.12 metres of sea level per 1024 joules stored.

Because the deep ocean takes a thousand years to fill up, thermal expansion continues long after the surface has stabilised. It is the clearest case of a commitment: it does not care what you do next, only what heat is already in the pipeline.

Glaciers outside the ice sheets respond within a century and contain about 0.3 m of sea level in total — so this term is large now and finite; it runs out. Greenland holds about 7 m and responds over millennia, mainly through surface melting. Antarctica holds about 58 m and responds mainly through the ocean melting its floating shelves; the West Antarctic marine sectors, worth 3–4 m, are the part with a plausible threshold, and are the subject of the second demonstration below.

Read the shape of that figure, not just the bars. The scenario separation by 2100 is modest — roughly 0.4 m between the lowest and highest pathway, against a range within each scenario that is nearly as wide. Sea level is slow, so a century is not long enough for policy to make much difference to it. Extend to 2300 and the separation becomes enormous: a few tenths of a metre to three metres under SSP1–2.6, against nearly two to seven metres under SSP5–8.5. The twenty-first century is where the commitment is made; the following centuries are where it is paid.

The commitment, stated as one number. AR6 assesses roughly 2–3 metres of sea level rise per degree of warming, sustained over 2000 years. That is a useful thing to carry in your head, because it converts the abstract targets of page 6 into something concrete: the difference between 1.5 and 2 °C is roughly a metre of eventual sea level, and the difference between 2 and 4 °C is five or six.

Question. Suppose emissions go net-negative and CO2 is brought back to pre-industrial levels over the next two centuries. Does the sea come back down? Essentially no, on any timescale you care about. Thermal expansion reverses only as fast as the deep ocean can shed heat, which is slower than it absorbed it. Ice sheets are worse: melting lowers the surface into warmer air and exposes bedrock with a lower albedo, so re-growing them requires a colder climate than the one that melted them, not just the same one. That is hysteresis, and it is the same phenomenon — and very nearly the same phase diagram — you met with the ocean circulation in Lecture 7, page 4 and with Saharan vegetation in PC Lab 3. Sea level rise is, for practical purposes, a one-way door.

The ocean circulation: what we know, and what we would need to know

You have already built the model for this. This card connects it to the projections rather than rebuilding it.

The AMOC is driven by the sinking of cold, salty water in the North Atlantic. Warm it or freshen it and the sinking weakens; a weaker circulation carries less salt northward, so it freshens further — the positive feedback that gives the Stommel two-box model of Lecture 7, page 4 two stable states and a threshold between them. Go back and use that demonstration now; nothing about the mathematics changes here. What changes is where the freshwater comes from. In the palaeo case it was a collapsing ice sheet. In the future case it is us.

So the useful question is quantitative: how much freshwater does warming actually deliver to the North Atlantic, compared with the amounts that move the Stommel model? That is a calculation you can do with the numbers from page 4.

What the assessments actually say. AR6 concludes that the AMOC will very likely decline over the twenty-first century — the CMIP models give something like a third of its strength by 2100 under a high scenario — but has only medium confidence that there will be no abrupt collapse before 2100. Those two statements are not in tension: a large, gradual, reversible weakening and a permanent threshold crossing are different events, and the models are much more confident about the first.

And the uncertainty, which is unusual and worth naming. Palaeo records show the real AMOC has crossed thresholds repeatedly — the Dansgaard–Oeschger events of Lecture 7. Yet coupled models are notably reluctant to collapse, and there is a known reason to distrust them: most simulate an Atlantic that is too stable, because they get the salinity transport at the southern boundary of the basin with the wrong sign compared with observations. So we have a case where the models agree with each other and there is a specific, diagnosable reason to think they may all be biased the same way. That is a much more uncomfortable position than a wide model spread, and it is why this remains one of the genuinely open questions.

The demonstration this lecture ends on

Marine ice sheet instability

A threshold you can locate on a map, produced by geometry alone. West Antarctica sits on bedrock that is below sea level and deepening inland — and that single fact is enough.

Where a grounded ice sheet meets the ocean, the ice starts to float. The thickness at that point — the grounding line — is fixed by flotation: deeper water means thicker ice. And the rate at which ice flows out through the grounding line rises very steeply with that thickness:

Now the argument. The ice sheet is in balance when the outflow equals the snow accumulated over the grounded area upstream. Push the grounding line back a little. If it retreats into deeper water, the ice there is thicker, so more ice flows out, so it retreats further. Nothing stops it until the bed shallows again. Retreat on a bed that deepens inland is self-sustaining — no additional warming required after the first push.

Question. Drag buttressing down towards 0.6. At some point the two outer equilibria merge and vanish, and only the far-interior one is left. What kind of event is that, and where have you seen it before? A saddle-node bifurcation — a stable and an unstable equilibrium colliding and annihilating, after which the system has no choice but to move to the remaining state. It is exactly what happens to the strong branch of the Stommel model when the freshwater flux passes its critical value, and exactly what happens to the vegetated state in PC Lab 3 when the precipitation feedback is strong enough. Three completely different systems — an ocean, a desert, an ice sheet — and one piece of mathematics. That recurrence is the most useful single idea in this course.

Note also the warning it contains. Right up until the bifurcation, the ice sheet is in a stable equilibrium and looks fine. The margin shrinks silently. There is no observable that says “approaching threshold” except the shrinking margin itself, and measuring that requires knowing the bed geometry — which is why so much effort goes into radar mapping of what is underneath Antarctica.
Question. The demonstration has no warming in it at all. Where does climate change enter? Through the buttressing slider, and the route matters. The dominant mechanism in West Antarctica is not warm air melting the ice from above — the air there stays far below freezing. It is warm ocean water reaching the underside of the floating ice shelves and thinning them from below. A thinner shelf pushes back less against the grounded ice behind it, the outflow increases, and the grounding line retreats. So the chain is: circulation change → warm water on the continental shelf → basal melt → loss of buttressing → grounding-line retreat → and then the geometry takes over and the climate is no longer needed. That last step is what makes it a tipping element rather than a response.

The tipping elements, and what the phrase should and should not mean

A closing inventory, with the assessed confidence attached to each.

ElementMechanismTimescale & confidence
West Antarctic ice sheet Marine ice sheet instability — the demonstration above. Retrograde bed, self-sustaining retreat. Centuries to millennia. Physically well understood; whether the threshold has already been passed in the Amundsen Sea sector is genuinely debated.
Greenland ice sheet Melt–elevation feedback: a lower surface sits in warmer air and melts faster. Millennia. Threshold plausibly between 1 and 3 °C; the response is slow enough that a temporary overshoot may be survivable.
AMOC Salt-advection feedback — the Stommel mechanism. Decades to a century once triggered. Threshold location poorly known; models may be biased stable.
Permafrost carbon Thaw releases CO2 and CH4, which warms, which thaws. Lecture 6, page 6. Centuries. Now thought to be a strong feedback rather than a sharp threshold — large, gradual, and irreversible on human timescales.
Amazon forest Forest recycles its own rainfall; lose enough of it and the remainder cannot sustain the hydrological cycle. The Sahara mechanism of PC Lab 3, with the sign reversed. Decades. Compounded by deforestation, which supplies the push independently of climate.
Low cloud decks A speculative break-up of subtropical stratocumulus at very high CO2, worth several kelvin if real. Found in one high-resolution simulation. Low confidence, listed here because it is a fair example of a possibility we cannot currently exclude or confirm.
Two misuses to resist. The first is treating “tipping point” as a synonym for “bad”. Most of the damage in most scenarios comes from the smooth, boring, well-understood response — the distribution shift of page 5 — not from any threshold. The second is the implied global cliff edge: there is no single date after which everything is lost. Each element has its own threshold, its own timescale, and its own uncertainty, and several of them take a thousand years to complete even once triggered. The framing that survives scrutiny is that risk rises with warming, that some of it is irreversible, and that irreversible is not the same as sudden. Overstating this material is not harmless: it is the fastest way to lose an audience that can check.

What to take from this lecture

Seven pages, in seven sentences.

  • A projection is conditional. It answers “if this forcing, then what?” A prediction also claims to know the phase of internal variability, and that claim expires after about a decade.
  • The scenario is an assumption, not a forecast — and which uncertainty dominates depends on the region, the averaging period and the lead time, not on how good the models are.
  • Two patterns are robust: land warms about 1.5 times as fast as ocean, for reasons of moisture, and the Arctic two to four times, mostly for reasons of lapse rate and Planck response rather than albedo.
  • Moisture rises at 7 %/K and precipitation at 1–3 %/K; the gap weakens the circulation, sharpens the wet–dry contrast, and makes heavy rainfall intensify far faster than mean rainfall.
  • The tails move much more than the mean. A shifted distribution and nothing else reproduces the assessed increase in hot extremes almost exactly.
  • Warming is proportional to cumulative emissions, which converts any temperature target into a finite quantity of carbon — a few hundred gigatonnes for 1.5 °C, less than a decade at current rates.
  • Surface temperature stabilises at net zero; sea level and the ice sheets do not. Several parts of the system have thresholds, they are the same mathematics as the ocean circulation and the Sahara, and crossing one is irreversible without necessarily being sudden.
