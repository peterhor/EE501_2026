The water cycle response

A warmer atmosphere holds about 7 % more water vapour per kelvin. It does not rain 7 % more per kelvin — it rains one to three per cent more. Those two numbers are both right, they are both derivable in a few lines, and the gap between them drives almost everything else on this page: the weakening of the tropical circulation, the widening gap between wet and dry places, and the fact that extreme rainfall intensifies much faster than mean rainfall.

Two constraints, pulling in different directions

One thermodynamic, one energetic. Neither is optional, and they do not agree.

The supply side is thermodynamic. How much water vapour the air can hold is set by the Clausius–Clapeyron relation, which is a statement about the latent heat of vaporisation and nothing else:

1/es · des/dT  =  Lv / (RvT2)
≈ 7 % per kelvin near 288 K
es saturation vapour pressure, Pa
T absolute temperature, K
Lv latent heat of vaporisation of water, 2.5×106 J kg−1
Rv specific gas constant for water vapour, 461.5 J kg−1 K−1

Relative humidity stays roughly constant as the climate warms — a robust result across models and observations — so the actual water content follows the saturation value. That is also why water vapour is the largest positive feedback in Lecture 6, page 4. The same exponential, used twice.

The demand side is energetic. Every kilogram of water that condenses releases latent heat into the atmosphere. For the atmosphere to be in steady state, it must get rid of that heat — and its only route is radiation to space and to the surface. So global mean precipitation is limited by the atmosphere’s radiative cooling:

LvP  =  Ratm  +  SH
80 W m−2 of latent heat, from the global energy budget
Lv latent heat of vaporisation, 2.5×106 J kg−1
P global-mean precipitation rate, kg m−2 s−1
Ratm net radiative cooling of the atmosphere, W m−2
SH sensible heat flux from the surface into the atmosphere, W m−2

You already assembled that ledger in Lecture 2, page 7: about 80 W m−2 of latent heat flux, 20 of sensible. The atmosphere cannot cool radiatively much faster just because there is more water available, so precipitation is rationed by the energy budget, not by the moisture supply.

Question. Which of the two constraints controls global evaporation? The same one — the energetic one. Globally and in steady state, evaporation must equal precipitation, so the surface latent heat flux is pinned to the same 80 W m−2. This is worth pausing on, because the intuition that “a warmer world evaporates more” is only weakly true globally. Locally it can be very true: over a particular land surface the evaporative demand rises steeply with temperature, limited only by how much water there is. The global constraint and the local one are different questions, exactly as in Lecture 3.

The two rates, and the circulation caught between them

Both rates are computed here from the equations above. The third number — what happens to the atmospheric overturning — is simply their difference, and it is a genuine prediction.

Think of the tropical atmosphere as a machine that lifts moist air, rains out its water, and returns dry. The rainfall it produces is roughly mass flux × moisture content. If the moisture goes up at 7 %/K and the rainfall is only allowed to go up at 2 %/K, then the mass flux has to come down. There is no way around it:

Question. Set the forcing to 0 and read the precipitation rate. Now set it back to 3.7. The precipitation rate falls by roughly half. What just happened, given that the temperature has not changed? The fast atmospheric adjustment. CO2 does not only trap outgoing radiation at the top of the atmosphere; it also makes the atmosphere itself a less efficient radiator, because it absorbs some of the upwelling longwave that would otherwise have escaped. That directly reduces Ratm, and therefore reduces precipitation — immediately, before any warming occurs at all. So the precipitation response has two pieces with opposite signs: a fast suppression proportional to the forcing, and a slow increase proportional to the warming. This is the Allen and Ingram (2002) decomposition, and it is why the observed and modelled rate lands near 2 %/K rather than the 2.7 %/K the temperature term alone would give. A solar forcing, which does not do this, gives a larger precipitation increase for the same warming — a point that matters a great deal for solar geoengineering.
Question. Drag the base temperature down to −10 °C. The Clausius–Clapeyron rate goes above 8 %/K. Does that mean the Arctic water cycle intensifies fastest? In fractional terms, yes — and it is one reason high-latitude precipitation is projected to rise so much. But be careful about what a percentage is a percentage of. Cold air holds very little water to begin with, so a large fractional increase is a small absolute one. The Arctic gets a big relative change and a small number of millimetres; the tropics get a small relative change and a large number of millimetres. Which matters depends entirely on what you are asking — and quoting the percentage without the baseline is one of the standard ways to mislead with a true number.

Wet gets wetter, dry gets drier — and what that sentence actually claims

The most quoted phrase in this part of the subject, and the most often overstated.

Consider the moisture budget of a column: the change in P − E is what the atmospheric circulation delivers or removes. If the circulation is unchanged and only the moisture content rises, then the existing convergence patterns simply carry more water: places where P > E get more surplus, places where E > P get more deficit, and both scale by the same Clausius–Clapeyron factor.

δ(P − E)  ≈  α ΔT · (P − E)
with α ≈ 0.07 K−1 — the existing pattern, amplified
P − E precipitation minus evaporation — the net moisture the circulation delivers, mm day−1
δ(P − E) its change under warming, mm day−1
α Clausius–Clapeyron scaling rate, ≈0.07 K−1
ΔT global-mean warming, K
K. The shape is fixed; only its amplitude changes.
Three caveats, and they matter more than the slogan. First, this is a statement about P − E, not about P. A subtropical region can get drier in P − E while its rainfall stays the same, because evaporative demand rose. Second, it holds far better over ocean than over land. Over land, E is limited by how much water is actually there — the bucket of Lecture 4, page 1 — so the scaling breaks down exactly where people live. Third, it assumes the circulation does not move. It does move, and that is the next card.

The circulation moves, and where it moves to decides who gets rain

Textbook Fig. 6.16 in three parts. This is the dynamic contribution, and it is where the model spread lives.

  • More moisture in the same circulation — the thermodynamic term of the previous card. It intensifies the existing wet and dry belts. Robust across models.
  • A weaker overturning — the number your demonstration computed above, a few per cent per kelvin. It reduces both the tropical rainfall maximum and the subtropical drying, partly cancelling the first term. Robust in sign, uncertain in size, and larger for the Walker circulation across the Pacific than for the Hadley cells.
  • A poleward shift — the descending branch of the Hadley cell and the mid-latitude storm tracks move poleward by roughly a degree of latitude per degree of warming. Where that happens, the change in rainfall is enormous and it is a displacement, not an intensification: the Mediterranean and southern Australia are projected to dry substantially because the subtropical dry zone arrives on top of them.

The three terms have different reliability, and that difference is why the precipitation maps are so heavily hatched compared with the temperature maps (page 3). The thermodynamic term is essentially Clausius–Clapeyron and every model agrees. The dynamic terms depend on the details of the circulation response, which is where models disagree most, and in the mid-latitudes the dynamic term often dominates. Temperature projections are mostly thermodynamics; precipitation projections are mostly dynamics. That one sentence explains most of the difference in confidence between them.

Question. Southern Norway. A model says winter precipitation rises 15 % by 2100. What are the two mechanisms, and which would you trust? Thermodynamic: the North Atlantic storms arriving are carrying more water, roughly 7 %/K, and that part is as reliable as anything in the subject. Dynamic: the storm track itself may shift, changing how many storms arrive and where they land — and that part is much less certain, and differs between models. The honest summary for a stakeholder is that the sign is confident (wetter winters), the magnitude less so, and the seasonal and local detail least of all. Add the internal variability from page 2 — northern European winter has a very large σ — and a 15 % change by 2100 is a projection you should quote with its range, never alone.

Drought, and why the word needs qualifying every time

Three different things are called drought, they respond to warming differently, and confusing them produces most of the contradictory headlines.

KindDefinitionWhat warming does to it
MeteorologicalA deficit of precipitation relative to normal. Changes where the circulation changes — regionally, and with low confidence outside the subtropical dry-zone expansion.
Agricultural / ecologicalA deficit of soil moisture in the root zone. Increases in many regions even where rainfall does not fall, because evaporative demand rises with temperature. This is the kind AR6 assesses as increasing with the most confidence.
HydrologicalA deficit of runoff, streamflow and groundwater. Depends on the seasonal timing as much as the total — and in snow-fed catchments on whether the snowpack survives. Norway’s rivers are a case in point.

The agricultural case is the one to understand mechanically, and you already have the physics. A warmer atmosphere has a larger vapour pressure deficit — the same exponential again, since es rises at 7 %/K while the actual vapour pressure over land rises more slowly. That deficit is the driving gradient for evaporation in the bulk-transfer formula of Lecture 3, page 2. So the atmosphere pulls harder on the soil every year, and unless rainfall rises to match, the soil dries.

And then the feedback closes: dry soil cannot evaporate, so the Bowen ratio rises, so the surface heats further — which is exactly the mechanism that raised the land–sea contrast on page 3 and which makes summer heatwaves in southern Europe so much hotter than the mean warming. Page 5 is where that becomes a number.

One honest complication. Higher CO2 also makes plants more water-efficient: stomata close a little for the same carbon uptake, so transpiration falls. That opposes the drying, and it is a real effect — the physiological forcing of Lecture 6, page 6. How much it offsets rising evaporative demand is genuinely contested, and it is one of the reasons different drought indices give different answers about the same future.
