Cumulative emissions and carbon budgets

There is one result in this whole lecture that behaves like a law: the warming is proportional to the total amount of carbon ever emitted, and almost nothing else. Not the rate, not the timing, not the pathway. That near-linearity is a coincidence of two cancelling non-linearities, it is extremely useful, and it converts a temperature target directly into a quantity of carbon — which is the single most policy-relevant number the science produces.

Where the carbon goes, and why the fractions are not fixed

The starting point is the budget you assembled in Lecture 4. Here it becomes the largest single uncertainty in a projection.

Of every tonne of CO2 emitted over the last decade, roughly 46 % has stayed in the atmosphere, about 26 % has been taken up by the ocean and about 28 % by the land biosphere. The atmospheric share is the airborne fraction, and it has been steady for sixty years — which is itself surprising, since emissions have quadrupled over that period. The sinks have kept pace.

They are not guaranteed to keep doing so, and the reason is a pair of feedbacks with opposite signs. Write the change in carbon stored by land or ocean as depending on two things:

ΔCsink  =  β ΔCatm  +  γ ΔT
β > 0 — concentration–carbon: more CO2 in the air drives more uptake  ·  γ < 0 — climate–carbon: warming reduces it
ΔCsink change in carbon stored by the land or ocean sink, PgC
ΔCatm change in the atmospheric carbon stock, PgC
ΔT global-mean surface warming, K
β concentration–carbon sensitivity, PgC per PgC — positive, so more CO2 drives more uptake
γ climate–carbon sensitivity, PgC K−1 — negative, so warming reduces uptake
  • β for the ocean is set by carbonate chemistry — the Revelle buffer factor of Lecture 4, page 3. It declines as the ocean acidifies: the buffer that lets sea water absorb CO2 is itself consumed by absorbing CO2. The ocean gets worse at its job the more of the job it does.
  • β for the land is CO2 fertilisation — higher CO2 raises net primary production. It saturates, and it is limited by nitrogen and phosphorus availability, which many models represent crudely or not at all.
  • γ for the ocean is negative because warm water holds less gas and because a more stratified ocean mixes carbon downward more slowly.
  • γ for the land is negative and the largest single uncertainty in the whole system: warming accelerates soil respiration, and in some models it turns the tropical land biosphere from a sink into a source. Add permafrost thaw — Lecture 6, page 6 — which most models in the archive do not include at all.
This is why concentration-driven and emission-driven experiments differ. Hand a set of models the same CO2 concentration and they agree fairly well on the warming. Hand them the same emissions and let each work out its own concentration, and the spread widens considerably — a few hundred ppm by 2100 in the high scenarios. Textbook Fig. 6.23 shows exactly that comparison. Since the real world hands us emissions, not concentrations, the emission-driven spread is the fair one, and most of the famous figures use the other.
Question. The demonstration on page 1 came out roughly 20 % low on the 2100 CO2 concentration under SSP5–8.5, and about right under SSP1–1.9. Given what you have just read, is that a coincidence? No — it is the missing γ term, showing up exactly where it should. That model uses a linear pulse response: a fixed fraction of every tonne stays in the air, no matter how warm the world or how acidified the ocean. Under a low scenario there is little warming and little acidification, so the assumption is nearly true and the concentration comes out right. Under a high scenario the sinks weaken substantially, more carbon stays airborne than the linear model allows, and it runs low. The size of the error on page 1 is a rough measure of how much the carbon-cycle feedback matters — and it is one of the reasons a carbon budget is quoted with a range rather than a number.

Why should this work at all? Two strong non-linearities cancel, and the cancellation is not exact but it is very good.

  • Radiative forcing is logarithmic in concentration. Each extra tonne of CO2 buys less forcing than the one before — the saturation argument from Lecture 6, page 1.
  • Ocean uptake of both carbon and heat declines as the perturbation ages. The fraction of each tonne still in the air rises with time, and the fraction of the forcing still being absorbed by the deep ocean falls. Both mean each extra tonne buys more realised warming than the one before.
  • The first effect makes the response saturate; the second makes it accelerate; over the range that matters they very nearly cancel, leaving a straight line. It is not a deep law. It is a numerical accident that happens to hold over precisely the range humanity is operating in.
Question. Follow the overshoot pathway — the one that goes net negative. On the cumulative axis it retraces its steps leftwards. Does the temperature come back down the same line? Not quite — it sits slightly above the line on the way back. The proportionality is not fully reversible: removing a tonne of CO2 from the atmosphere does not undo the warming that emitting it caused, because the deep ocean has meanwhile absorbed heat that it now releases slowly. The asymmetry is modest in this model and larger in comprehensive ones. The practical consequence is worth stating plainly: overshoot-and-remove is not equivalent to not emitting, and a budget that assumes it is will be too generous. It is also why sea level, on page 7, does not come back at all.

Question. Drag TCRE from 1.0 to 2.3 with everything else fixed. The budget changes by more than a factor of two. Which uncertainty is that, in the language of page 2? Model uncertainty — physics we do not yet know well enough, not policy we have not yet chosen. And notice the asymmetry it creates. The budget is far more sensitive to TCRE than the warming for a given scenario is, because you are dividing by the uncertain quantity instead of multiplying by it. That is why quoted budget numbers move around between assessments in a way that looks alarming and is mostly arithmetic. The safe reading is not the central number: it is that a 1.5 °C budget is a small number of years at present rates under any value in the range.
Question. Set the target to 2.0 °C. The computed budget overshoots the AR6 assessed value of about 1350 GtCO2. What do you have to change to reproduce it, and why is that change physically justified rather than a fudge? Raise the non-CO2 allowance to about 0.32 °C. It is justified because a 2 °C pathway takes longer to reach its target than a 1.5 °C pathway, so by the time you get there more methane and nitrous oxide have accumulated and more of the cooling sulphate aerosol has been cleaned out of the air — the unmasking effect from page 1. AR6 does exactly this: its non-CO2 allowance is not a constant but is derived from the scenarios consistent with each target. The lesson is that a carbon budget is never purely a statement about carbon.
Three ways a budget can be wrong that are not in the slider set. Earth system feedbacks not represented in most models — permafrost carbon, wetland methane, fire — which AR6 estimates reduce the remaining budget by roughly 26 GtCO2 for each decade of delay, and which are one-directional. The zero-emissions commitment, assumed here to be zero, which is likely but not certainly small. And the definition of the target itself: a budget for holding warming below 1.5 °C is not the same as a budget for a 50 % chance of doing so, which is not the same as one that permits an overshoot and a return. The three differ by hundreds of gigatonnes, and public discussion routinely conflates them.

Net zero, and the near-cancellation that makes the target meaningful

Why “stop emitting and the warming stops” is approximately true — which is neither obvious nor guaranteed.

Suppose CO2 emissions go to zero tomorrow. Two things then happen, and they pull in opposite directions.

Cooling: the ocean and land keep absorbing the existing atmospheric excess. Concentration falls, forcing falls, temperature is pulled down. You can watch this on the middle panel of page 1.

Warming: the system was never in equilibrium with the forcing it already had. As the deep ocean warms, its uptake of heat declines, and the surface continues to catch up — the committed warming of Lecture 6, page 5.

The zero-emissions commitment is the net of the two, and the multi-model assessment puts it close to zero — likely within about ±0.3 °C over the fifty years after emissions cease. That near-cancellation is what makes “net zero” a coherent goal at all. If the commitment were strongly positive, reaching net zero would not stop the warming and the whole framing of climate policy would be different. It is a physical result, not a definition, and it could have come out otherwise.

Note carefully what does not stop. Sea level keeps rising for centuries. The deep ocean keeps warming. The ice sheets keep responding. Net zero stabilises the surface temperature and almost nothing else, which is why page 7 exists.

Where the emissions actually are

The scenario question of page 1, brought up to date.

Global CO2 emissions have been roughly flat to slowly rising for a decade, at around 40 GtCO2 per year, after two decades of steep growth. The 2020 pandemic produced a drop of a few per cent that was fully recovered within two years — a natural experiment worth dwelling on, because it showed what an unprecedented disruption to global activity does to the emissions curve, and the answer was: very little, briefly.

Set against a 1.5 °C budget of a few hundred gigatonnes, a plateau is not a solution — it is a constant rate of spending down a fixed account. The budget arithmetic makes this unusually plain: the quantity that matters is the area under the emissions curve, and a flat curve has a great deal of area under it. That is the single most useful thing this page gives you for reading any claim about climate progress.

Try it yourself. En-ROADS lets you move real policy levers — carbon price, electrification, deforestation, methane, energy efficiency — and see the 2100 temperature respond. Two things are worth testing deliberately. First, find the single lever with the largest effect, and notice how few of the obvious candidates qualify. Second, watch the cumulative emissions readout rather than the temperature readout, because after this page you know that is where the answer really lives.
