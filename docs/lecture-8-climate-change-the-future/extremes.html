Extremes and distribution shifts

Nothing is damaged by the mean. Crops fail, people die and infrastructure breaks in the tail of the distribution, and the tail moves in a way that is deeply counter-intuitive: a shift far too small to notice in the average multiplies the frequency of rare events several times over. This page is mostly one calculation, done properly — and it turns out to reproduce the IPCC’s assessed numbers almost exactly.

Why the tail is so much more sensitive than the middle

The whole page in one idea, before any numbers.

Take a bell-shaped distribution of summer temperatures and slide it to the right by a small amount. Near the middle, where the curve is nearly flat, the change in probability is tiny. Out in the tail, where the curve is falling steeply, the same horizontal shift produces an enormous fractional change — because you are comparing two small numbers, and the ratio of two small numbers can be huge.

This is not a subtlety about climate. It is a property of any steeply-falling function, and it means the correct summary of the situation is not “summers are a bit warmer” but “the summer that used to happen once a generation now happens most years”. Both statements describe the same 1 °C.

Question. Before you touch the demonstration, guess. If the mean of a normal distribution moves by one standard deviation, by what factor does the probability of exceeding the old three-sigma threshold change? Just under 17. The probability goes from 0.00135 to 0.0228 — a once-in-740 event becomes a once-in-44 event. Almost nobody guesses above five. Do the same exercise for a four-sigma threshold and the factor is about 44. The rarer the event you started with, the more dramatically its frequency changes — which is exactly why the events that break things are the ones changing fastest.

The demonstration this page is built around

Moving the distribution

Set a global warming, and the page works out what it does to the local extreme temperature distribution. The tail probabilities are exact — there is no fitting and no simulation, only the normal distribution and arithmetic.

The chain has three steps and you control all of them. Global warming becomes local warming through the amplification factor of page 3 — hot extremes over land warm faster than the global mean. Local warming becomes a shift in standard deviations by dividing by σ, the year-to-year spread of the annual hottest day at that place. And a shift in standard deviations becomes a probability, exactly.

Question. With the default settings the agreement is close at every warming level. What does that tell you — and what does it not tell you? It tells you the mechanism is right: to a very good approximation, the projected increase in hot extremes is a shifted distribution and nothing more. No new physics is needed, no change in variability is needed, and the enormous multipliers in the IPCC figure are an arithmetic consequence of a few degrees of warming rather than a separate claim. That is genuinely reassuring about the assessment, and it is the most useful thing to take away.

What it does not tell you: that the shift is the same everywhere (it is not — the amplification varies a great deal by region and season), that variability is unchanged everywhere (in some regions, particularly where soil moisture dries out, σ rises and makes things worse than this), or that temperature extremes are the whole story. Drought, rainfall and compound events do not follow a single Gaussian.
Question. Set σ to 0.9 K — a tropical value, where the year-to-year swing is small. The multiplier becomes enormous. Is a stable climate more or less vulnerable? More, and this is one of the more important results in the whole field. The tropics have small interannual variability, so the same absolute warming is a much larger shift in units of σ. That means tropical extremes leave the historical range sooner — the earliest time of emergence anywhere on Earth is in the tropics, not in the Arctic, even though the Arctic warms three times faster. It is the same σ in the denominator as in Lecture 7, page 1, doing the same work. And the ecological version is sharper still: organisms adapted to a narrow range have little margin, so a small absolute change can exceed their thermal tolerance while a larger change elsewhere does not.

The same arithmetic, run backwards: event attribution

Where the probability ratio stops being a projection and becomes a claim about an event that has already happened.

A journalist asks whether last summer’s heatwave was caused by climate change. The question as posed has no answer — no single event has a single cause. But a different question does have an answer, and it is the one the demonstration above is already computing:

PR  =  p1 / p0       FAR  =  1  −  p0/p1
p1 — probability of the event in the world we have  ·  p0 — in a counterfactual world without the anthropogenic forcing

The probability ratio is the frequency multiplier you just computed. The fraction of attributable risk converts it to a statement about a particular event: if the event is ten times more likely now, then nine tenths of its present probability would not exist without the forcing. That is a defensible, quantitative, falsifiable claim — and it is a claim about probabilities, never about a single event’s cause.

The counterfactual world is the hard part, and it comes from exactly the machinery of Lecture 7, page 6: large ensembles run with and without the anthropogenic forcing, with the fingerprint regression establishing that the forced signal is really there. Event attribution is detection and attribution applied to a tail instead of a trend, and the reason it needs enormous ensembles is that you are estimating a small probability twice.

Two honest limits. First, PR is very sensitive to how the event is defined — the same heatwave gets a different number depending on whether you frame it as a three-day mean or a single day, a national average or one station. Choose the definition after seeing the data and you have cherry-picked, which is the trap Lecture 7’s last demonstration is about. Second, the method works far better for temperature than for rainfall or storms, because the temperature distribution is well behaved and well sampled. Where you see a confident PR for a heatwave and a cautious “consistent with” for a flood, that asymmetry is real and is about statistics, not about caution.

Rainfall extremes, and why they behave differently from mean rainfall

The 7 % versus 2 % gap of page 4, arriving where it does the most damage.

Global mean precipitation is rationed by the atmosphere’s energy budget. An individual storm is not: it converges the moisture available in its neighbourhood and rains it out. So the intensity of heavy rainfall follows the moisture content, at roughly the Clausius–Clapeyron rate of 7 % per kelvin, while the mean rises at 1–3 %. The consequences are two, and they sound contradictory until you see the mechanism.

  • When it rains, it rains harder. AR6 assesses the intensity of the annual wettest day rising about 7 % per degree of global warming, and a 1-in-10-year heavy precipitation event becoming about 1.7 times as frequent at 2 °C of warming and 2.7 times at 4 °C.
  • And there are more dry days between the rains. If the total is nearly fixed and each event delivers more, the events must become less frequent. The same region can get more flooding and more drought, from one mechanism, without any contradiction at all.
Worked aside. Suppose the tail of daily rainfall is exponential, P(X > x) = exp(−x/β), and warming multiplies β by 1.21 (three kelvin at 7 %/K). What happens to a 1-in-100-year daily rainfall? Scaling β turns the exceedance probability p into p1/1.21, so the return period T becomes T1/1.21: 100 years becomes 1000.826 ≈ 45 years, a frequency multiplier of 2.2. Notice that this is a bigger effect for rarer events, the same way the Gaussian tail was.

And an honest caveat, because this one matters. Real rainfall tails are heavier than exponential — a generalised Pareto with a positive shape parameter fits far better — so the true multiplier is larger than this calculation gives, which is why AR6’s assessed numbers exceed it. A pure Gaussian works remarkably well for temperature and a pure exponential does not work for rainfall. That is why the demonstration on this page is about heat and not about floods.

Compound events, and the reason the risk is worse than any single number

The frontier of this part of the subject, and the part impact assessments most often miss.

The demonstration above treats one variable in isolation. Real damage usually comes from combinations — and the combinations are not independent, so multiplying individual probabilities together underestimates the risk, often by a lot.

  • Hot and dry together. The physical link is the soil-moisture feedback you have now met three times: dry soil cannot evaporate, so the Bowen ratio rises (Lecture 3) and the heatwave gets hotter. Drought and heatwave are not two independent draws; each makes the other worse. Europe 2003, Russia 2010 and western North America 2021 were all of this kind.
  • Storm surge on a high tide on a raised sea level. The sea-level rise of page 7 is slow and undramatic and it changes the flooding statistics enormously, because it moves the whole surge distribution up — the same shift argument as this page, applied to water instead of temperature.
  • Sequences. Drought followed by intense rain on baked ground; a wet winter that saturates soil followed by a storm. The order matters and a marginal distribution cannot see it.
  • Spatially concurrent events. Simultaneous harvest failure in several breadbaskets, because they are linked by the same mode of variability (Lecture 7, page 2). Independence fails at exactly the moment when it would have saved you.
Question. Where in this course have you already seen a system in which two variables reinforce each other until the combination flips into a different state? PC Lab 3 — vegetation and precipitation in the Sahara, where each sustains the other and the pair has two stable states. Compound events are the statistical face of the same idea: coupled variables, a joint distribution with much more weight in the corner than independence would give, and in the extreme case a threshold beyond which the system does not come back. Page 7 returns to that last possibility with the ocean circulation.
