PART I

The Earth's energy budget

2.1 – 2.13 + §2.1.5

Two ideas run through this block: the planet must radiate away exactly what it absorbs, and an infrared-absorbing atmosphere forces the surface to sit hotter than that balance temperature. The last three entries are standard relations from §2.1.2 and §2.1.5 rather than numbered equations in the text.

2.1
Stefan–Boltzmann law
$$A^{\uparrow} = \sigma T_e^{4}$$
Energy radiated per square metre by a black body. Applied to Earth it defines the effective emission temperature \(T_e\) — a bookkeeping temperature, not one you can measure anywhere.
A↑ — emitted flux, W m⁻²
σ — 5.67×10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴
Te — effective emission temp., K
2.2
Planetary equilibrium
$$\pi R^{2} S_0 \left(1 - \alpha_p\right) = 4\pi R^{2}\,\sigma T_e^{4}$$
Absorbed solar radiation equals emitted terrestrial radiation. Sunlight is intercepted over a disc \(\pi R^2\); emission leaves the whole sphere \(4\pi R^2\).
S₀ — solar constant, 1368 W m⁻²
αp — planetary albedo ≈ 0.3
R — Earth radius, 6371 km
2.3
Balance per unit surface
$$\tfrac{1}{4} S_0 \left(1 - \alpha_p\right) = \sigma T_e^{4}$$
The same statement divided by the Earth's surface area. The factor ¼ is pure geometry — disc over sphere — and gives the familiar 342 W m⁻² of incoming solar radiation.
¼S₀ ≈ 342 W m⁻²
absorbed ≈ 239 W m⁻²
2.4
Effective emission temperature
$$T_e = \left[\frac{S_0\left(1-\alpha_p\right)}{4\sigma}\right]^{1/4}$$
Solving 2.3 for temperature gives 255 K (−18 °C). The 33 K gap to the observed 288 K surface mean is the greenhouse effect.
Te = 255 K = −18 °C
observed Ts = 288 K = 15 °C
2.5
One opaque layer — top of atmosphere
$$\sigma T_a^{4} = \tfrac{1}{4} S_0\left(1-\alpha_p\right) = \sigma T_e^{4}$$
Slab model: the atmosphere is transparent to sunlight, opaque to infrared. Everything leaving to space comes from the slab, so \(T_a = T_e\).
Ta — atmospheric layer temp., K
⇒ Ta = Te = 255 K
2.6
One opaque layer — surface
$$\sigma T_s^{4} = \tfrac{1}{4} S_0\left(1-\alpha_p\right) + \sigma T_a^{4}$$
The surface receives sunlight and the downward infrared emission of the atmosphere — the second term is the greenhouse contribution.
Ts — surface temperature, K
2.7
Doubling of surface emission
$$\sigma T_s^{4} = \sigma T_e^{4} + \sigma T_e^{4} = 2\,\sigma T_e^{4}$$
Combining 2.5 and 2.6: an opaque slab makes the surface emit exactly twice what the planet loses to space.
each extra opaque layer adds one Te⁴ of surface emission
2.8
Blackbody-slab surface temperature
$$T_s = 2^{1/4}\,T_e = 1.19\,T_e$$
Gives 303 K (30 °C) — above the observed 288 K, because a single perfectly opaque layer overstates the trapping.
Ts = 303 K = 30 °C
2.9
Grey atmosphere — surface balance
$$\sigma T_s^{4} = \tfrac{1}{4} S_0\left(1-\alpha_p\right) + \varepsilon\,\sigma T_a^{4}$$
More realistic: the atmosphere emits (and absorbs) only a fraction \(\varepsilon\) of blackbody radiation.
ε — infrared emissivity, 0 ≤ ε ≤ 1
= fraction absorbed (Kirchhoff)
2.10
Grey atmosphere — top of atmosphere
$$\tfrac{1}{4} S_0\left(1-\alpha_p\right) = \varepsilon\,\sigma T_a^{4} + \left(1-\varepsilon\right)\sigma T_s^{4} = \sigma T_e^{4}$$
Space sees two contributions: the atmosphere's own emission, plus the surface radiation transmitted through it, \(1-\varepsilon\).
(1−ε) — transmitted fraction
2.11
Solving the grey model
$$\sigma T_s^{4} = \frac{2}{2-\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{S_0\left(1-\alpha_p\right)}{4} = \frac{2}{2-\varepsilon}\,\sigma T_e^{4}$$
Eliminate \(T_a\) between 2.9 and 2.10. The whole greenhouse effect sits in the factor \(2/(2-\varepsilon)\).
ε = 0 → no greenhouse
ε = 1 → recovers Eq. 2.7
2.12
Surface temperature vs. emissivity
$$T_s = \left(\frac{2}{2-\varepsilon}\right)^{1/4} T_e$$
With a typical \(\varepsilon = 0.97\): \(T_s = 1.18\,T_e = 301\) K (28 °C). Setting \(\varepsilon = 0\) returns \(T_s = T_e\), the no-atmosphere case.
ε = 0.97 → Ts = 301 K
ε = 0 → Ts = Te = 255 K
2.13
Atmospheric temperature
$$T_a = \left(\frac{1}{2-\varepsilon}\right)^{1/4} T_e = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{1/4} T_s$$
The slab is always colder than the surface by the fixed ratio \(2^{-1/4} \approx 0.84\) — the vertical temperature contrast the greenhouse effect requires.
Ta / Ts = 0.84 always
§2.1.2
Emission level and lapse rate
$$T_s = T_e + \Gamma\, z_e$$
The greenhouse effect stated as geometry. Top-of-atmosphere balance pins the emission temperature at \(T_e\); the surface is warmer by the lapse rate times the height of the level that radiates to space. Adding CO2 raises \(z_e\), and the whole profile shifts up with it.
Γ — lapse rate, ≈ 6.5 K km⁻¹
ze — emission level, ≈ 5.1 km
⇒ Ts ≈ 288 K
§2.1.5
Column energy budget
$$\frac{\partial E_s}{\partial t} = R_{TOA} - F_{transport}$$
A local imbalance at the top of the atmosphere is either stored or exported. Storage dominates on seasonal timescales; on the annual mean it nearly vanishes and transport carries the whole thing.
Es — stored energy, J m⁻²
RTOA — net radiation, W m⁻²
global mean today ≈ +0.6 W m⁻²
§2.1.5
Meridional heat transport
$$F(\varphi) = 2\pi a^{2}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\varphi} R_{TOA}(\varphi')\cos\varphi'\,\mathrm{d}\varphi'$$
Nobody measures petawatts crossing a latitude circle. The transport is inferred by integrating the satellite radiation imbalance from one pole; F must return to zero at the other, and it is the failure to do so that reveals the observational error.
a — Earth radius, 6371 km
peak ≈ 5.5 PW near 35–40°
1 PW = 10¹⁵ W
PART II

Heat balance at the surface

2.34 – 2.37

Turbulent exchange between surface and air, and the full budget that a model actually solves to obtain the surface temperature \(T_s\).

2.34
Latent heat flux — bulk formula
$$F_{LE} = L_v\,\rho_a\,c_L\,U_a\left(q_s - q_a\right)$$
Evaporation cools the surface in proportion to wind speed and the humidity deficit. Its companion is the sensible heat flux (Eq. 2.33), \(F_{SH} = \rho_a c_p c_h U_a (T_s - T_a)\). Because \(q_s\) follows Clausius–Clapeyron, \(F_{LE}\) is far larger in the tropics.
Lv — latent heat of vaporisation
ρa — air density
cL, ch — bulk coefficients, 5×10⁻⁵–1×10⁻³
Ua, Ta, qa — at 2 or 10 m
qs — surface specific humidity
2.35
Bowen ratio
$$B_o = \frac{F_{SH}}{F_{LE}}$$
How the surface splits its turbulent heat loss. \(B_o \ll 1\) over warm ocean, \(B_o \gg 1\) over dry land where water availability limits evaporation.
Bo ≈ 1 — high-latitude ocean
Bo > 1 — arid land
2.36
Full surface heat balance
$$\begin{aligned}\rho\,c_p\,h_{su}\frac{\partial T_s}{\partial t} =\;& \left(1-\alpha\right)F_{sol} + F_{IR}^{\downarrow} + F_{IR}^{\uparrow} \\ &+ F_{SE} + F_{LE} + F_{cond}\end{aligned}$$
Heat storage in a thin surface layer of thickness \(h_{su}\) equals the sum of all fluxes. This is how \(T_s\) is obtained in practice. Over snow or ice it holds only while \(T_s\) is below freezing; at the melting point a latent-heat-of-fusion term is added instead.
Fsol — incoming solar at surface
α — surface albedo
FIR↓ — downward longwave, 333 W m⁻²
FIR↑ — upward longwave (Stefan–Boltzmann), 396 W m⁻²
Fcond — conduction from below (Fourier)
hsu — surface layer thickness
2.37
Oxidation of methane
$$\mathrm{CH_4} + 2\,\mathrm{O_2} \;\longrightarrow\; \mathrm{CO_2} + 2\,\mathrm{H_2O}$$
Methane is far more reactive than CO₂ and is removed by oxidation — which is why its atmospheric lifetime is short but its end product is another greenhouse gas.
CH₄: 725 → 1780 ppb in 150 yr
CO₂: 280 (1800) → 384 ppm (2007)
PART III

The carbon cycle

2.40 – 2.49

The carbonate chemistry that lets the ocean hold far more carbon than the atmosphere, the biological pumps that move it downward, and the million-year rock loop that closes the budget.

2.40
2.41
Dissociation of carbonic acid
$$\mathrm{H_2CO_3} \;\rightleftharpoons\; \mathrm{H^{+}} + \mathrm{HCO_3^{-}}$$
$$\mathrm{HCO_3^{-}} \;\rightleftharpoons\; \mathrm{H^{+}} + \mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}$$
Following dissolution (Eq. 2.39), carbonic acid dissociates twice, releasing H⁺ — this is ocean acidification. The reactions are fast enough to be treated as always in equilibrium.
at equilibrium in seawater:
HCO₃⁻ ≈ 90 % of DIC
CO₃²⁻ ≈ 10 %
H₂CO₃ ≈ 0.5 %
2.42
Dissolved Inorganic Carbon
$$\mathrm{DIC} = \left[\mathrm{H_2CO_3}\right] + \left[\mathrm{HCO_3^{-}}\right] + \left[\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}\right]$$
Atmospheric CO₂ must balance the whole DIC pool, not just H₂CO₃ — the reason the ocean stores far more carbon than gases of similar solubility such as O₂.
DIC is ~15 % higher at depth
than at the surface
2.43
Solubility of CO₂
$$K_H = \frac{\left[\mathrm{H_2CO_3}\right]}{p\mathrm{CO_2}}$$
Links dissolved carbonic acid to the partial pressure of CO₂, so the air–sea flux (Eq. 2.38) can be written in terms of concentrations. \(K_H\) rises as water cools — cold water takes up CO₂, warm water releases it.
Eq. 2.38: ΦCO₂ = kCO₂(pW − pA)
k — wind-dependent transfer coeff.
2.44
Carbonate buffering
$$\mathrm{H_2CO_3} + \mathrm{CO_3^{2-}} \;\rightleftharpoons\; 2\,\mathrm{HCO_3^{-}}$$
Only about 1 CO₂ molecule in 20 entering the ocean stays as H₂CO₃; the rest is buffered into bicarbonate. This slows equilibration to roughly 6 months — 20× longer than without the reaction.
uptake depends on CO₃²⁻ availability
equilibration time ≈ 6 months
2.45
Alkalinity
$$\begin{aligned}\mathrm{Alk} =\;& \left[\mathrm{HCO_3^{-}}\right] + 2\left[\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}\right] + \left[\mathrm{OH^{-}}\right] \\ &- \left[\mathrm{H^{+}}\right] + \left[\mathrm{B(OH)_4^{-}}\right] + \text{minor bases}\end{aligned}$$
The excess of bases over acids, dominated by bicarbonate and carbonate. A 1 % drop in Alk raises oceanic \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\) by about 10 %, weakening the ocean's uptake.
−1 % Alk → +10 % pCO₂
B(OH)₄⁻ — borate ion
2.46
Photosynthesis / respiration
$$6\,\mathrm{CO_2} + 6\,\mathrm{H_2O} \;\rightleftharpoons\; \mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} + 6\,\mathrm{O_2}$$
Read left to right it is photosynthesis; right to left, respiration and remineralisation. Sinking organic matter drives the soft tissue pump, the largest contributor to the vertical DIC gradient. Nutrients (nitrate, phosphate, iron) usually limit production.
three ocean pumps:
soft tissue · carbonate · solubility
2.47
Calcium carbonate formation
$$\mathrm{Ca^{2+}} + \mathrm{CO_3^{2-}} \;\rightleftharpoons\; \mathrm{CaCO_3}$$
Shell building consumes CO₃²⁻, lowering alkalinity — so, counter-intuitively, it raises oceanic \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\) and reduces uptake. Sinking shells form the carbonate pump.
calcite undersaturated below
~4500 m Atlantic · ~800 m Pacific
2.48
Metamorphism (subduction)
$$\mathrm{CaCO_3} + \mathrm{SiO_2} \;\longrightarrow\; \mathrm{CaSiO_3} + \mathrm{CO_2}$$
Buried limestone is carried down at plate boundaries and cooked into calcium silicate, releasing CO₂ that returns to the atmosphere through volcanism. A source of atmospheric CO₂.
>50×10⁶ GtC stored in the crust
~1000× the ocean stock
2.49
Silicate weathering
$$\mathrm{CaSiO_3} + \mathrm{H_2CO_3} \;\longrightarrow\; \mathrm{CaCO_3} + \mathrm{SiO_2} + \mathrm{H_2O}$$
Uplifted silicate rock reacts with carbonic acid in rainwater. Products travel by river to the sea, so weathering is a CO₂ sink. With 2.48 it closes the long-term inorganic carbon cycle over millions of years.
weathering → CO₂, → alkalinity
metamorphism + sedimentation → CO₂
PART IV

Radiative forcing

4.1 – 4.3

Radiative forcing \(\Delta Q\) is the net change in the radiative budget at the tropopause, with stratospheric temperatures allowed to re-equilibrate but the surface and troposphere held fixed. Reference values for 1750: CO₂ 278 ppm, CH₄ 715 ppb, N₂O 270 ppb.

4.1
Carbon dioxide forcing
$$\Delta Q = 5.4 \ln\!\left(\frac{\left[\mathrm{CO_2}\right]}{\left[\mathrm{CO_2}\right]_r}\right)$$
Logarithmic, not linear: each doubling adds the same ~3.7 W m⁻². Concentrations in ppm. Gives ≈ 1.7 W m⁻² for 1750–2005, the largest single anthropogenic forcing.
ΔQ — radiative forcing, W m⁻²
[ ]r — reference period
CO₂ 1750–2005: +1.7 W m⁻²
4.2
Methane forcing
$$\Delta Q = 0.036\left(\sqrt{\left[\mathrm{CH_4}\right]} - \sqrt{\left[\mathrm{CH_4}\right]_r}\right)$$
Square-root dependence, concentrations in ppb. Reference \(\left[\mathrm{CH_4}\right]_r = 715\) ppb.
concentrations in ppb
CH₄ ref. 715 ppb (1750)
4.3
Nitrous oxide forcing
$$\Delta Q = 0.12\left(\sqrt{\left[\mathrm{N_2O}\right]} - \sqrt{\left[\mathrm{N_2O}\right]_r}\right)$$
Same form as 4.2, in ppb, with \(\left[\mathrm{N_2O}\right]_r = 270\) ppb. For halocarbons a linear expression is adequate.
N₂O ref. 270 ppb (1750)
halocarbons — linear in concentration
PART V

Feedback and response

4.4 – 4.16

How a forcing \(\Delta Q\) becomes a temperature change. Downward fluxes are positive throughout, so \(\lambda_f\) must be negative for the system to reach an equilibrium at all. Reference value: a doubling of CO₂ gives \(\Delta Q \approx 3.7\) W m⁻².

4.4
Radiative imbalance and the feedback parameter
$$\Delta R = \Delta Q + \lambda_f \Delta T_s$$
The linearisation the whole of Chapter 4 rests on: every feedback in the system, collapsed into one slope. \(\Delta R\) is the energy currently accumulating — about 0.9 W m⁻² today, almost all of it entering the ocean.
ΔR — imbalance, W m⁻²
λf — feedback parameter, W m⁻² K⁻¹
downward positive
4.5
Equilibrium temperature change
$$\Delta T_s = -\frac{1}{\lambda_f}\,\Delta Q$$
Set \(\Delta R = 0\). The quantity \((-1/\lambda_f)\) is the climate sensitivity in K per W m⁻². Defined against a CO₂ doubling it is the equilibrium climate sensitivity, likely 1.5–4.5 °C.
ECS 1.5 – 4.5 K
⇒ λf between −2.5 and −0.8
4.7
Decomposition into direct physical feedbacks
$$\lambda_f = \sum_i \lambda_i = \lambda_0 + \lambda_L + \lambda_w + \lambda_c + \lambda_\alpha$$
Blackbody, lapse rate, water vapour, cloud, surface albedo. Circulation changes and biogeochemical feedbacks are excluded by construction.
λ0 = −3.2   λw = +1.8
λL = −0.8   λc = +0.68
λα = +0.26   ⇒ λf ≈ −1.26
4.10
Blackbody (Planck) response
$$\lambda_0 = \frac{\partial R}{\partial T} = -4\sigma T_e^{3}$$
Differentiate the top-of-atmosphere balance (Eq. 2.4) assuming uniform warming. With \(T_e = 255\) K this gives −3.8; radiative transfer calculations refine it to −3.2 W m⁻² K⁻¹. Alone it gives \(\Delta T_{s,0} = -\Delta Q/\lambda_0 \approx 1.2\) K per doubling.
Te — emission temperature, 255 K
σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴
the only negative feedback that is large
4.13
The climate feedback factor
$$\Delta T_s = \frac{1}{1 + \dfrac{\lambda_L + \lambda_w + \lambda_c + \lambda_\alpha}{\lambda_0}}\;\Delta T_{s,0} = f_f\,\Delta T_{s,0}$$
How much larger the real response is than the blackbody one. \(f_f > 1\) means amplification. With the standard values \(f_f \approx 2.5\). Because this is a hyperbola in \(\lambda_f\), a symmetric uncertainty in the feedbacks gives a lopsided range in \(\Delta T_s\).
ff ≈ 2.5
ΔTs,0 ≈ 1.16 K
⇒ ΔTs ≈ 2.9 K
4.14
Transient response
$$C_s \frac{\mathrm{d}\Delta T_s}{\mathrm{d}t} = \Delta Q + \lambda_f \Delta T_s$$
Eq. 4.4 with the imbalance heating a slab of heat capacity \(C_s\) instead of being discarded. This is Eq. 3.x of the zero-dimensional model, linearised about the present state.
Cs — heat capacity, J K⁻¹ m⁻²
200 m of water: 8.36 × 10⁸
ρcw = 4.18 × 10⁶ J m⁻³ K⁻¹
4.15
4.16
Solution for a step forcing
$$\Delta T_s(t) = -\frac{\Delta Q}{\lambda_f}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right), \qquad \tau = -\frac{C_s}{\lambda_f}$$
At \(t = \tau\) the system has realised 63 % of its equilibrium response. Note that \(\tau\) contains \(\lambda_f\): a more sensitive climate is also a slower one. At \(t = 0\) the slope is \(\Delta Q/C_s\), independent of \(\lambda_f\) — which is why the early years of a warming carry no information about climate sensitivity.
τ — response time, s
λf = −1.85 ⇒ τ ≈ 14 yr
λf = −0.93 ⇒ τ ≈ 28 yr
PART VI

Variability, orbits and attribution

Chapter 5

The relations used in Lecture 7. Chapter 5 is largely descriptive and numbers few equations, so these are labelled by name rather than by equation number; each is standard and its source is given in the right-hand column and on the corresponding teaching page.

insol
Daily mean insolation at any latitude, season and orbit
$$\bar{Q}_{\text{day}} = \frac{S_0}{\pi}\left(\frac{a}{r}\right)^{2}\left[H_0\sin\phi\sin\delta + \cos\phi\cos\delta\sin H_0\right]$$
With \(\cos H_0 = -\tan\phi\tan\delta\), \(\sin\delta = \sin\varepsilon\sin\lambda\) and \(a/r = (1 + e\cos\nu)/(1-e^2)\), \(\nu = \lambda - \bar\omega\). Exact. The three orbital parameters \(e,\ \varepsilon,\ \bar\omega\) enter here and nowhere else — everything the astronomical theory says about forcing is contained in this one expression.
φ — latitude   λ — solar longitude
ε — obliquity, 22.0–24.5°
e — eccentricity, 0–0.058
ω̄ — longitude of perihelion
65°N, June solstice, today: 478 W m⁻²
annual
Global annual mean insolation
$$\bar{Q} = \frac{S_0}{4\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}$$
Neither obliquity nor precession appears. Across the full range of eccentricity this varies by 0.17 %, about 0.5 W m⁻² — for comparison, a CO₂ doubling is 3.7. Orbital forcing redistributes energy; it barely adds any. That is why it needs an amplifier.
e = 0.0167 ⇒ 340.3 W m⁻²
e = 0.058  ⇒ 340.8 W m⁻²
S/N
Emergence of a forced signal from internal variability
$$\frac{S}{N}(t) = \frac{b\,t}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}, \qquad t_{\text{emerge}} = \frac{2\sigma}{b\sqrt{n}}$$
A trend \(b\) accumulates linearly while noise averages down as \(1/\sqrt{n}\). Both the area averaged over and the number of years contribute to \(n\), which is why the global mean shows a trend that a single grid box does not. Time of emergence is conventionally taken at \(S/N = 2\).
σ, annual mean SAT:
global 0.11 K   Europe 0.45 K
grid box 0.7 K   ice edge 1.8 K
after Hawkins & Sutton (2012)
Stommel
Two-box overturning circulation
$$q = k(\alpha\Delta T - \beta\Delta S), \qquad V\frac{\mathrm{d}\Delta S}{\mathrm{d}t} = 2H - |q|\,\Delta S$$
Stommel (1961). Temperature is restored fast and treated as fixed; salinity is prognostic. The equilibria are the roots of a quadratic in \(q\), giving two stable branches separated by an unstable one, and a saddle-node bifurcation at \(H_c\) beyond which the strong branch ceases to exist. The source of hysteresis and of abrupt change.
q — overturning, Sv
H — freshwater flux, Sv
α, β — expansion coefficients
present-day Atlantic ≈ 18 Sv, ≈1 PW
seesaw
The thermal bipolar seesaw
$$\frac{\mathrm{d}T_S}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{-T_N - T_S}{\tau}$$
Stocker and Johnsen (2003). The southern reservoir integrates the northern signal rather than mirroring it, which is why Antarctic ice cores show smooth maxima at the moment Greenland jumps. One equation, and the observed inter-hemispheric phasing follows.
τ ≈ 1000 yr
short τ ⇒ mirror image
long τ ⇒ smoothed integral
ice
Asymmetric ice-volume response
$$\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{x - y}{\tau}, \qquad \tau = \begin{cases}\tau_0/(1+b) & \text{melting}\\[2pt] \tau_0/(1-b) & \text{growing}\end{cases}$$
Imbrie and Imbrie (1980). Ice melts faster than it grows. That single non-linearity turns a forcing containing only 19, 23 and 41 kyr into a sawtooth response with power near 100 kyr — a frequency that is not in the forcing. It does not, on its own, solve the 100-kyr problem.
y — ice volume
x — normalised 65°N summer
insolation, sign reversed
τ0 ≈ 17 kyr   b ≈ 0.6
D&A
Detection and attribution regression
$$T(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} \beta_i\,\mathrm{Resp}_i(t) + u(t)$$
Models supply the shape of each response — the fingerprint; the observations supply the amplitude through the fitted \(\beta_i\); the statistics of \(u\) come from long control simulations. \(\beta_i\) significantly greater than zero is detection; \(\beta_i\) consistent with one means the model amplitude needs no rescaling.
βi — scaling coefficient
u — internal variability
worked example in Goosse
Fig. 5.43: β1 = 0.6, β2 = 1.2

Part VI is a companion to Lecture 7 rather than a transcription of Chapter 5, which sets out the physics of variability and of past climates largely in words. The insolation integral and the global annual mean are standard results of orbital mechanics; the signal-to-noise scaling is the argument behind Fig. 5.1 written out; the Stommel, seesaw and Imbrie–Imbrie relations are the published conceptual models named in the right-hand column, used on the teaching pages to make Chapter 5's qualitative statements quantitative; and the regression is Chapter 5's own detection and attribution formulation, of which Fig. 5.43 is the worked illustration.

Part V uses ΔQ = 3.7 W m⁻² for a CO₂ doubling and λ0 = −3.2 W m⁻² K⁻¹, following AR5 and the values Goosse quotes from Soden and Held (2006). The textbook's own worked examples use 3.8 for both, so its climate sensitivities come out slightly larger. Equations 4.6, 4.8, 4.9, 4.11 and 4.12 are intermediate steps and are folded into the entries above rather than listed separately.
