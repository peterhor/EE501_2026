What the surface does to the radiation

Albedo is the first thing that happens to sunlight and the most powerful single control on local climate. But it is not a fixed number for a surface — it depends on how wet the ground is, how high the Sun is, and, in a canopy, on how many times a photon can bounce before it escapes.

Albedo and emissivity

The range every natural surface occupies

Emissivity = absorptivity. A surface that radiates infrared efficiently absorbs it just as efficiently — Kirchhoff’s law, at the same wavelength. So ε is doing double duty in the radiative budget: it sets FIR↑ through εσTs4, and it sets how much of FIR↓ the surface actually takes in.

FIR↑ total upward longwave flux leaving the surface, W m−2

1 − ε the fraction of incoming longwave the surface reflects rather than absorbs

Notice how much narrower the emissivity range is. Albedo spans 0.03 to 0.95 — a factor of thirty. Emissivity spans 0.71 to 0.99, and almost everything living sits above 0.95. In the longwave, nature is close to a blackbody and the differences barely matter; in the shortwave, the surface has far more influence. That asymmetry is why land-cover change is such a powerful climate forcing.

The two exceptions are worth remembering. Desert sand and dry snow are the natural surfaces with notably low emissivity, around 0.84–0.90. Both radiate less than a blackbody at their temperature would — which slightly limits their night-time cooling, and slightly raises the daytime skin temperature they need in order to shed the same energy.

Albedo is not a constant

Albedo against solar altitude

Six canopies, measured through the day. The Sun low in the sky is reflected far more strongly than the Sun overhead — and how much more depends on the structure of the canopy.

Two effects, working together. At a low solar altitude the beam strikes leaves at a glancing angle, where the Fresnel reflection off the waxy leaf surface is much stronger. And the beam penetrates less deeply into the canopy, so a photon has fewer opportunities to be trapped in the gaps and absorbed on a second or third encounter.

The steepness is the structural signature. Short grass is nearly flat — a dense, shallow, more or less closed surface, so angle hardly matters. Pine falls from 0.20 to 0.08 across the same range, because a tall open conifer canopy is full of deep gaps that swallow a high Sun completely and barely exist for a low one. This is also why the same forest has a measurably higher daily-mean albedo in winter at high latitude, before you add any snow at all.

Question. Two of these curves are the same oak stand, in leaf and bare. The bare canopy has the lower albedo at every solar altitude. Why does putting leaves on a tree make it brighter, when a single leaf is darker than a bare twig is?

Inside the canopy

How far light gets in

Radiation is absorbed layer by layer as it works down through the leaves. The controlling quantity is not height but cumulative leaf area — the LAI accumulated above you.

F(L)  =  F0 · e−kL   Beer–Lambert, with L the cumulative leaf area index from the top
F(L) radiation flux surviving below cumulative leaf area L, W m−2
F0 flux arriving at the top of the canopy, W m−2
k extinction coefficient of the canopy, dimensionless (≈0.5 for randomly oriented leaves)
L cumulative leaf area index measured down from the top, m2 leaf per m2 ground

The floor of a forest is lit almost entirely by diffuse light. Direct beam has to find a clear line through every layer of leaves and is extinguished fast. Diffuse light arrives from all directions at once, is scattered rather than blocked, and survives much better. In a typical stand the diffuse share rises from about 15 % above the canopy to about 46 % at the floor — the light that reaches the understorey is not just dimmer, it is a different kind of light, and much greener, because leaves transmit and reflect strongly in the green and near-infrared while absorbing red and blue.

Set the canopy height to 0.2 m and read it as the grass stand from the lecture. The same curve applies: Fsolar falls off through the stand and net radiation FNR follows it closely, because within a canopy the longwave terms are nearly in balance between neighbouring leaves. Almost all of the absorption — and therefore almost all of the surface’s energy exchange with the atmosphere — happens in the upper part of the foliage. In a tall stand, the ‘active surface’ is not the ground at all; it sits several metres up in the crown.

Why a canopy is darker than its own leaves

A single leaf reflects about 10–15 % of visible light. A closed canopy of the same leaves reflects less. A photon reflected off one leaf usually hits another one rather than escaping upward, and each encounter takes another bite. The deeper and more open the structure, the more encounters, and the darker the canopy looks from above — which is why taller vegetation generally has a lower albedo: grass 0.20–0.26, crops 0.18–0.25, deciduous forest 0.15–0.20, conifers 0.05–0.15.

This is a real climate feedback and not a curiosity. Boreal conifer forest is one of the darkest natural surfaces on Earth; the tundra it is currently advancing into is one of the brightest in winter, because tundra is buried under snow while trees stand above it. Shrubs and trees moving north therefore warm the local climate directly, through albedo, quite separately from any carbon they store.

The microclimate a canopy builds

Profiles through a spruce forest, sunny July day, 57°N

Mean hourly profiles of temperature, vapour pressure, wind speed and CO2 concentration. Average tree height 11.4 m; the shaded silhouette is foliage density.

Everything happens in the crown, not at the ground. The temperature and vapour-pressure profiles both bulge where the foliage density peaks, around 7 m, because that is where the radiation is being absorbed and where the transpiration is happening. The active surface has been lifted off the soil and suspended in the air.

The wind profile is the reason the canopy can do that. Above the trees the wind is strong and the exchange with the atmosphere is vigorous; inside, it collapses almost to zero. The trunk space is aerodynamically sheltered, which decouples it from the air above — and that decoupling is what lets the two remaining profiles develop.

CO2 tells you where the biology is. The concentration is highest right at the ground, where soil respiration is releasing it, and falls steadily upward as photosynthesis in the crown draws it down. On a still night the profile is far stronger still, because the respired CO2 has nowhere to go — the same stable stratification that traps cold air near the surface traps the CO2 with it.
