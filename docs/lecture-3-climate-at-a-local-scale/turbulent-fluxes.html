Sensible and latent heat: what the formulas actually say

FSH and FLH carry most of the energy away from the surface, and both are written the same way: a transfer efficiency, times the wind, times a difference between the surface and the air. Move the four things you can measure and watch the fluxes follow.

The two bulk formulas

Identical in structure. Only the gradient being transported, and the constant in front of it, are different.

FSH  =  ρ · cp · ch · Ua · (Ts − Ta)   [W m−2]
FSH sensible heat flux, W m−2
ρ air density, about 1.2 kg m−3 near sea level
cp specific heat of dry air, 1004 J K−1 kg−1
ch bulk transfer coefficient for heat — the surface's aerodynamic grip on the air, dimensionless
Ua wind speed at the reference level (10 m), m s−1
Ts − Ta surface-to-air temperature difference, air taken at 2 m, K
FLH  =  ρ · Lv · cL · Ua · (qs − qa)   [W m−2]
FLH latent heat flux, W m−2
ρ air density, about 1.2 kg m−3 near sea level
Lv latent heat of vaporisation, 2.50×106 J kg−1 at 0 °C
cL bulk transfer coefficient for water vapour, dimensionless (usually taken equal to ch)
Ua wind speed at the reference level (10 m), m s−1
qs − qa specific humidity difference between the surface and 2 m, kg kg−1

Three ingredients each, and all three must be non-zero. No wind, no flux — however hot the surface is. No gradient, no flux — however hard the wind blows. And a rough surface moves far more heat than a smooth one at the same wind speed and the same temperature difference, because ch is larger.

Where the sign changes

The Bowen ratio as a function of the two gradients. Lines through the origin are contours of constant Bo.

The Bowen ratio does not care about wind or roughness. Divide the two formulas and, as long as heat and vapour are carried by the same eddies (ch = cL), ρ, c and Ua all cancel:
Bo  =  (cp / Lv) · (Ts − Ta) / (qs − qa)   ≈   0.40 · ΔT [K] / Δq [g kg−1]
Bo Bowen ratio, dimensionless
cp specific heat of dry air, 1004 J K−1 kg−1
Lv latent heat of vaporisation, 2.50×106 J kg−1
Ts − Ta surface-to-air temperature difference, ΔT, K
qs − qa surface-to-air specific humidity difference, Δq, kg kg−1
0.40 the value of cp/Lv once Δq is expressed in g kg−1
Blow harder and both fluxes grow together. Move to a rougher surface and both grow together. The split is set by nothing except the ratio of the two gradients — which is another way of saying it is set by how much water the surface can supply. Check it on the sliders: change Ua or ch and watch Bo refuse to move.

Question. A surface has ΔT = 8 K. How large does Δq have to be before evaporation takes more energy than heating the air? What kind of surface supplies that much water vapour, and what kind cannot?

The oasis effect

How FLH can exceed FNR

A small moist patch inside a dry landscape — an oasis, an irrigated field, a city park in August.

Follow the temperature difference. The wet patch evaporates hard, so it stays cool — cooler than the hot dry air arriving from upwind. That makes Ts − Ta negative, so FSH reverses and the air starts delivering heat to the surface. The surface now has two energy supplies, radiation and advection, and can evaporate more than the radiation alone could ever pay for. FLH > FNR, and Bo is negative.

It only works because the patch is small. The extra energy is being carried in horizontally from the surrounding dry land. Irrigate the whole desert and the upwind air becomes cool and humid, the advection stops, and the oasis effect disappears. This is a genuinely local phenomenon — it cannot appear in a global energy budget, because globally there is no upwind.

Preset Irrigated field above puts you in this state: ΔT negative, Δq large, Bo ≈ −0.13.

Heat storage in the surface layer

The fourth term

Not everything leaves. Some of the net radiation simply warms up a thin layer of the surface itself — and how thin that layer is, is a property of the material.

heat storage  =  ρ cground · hsu · δTs/δt   [W m−2]
heat storage rate at which energy accumulates in the surface layer, W m−2
ρ cground volumetric heat capacity of that layer, J m−3 K−1
hsu thickness of the surface layer that participates, m

This is why deserts get so hot. Dry sand has a low volumetric heat capacity and a low diffusivity, so the absorbed energy is trapped in a very thin layer and has nowhere to go but up in temperature. Set the material to dry sand and hsu to 5 cm and read the last box: a modest 100 W m−2 heats it more than 5 K every hour. Do the same to wet clay 30 cm deep and it barely moves.

And why snow behaves so strangely. Fresh snow has the lowest heat capacity on the list. A thin snow layer changes temperature very fast — but it also insulates whatever is beneath it almost completely, which is why the soil under a snowpack stays near 0 °C all winter while the snow surface goes to −30 °C.

The transfer coefficient spans two orders of magnitude. The lecture quotes a range of 5 × 10−5 to 1 × 10−3, which covers smooth surfaces — open water, snow, short grass. A forest canopy is aerodynamically far rougher and sits an order of magnitude above that, which is why a forest can move several hundred watts per square metre on a light breeze and a lake cannot.
