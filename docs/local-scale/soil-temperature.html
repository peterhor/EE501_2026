Heat in the ground

The surface heats and cools every day, and that signal travels downward into the soil as a wave — getting weaker and arriving later the deeper it goes. How fast it fades is a property of the material, and it explains everything from why the Sahara cools 35 K overnight to why the ground under snow never freezes.

One equation, two consequences

Drive a semi-infinite solid with a sinusoidal surface temperature and the solution is a travelling, decaying wave.

T(z, t)  =  T̄  +  A0 · e−z/D · sin(ωt − z/D)
D  =  √(2κ / ω)      κ  =  k / C   the damping depth, and the thermal diffusivity behind it
A0 amplitude of the surface temperature wave [K]  ·  ω angular frequency, 2π/24 h for the daily cycle and 2π/365 d for the annual one
κ thermal diffusivity [m2 s−1]  ·  k thermal conductivity [W m−1 K−1]  ·  C volumetric heat capacity [J m−3 K−1]

The same D appears twice, and that is the whole story. e−z/D says the wave loses 63 % of its amplitude in every damping depth. −z/D inside the sine says it also falls one radian further behind in that same distance — about 3.8 hours for a daily wave. Weaker and later, locked together.

Three things this explains

1 · Why the Sahara surface reaches 64 °C and 27 °C on the same day

Central Sahara, summer. Surface, air at 2 m, and dry sand at 0.3 m and 0.75 m.

Dry sand has a damping depth of about 8 cm. At 0.3 m that is nearly four damping depths, so the daily wave arrives with 2 % of its amplitude — the 0.3 m line is flat, exactly as measured. All of the day’s energy is therefore crammed into a layer a few centimetres thick, and a thin layer with a low heat capacity has no choice but to get extremely hot. At night the same thinness works in reverse: there is almost no stored heat to draw on, so radiative cooling drops the surface 35 K before dawn.

Now notice which line is on top, and when. By day the surface is far hotter than the air — strongly unstable, deep convection, the boundary layer grows to kilometres. Between about 19:00 and 07:00 the surface is colder than the air at 2 m: a temperature inversion, convectively stable, turbulence suppressed. FSH reverses and points downward through those hours.

2 · Why the soil is warmer than the surface in winter

The annual wave, same physics, 365 times slower — so the damping depth is about 19 times larger.

Switch the control above to Annual cycle. The damping depth jumps from centimetres to metres, and the phase lag from hours to months. At 5 m in a moist soil the annual maximum arrives in autumn and the minimum in spring — a full season out of step with the surface. So in midwinter, when the surface is at its coldest, the soil a metre or two down is still carrying last summer’s heat. The conduction flux runs upward all winter, and downward all summer, on exactly the same logic as the day/night reversal but a hundred times slower.

Question. Set the annual cycle to saturated clay and read the phase lag per metre. How deep do you have to go before the ground is warmest in January?

3 · Why snow keeps the ground from freezing

Fresh snow has one of the lowest thermal conductivities in the table below — 0.08 W m−1 K−1, beaten only by dry peat, and some thirty-five times lower than rock or twenty times lower than saturated sand. It is mostly still air held in place. That means a snowpack transmits almost no heat: the snow surface can sit at −30 °C while the soil underneath stays within a degree of 0 °C, insulated from the atmosphere entirely. Plants, soil microbes and small mammals all depend on it, and it is why a winter with little snow can be far harder on an ecosystem than a cold one with deep snow.

Note the trap in the numbers: fresh snow has a fairly high diffusivity (κ = 0.38 × 10−6 m2 s−1, higher than any of the dry soils here) because its heat capacity is so tiny. Diffusivity tells you how fast a temperature signal propagates; conductivity tells you how much energy travels with it. Snow passes the signal on readily and the energy hardly at all — so its damping depth is respectable while its insulating power is extreme. Read both columns, never just one.

Wet soil conducts heat far better than dry soil. Water fills the pores that were holding still air, and both k and C go up — but k rises faster, so κ rises too. Saturating a sandy soil roughly triples its diffusivity and doubles its damping depth. The daily wave then reaches twice as deep, the heat is spread through twice as much material, and the surface temperature swing is correspondingly smaller. That is a large part of why a moist soil has a gentle diurnal range and a dry one is extreme.

Thermal properties after Oke, Boundary Layer Climates (2nd edn, 1987), Table 2.1; κ is computed here as k / C so the table is internally consistent, and may differ in the last digit from the printed κ column. The analytic solution assumes a uniform, semi-infinite, dry solid with a purely sinusoidal surface forcing — real soils have layers, moisture that moves, and freeze–thaw. Applied to still water and to snow it describes conduction only, and both in reality also transport heat by convection and by vapour movement.
