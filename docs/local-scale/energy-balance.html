Where the energy goes at the surface

The global energy budget tells you what the planet does on average. Stand on one patch of ground and the numbers look nothing like it — and what they look like is decided almost entirely by what that patch is made of. Eight real measurement sites, the same two equations.

Two equations, in order

The first says how much radiative energy the surface is left holding. The second says what it does with it. They are joined at FNR.

1 · Radiative budget
FNR  =  Fsolar − αFsolar  +  FIR↓ − FIR↑   [W m−2]
2 · Energy balance
FNR  =  FSH  +  FLH  +  Fcond  +  heat storage   [W m−2]

Sign convention used here. Positive means energy arriving at the surface for the radiative terms, and energy leaving the surface for FSH, FLH and Fcond. So a positive FNR is a surface being loaded with energy, and a positive FSH is that energy being handed to the air. Both flip sign at night.

The Bowen ratio

How the surface splits its turbulent loss

Bo  =  FSH / FLH

All eight sites on one axis. The scale is logarithmic in |Bo|, because the spread runs over three orders of magnitude — and it crosses zero, which an isolated surface is not supposed to be able to do.

Bo > 1 means the sensible heat flux exceeds the latent heat flux: the surface has more energy available than it has water to evaporate. Bo < 0 means FSH and FLH point in opposite directions — the air is delivering heat to the surface, and the surface is spending it on evaporation. That is the oasis effect, and it is the only way FLH can exceed FNR.

Question. The irrigated field and the Antarctic snow both have a negative Bowen ratio, for entirely different reasons. What is different about the sign of FNR in the two cases — and what is each surface doing with the sensible heat it takes from the air?

Day and night

The same surface, twelve hours apart

At night Fsolar is zero, so the entire shortwave half of the radiative budget disappears and FNR becomes a small negative number set purely by the two longwave terms. Everything downstream flips with it.

Question. By day the surface records the highest temperature in the profile; by night the lowest. Why is it the surface both times, rather than the air at 1.2 m or the soil at 0.2 m?

Read down the albedo column, then down FNR. The Antarctic snow receives 26 MJ of sunlight — slightly less than the Canadian grass, over 21 hours of daylight — and ends the day with less than nothing, because four fifths of it is reflected straight back while the longwave loss carries on regardless. The desert receives the most sunlight of all and still ends up with half the net radiation of a moist bare soil.

Then read FSH against FLH. The pine and the fir stand sit within a few degrees of latitude of each other, in the same month, with albedos of 0.08 and 0.09 — and they split their energy the opposite way round. Nothing in the radiative budget predicts that. It is decided by how much water each stand can reach.

Every one of these is a property of the surface, not of the atmosphere above it. That is why local climate can change across a fence line — and why changing land cover changes climate without changing anything at all about the radiation arriving from the Sun.

Take it further

The same budget, hour by hour — the live explorer

Everything on this page is a daily total. The explorer opens the same four radiative terms out across the day.

Pick a latitude and a date and it computes Fsolar from real solar geometry — the same declination and hour-angle engine behind the insolation animation — then lets you drop six different surfaces underneath it: desert, moist soil and oasis, snow and ice, grass and crop, coniferous forest, urban pavement. Each has its own albedo, emissivity, atmospheric emission and thermal lag, so you can watch the identical incoming energy partition completely differently.

Three things to do with it in front of a class. Put the site at 45°N in midsummer and step through all six surfaces without touching anything else — only the surface changes, and FNR changes with it. Then switch to snow and note that FNR can sit near or below zero through a whole sunlit day. Then compare desert against urban pavement and watch the thermal lag push FIR↑ hours past sunset — the urban heat island, visible as a phase shift.

What it shows and what it does not. The explorer plots the four radiative terms and their sum. It stops at FNR; the turbulent and conduction terms that spend it are the subject of page 2 and page 3. Its surface temperature is a lagged cosine rather than a solved thermal model, and it assumes a fixed atmospheric transmissivity of 0.75 — so read it for the mechanism, and read the eight measured sites above for the numbers.
