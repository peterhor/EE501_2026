Forced and internal variability

Lecture 6 asked what the climate does when you push it. This lecture asks a harder question: when you look at a climate record, how do you know whether anything pushed it at all? Part of what a record contains is a response to something outside the system. The rest the system did by itself. This page is about telling those apart — and about why that is far easier for the globe than for Bø.

Two kinds of variation

The distinction runs through the whole lecture, and it is a distinction about causes, not about size.

Forced variability is driven by a change in an external forcing — something that acts on the climate system without being part of it. Insolation, volcanic aerosol, the CO2 humans put in the air.

Because the cause sits outside the system, you can in principle name it. Forced variability has an ultimate cause: the eruption happened, the orbit changed, the emissions occurred.

Internal variability is generated by the interactions between the components of the system — atmosphere with ocean, ocean with sea ice, all of it with the land surface. No external push is required. It happens in a model with every forcing held perfectly constant.

Here there is no ultimate cause to find. You can trace the chain of events — the trade winds slackened, so the thermocline flattened, so the eastern Pacific warmed — and that chain is the proximate cause. Asking what caused the first link is not a well-posed question.

The test. Can the climate system change it by responding? If it cannot, it is a forcing. This is the same test used in Lecture 6, page 1 — and it carries the same awkward rider. The boundary is a choice. Treat the ice sheets as part of the system and a glacial cycle is an internally amplified response; treat them as fixed and the same ice sheets are a boundary condition. Same ice, same physics, different question. You will meet this again on page 3, where it decides what the astronomical theory is actually claiming.

So where does a feedback belong? Neither category. Forced variability is the input, internal variability is what the system does unprompted, and feedbacks set the gain on both. A world with strong feedbacks has both a large forced response and loud internal variability — which is precisely why the two are hard to separate.

Why internal variability exists at all

One sentence of dynamics, with three consequences that run the rest of the lecture.

The equations of the atmosphere and ocean — the seven you met in Lecture 5 — are non-linear. Systems of this kind are sensitive to small perturbations: two states differing by an amount too small to measure will, after a while, differ by an amount as large as the variability itself. Nothing has to push the system for it to wander. The wandering is what the equations do.

Drag it and watch where the curves separate. Making the starting error a thousand times smaller does not buy a thousand times more forecast — it buys a fixed extra interval, because the error grows exponentially. That is why weather forecasts have a horizon, and why it is about two weeks whatever the observing network costs.

Three consequences, all of them about what you are entitled to expect.

  • Weather forecasts have a finite horizon. It is a property of the equations, not of the computer.
  • Two simulations of the same model with the same forcing are not the same simulation. They contain different realisations of internal variability. Neither is wrong. This is why modern experiments are run as ensembles.
  • A model is not expected to reproduce the timing of an unforced event. If a model puts a cold decade in the 1690s and the record puts it in the 1450s, that is not by itself a model failure — it is the reason Lecture 5’s validation compares statistics rather than dates. You will see this used in earnest on page 5.
What chaos does not mean. An unpredictable trajectory can still have a perfectly predictable climate. You cannot say what the weather in Bø will be on 3 March 2043; you can say a great deal about the distribution it will be drawn from. Climate projection is a boundary-value problem, weather forecasting an initial-value problem, and they fail for different reasons.

Signal and noise

Same forced trend everywhere. Only the amount of internal variability changes. Watch how long it takes before you could tell the trend was there.

The magnitude of internal variability is a strong function of the spatial and temporal scale you look at. Averaging over a large area cancels variability that is regional and out of phase; averaging over many years cancels variability that is fast. The forced signal survives both averagings, because it is the same sign everywhere and it accumulates. That asymmetry is the entire basis of detecting climate change — and it is why the global mean temperature curve is the famous one.

Now raise the trend to 0.6 K per decade — roughly what the Arctic is actually doing — and it reappears. The Arctic is both the noisiest place on the planet and the fastest-warming, and which of those wins depends entirely on how many years you have.
Read the arithmetic, not just the picture. The noise falls as 1/√n with the length of the averaging window, while the signal grows linearly with time. Signal-to-noise therefore grows like t 3/2 for a trend measured over its own record. Long records are worth disproportionately more than good ones — which is the argument for palaeoclimate, and the subject of the rest of this lecture.

How loud is internal variability, really?

Representative standard deviations of the annual mean surface air temperature in unforced control simulations. These are the numbers behind the presets above.

Scaleσ (K)Why
Global mean~0.1 Almost everything cancels. What is left is mostly ENSO redistributing heat between ocean and atmosphere.
Hemispheric~0.15 Slightly less cancellation, and the two hemispheres are not in phase.
Continental (Europe)~0.4–0.5 One circulation regime covers the whole area. A blocked winter is blocked everywhere at once.

The pattern is the point. Variability is loudest exactly where the local feedbacks are strongest — the sea-ice margin, the upwelling zones, the monsoon fringe. The same amplification that makes those regions respond strongly to a forcing also makes them noisy without one. You cannot buy sensitivity without buying noise, and Lecture 6, page 4 explains why.

Every process has a timescale, and that is how you tell them apart

Forcings and internal processes occupy different bands. If a record has power at 41,000 years, that is not the ocean.

How to use this diagram. It is the lecture’s table of contents, and it is also a diagnostic. Reading a record, ask first what is the timescale of the variation, then look at the bar chart for what can possibly operate there. A 41-kyr cycle can only be obliquity: nothing internal to the climate system has a clock that slow and that precise. A 3–7 year oscillation cannot be orbital: nothing external varies that fast. The ambiguous band — decades to centuries — is exactly where the last millennium sits, and exactly why page 6 needs a formal method rather than an eyeball.

Question to hold onto. Solar variability and the AMO both have power near 60–90 years. One is a forcing and one is internal. From a single temperature record, could you tell which produced an observed 80-year swing? No — and that is why attribution uses spatial fingerprints rather than timing.
