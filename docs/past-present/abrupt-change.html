Abrupt change and the ocean circulation

Page 3 dealt with a forcing that takes twenty thousand years to change sign. This page deals with responses that take twenty years. Nothing external moves that fast, so the speed has to come from inside the system — and that means a threshold. This is the part of the record that changed how the field thinks about what climate can do.

Dansgaard–Oeschger events

The observation that started it, and the four features of it that a mechanism has to explain.

Greenland ice cores through the last glacial period contain about twenty-five abrupt warmings. Each begins with a jump of roughly 8–16 K in Greenland surface temperature within a few decades — in some cases within a single decade — followed by a gradual cooling over centuries to a few thousand years, and then an abrupt return to the cold state. They are named after Willi Dansgaard and Hans Oeschger, and they are numbered.

FeatureWhat it rules out
Enormous local amplitude — 8 to 16 K Any explanation in which Greenland simply follows a global mean. This is a regional redistribution, and something must be moving a great deal of heat.
Extreme speed — decades Ice sheets (too slow), orbital forcing (far too slow), CO2 (it barely moves across a D–O event). Only the atmosphere, sea ice and the ocean’s upper circulation are fast enough.
Sawtooth asymmetry — fast on, slow off Any linear response to a symmetric forcing. Asymmetry in time means non-linearity, exactly as on page 3.
Only during glacials A mechanism that does not care about the background state. Whatever it is, it needs ice sheets, or a cold North Atlantic, or both. The Holocene has nothing like it.

And a fifth feature, which is the strongest clue of all. The signal is not confined to Greenland. It appears, with the right timing, in Chinese cave deposits (as monsoon strength), in South American speleothems, in the tropical Atlantic (as a shift of the rain belt), and — crucially, and with a different shape — in Antarctic ice. A mechanism has to explain the pattern, not just the Greenland record.

Heinrich events, and the mechanism they point to

A second kind of event, rarer and larger, that leaves an unambiguous physical trace.

North Atlantic sediment cores contain a handful of thick layers of coarse rock debris that no ocean current could have carried there. The only plausible delivery mechanism is icebergs: rock ground off the bed by an ice sheet, rafted out to sea, and dropped as the ice melted. These are Heinrich events — episodes of massive iceberg discharge, mainly from the Laurentide ice sheet through Hudson Strait, occurring roughly every 5–10 kyr during the last glacial.

They matter here for one reason: an armada of icebergs is a delivery of fresh water to precisely the part of the ocean where deep water forms. And fresh water is exactly what the deep circulation cannot tolerate.

The chain of reasoning to hold onto. Icebergs → fresh water in the North Atlantic → the surface there becomes less dense → it stops sinking → the overturning circulation weakens or stops → the northward heat transport it carries stops with it → the North Atlantic region cools sharply while the Southern Ocean, no longer giving heat away, warms. Every link is ordinary physics. The interesting part is that the middle step has a threshold.

What the overturning circulation actually is

Enough of it to understand why it can have two states.

  • Density is set by temperature and salinity. Cold water is dense; salty water is dense. In the North Atlantic both work in the same direction, and the surface water becomes dense enough to sink to great depth.
  • The circulation carries heat. Warm surface water flows north, releases heat to the atmosphere, sinks, and returns south at depth. The northward heat transport in the Atlantic is of order 1 PW — comparable to the entire anthropogenic energy imbalance many times over, delivered to one region.
  • The circulation carries salt, too, and that is what makes it interesting. Northward flow brings salty subtropical water into the North Atlantic, which keeps the surface dense, which keeps the flow going. A positive feedback — and by the sign rule of Lecture 6, a self-amplifying one.
  • Which means it can fail. Weaken the flow enough and less salt arrives; less salt means less dense surface water; less dense surface water means still weaker flow. Beyond a threshold, the loop runs away and the circulation shuts down — and then the same feedback holds it shut.
You have seen this shape before. A positive feedback strong enough to create two stable states, with an abrupt jump between them and a different threshold in each direction. That is exactly the structure of PC Lab 3, where vegetation and precipitation in the Sahara feed each other. The physics is unrelated; the mathematics is identical. That is worth noticing, because it means the behaviour is a property of the feedback structure, not of the ocean.

The demonstration this page is built around

The two states of the conveyor

The Stommel two-box model — two equations, and every qualitative feature of the real thing. Push fresh water into the northern box and watch what the circulation does about it.

q = k(αΔT − βΔS)     V d(ΔS)/dt = 2H − |q| ΔS
q is the overturning strength; ΔT and ΔS the low-minus-high-latitude temperature and salinity differences; H the freshwater flux to the northern box. Temperature is held at its equilibrium value, because the atmosphere restores it far faster than the ocean can change it.

Read the two equations against each other. The first says the flow is driven by density, and salt opposes temperature. The second says the flow itself removes the salinity contrast that is opposing it. A strong circulation flushes fresh water away and keeps itself strong; a weak one lets fresh water accumulate and gets weaker. That is the whole model, and it is enough.

1 Sv = 106 m3 s−1. Present-day net export of fresh water to the region is of order 0.1 Sv; a Heinrich event delivers several tenths on top of that.

Experiment
The two things to take from the sweep experiment. First, the collapse happens at a freshwater flux far above the one at which the circulation will restart — the path up is not the path down. That gap is hysteresis, and it is why the collapsed state persists after the icebergs have gone. Second, look at the rate: the forcing is changing smoothly and slowly throughout, and the response contains a near-vertical jump. An abrupt change does not require an abrupt cause.
What this model is and is not. Two boxes, one salinity equation, no geography, no wind, no eddies, no sea ice. It is a demonstration that the structure permits two states and a threshold — nothing more. The real threshold is not known to a factor of two; comprehensive models disagree with each other about how close the present circulation is to it, and some show no bistability at all under present conditions. Treat the number 0.35 Sv as an illustration, and treat anyone who quotes a precise threshold for the real ocean with suspicion.

The bipolar seesaw

Why the Antarctic record of the same events looks so different — and why that difference is the best evidence that the ocean is the mechanism.

Greenland and Antarctic ice cores both record the millennial events, but they do not look alike. Greenland has sharp sawteeth; Antarctica has smooth, rounded, smaller undulations that reach their maximum at the moment Greenland jumps. Stocker and Johnsen (2003) showed that a single idea explains this: if the overturning circulation transports heat northward across the equator, then when it shuts down that heat stays in the south. The Southern Ocean is not a mirror of the north — it is a reservoir being filled and drained, so it responds to the time integral of the northern signal, not to the signal itself.

dTS/dt  =  (−TN − TS) / τ
the southern temperature relaxes towards the negative of the northern one, with a reservoir time constant τ of order 1000 years
Set it very short and the two records become mirror images. Set it long and the southern signal turns into a smooth integral.
Dansgaard–Oeschger events recur at intervals loosely clustered around 1500 years.
Check the phase relationship yourself. Antarctic warming happens while Greenland is cold, and Antarctic temperature peaks at the instant Greenland warms. That is not what a common global forcing would produce, and it is not what an atmospheric teleconnection would produce either. It is what a heat reservoir on the other side of a broken conveyor produces. This phasing, observed in precisely dated ice cores from both poles, is why the ocean-circulation explanation is accepted.

The deglaciation: everything on this page, happening at once

From 21,000 years ago to now

The best-observed large climate change in the record, and a rehearsal of every mechanism in this lecture.

  • It starts with insolation. Northern summer insolation at 65°N rises from its minimum around 21 ka. Check that on page 3: it is a rise of some 50 W m−2 in summer at high northern latitude. The northern ice sheets begin to retreat.
  • Then the Southern Ocean and CO2 take over. Antarctic temperature and atmospheric CO2 rise almost synchronously from about 18 ka, and the global mean follows CO2, not northern insolation. Compilations of proxies from all latitudes show the global mean lagging Antarctic temperature and CO2, which is the signature of a Southern-Ocean-released, CO2-transmitted global signal.
  • Meltwater interrupts it. The retreating ice sheets deliver enormous freshwater fluxes to the North Atlantic. The circulation weakens; the north cools while the south keeps warming — the seesaw, running in real time.
  • The Younger Dryas. Between about 12.9 and 11.7 ka the North Atlantic region returns almost to glacial conditions for more than a thousand years, then exits in a matter of decades. It is the last, and best dated, of the abrupt events.
  • Then it stops. Once the ice sheets are gone there is no more meltwater, and the Holocene has no D–O events at all. The mechanism did not disappear; the fuel did.
The lead–lag argument, done properly. You will meet the claim that “CO2 lags temperature, so CO2 cannot cause warming”. The premise is partly true and the conclusion does not follow. At the start of a deglaciation, orbital forcing and Southern Ocean changes release CO2 first — CO2 is a feedback there, as page 3 said. But once released it is a forcing on everything else, and the global mean temperature lags it. A quantity can be caused by something and cause something else; that is what an amplifier is. The modern situation is different again, since the CO2 is being added from outside the system — which is precisely the definition of a forcing from page 1.

Why a lecture on the past ends up talking about the future

The transferable result, stated carefully.

What the record establishesWhat it does not establish
The climate system contains thresholds, and has crossed them That any particular threshold is near today
Crossing one produces regional changes of ~10 K in decades That a future crossing would look like a past one; the background state is different
The freshwater balance of the North Atlantic is the relevant control The value of the threshold, which models disagree about by a large factor
Recovery is not the reverse of collapse — hysteresis is real How long a recovery would take, or whether the same two states exist in a warm world

The honest summary for a modern audience. Greenland and West Antarctic meltwater is freshening the North Atlantic now, and the observed circulation has probably weakened. Comprehensive models project further weakening through this century and mostly do not project a collapse; they also disagree with each other and with the palaeo record about how easily the real ocean switches states, which is itself a reason for caution rather than comfort. The palaeoclimate contribution is not a prediction. It is the demonstration that the possibility is physical.

What to carry to page 5. Everything on this page happened during a glacial or a deglaciation. The next page asks what the climate does in a world that looks like ours — no ice sheets to collapse, no meltwater, no D–O events. The amplitudes get much smaller, and the problem of telling forced from internal gets correspondingly harder.
