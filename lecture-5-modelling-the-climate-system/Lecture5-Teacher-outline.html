Modelling the climate system — lecture outline

A running order across the two slots the topic occupies — Thursday 10 September 08:15–12:00 and Monday 14 September 10:15–12:00 — with the point to make at each step, the number to put on the board, which interactive page to open, and the question to throw back at the room. Built from Lecture 5 — Modelling the climate system and the five teaching pages that go with it.

The one sentence the whole session is built on

A climate model is equations plus a way of solving them — and almost everything that goes wrong lives in the second half. The equations are settled physics. The grid, the time step and the parameterisations are choices, and every disagreement between models is a disagreement about those choices.

Say it in the first five minutes and then land it four times: resolution decides what the model can see; parameterisation decides what it does about everything else; complexity is a trade against duration, not a measure of quality; and evaluation is what separates a model from an opinion.

The second thread, worth naming explicitly once. Every earlier lecture asked “what does the climate system do?” This one asks “how would you find out, given that you cannot experiment on the planet?” That shift — from describing the system to building an instrument for reasoning about it — is the point of the week, and it is what makes the three PC labs make sense rather than being toy programs.

The trap to head off early. Students arrive believing either that models are basically right or that they are basically made up. Both are wrong in the same way: they treat the model as a single object with a single trustworthiness. The useful habit is to ask, of any given result, which part of the model produced it — resolved dynamics, a parameterisation, or a prescribed boundary condition. A model can be excellent at one and hopeless at another in the same simulation.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the two sessions students should be able to:

  • State what a climate model is, in Goosse’s two-part definition, and say why the second part matters.
  • Explain why the equations must be solved numerically, on a grid, in time steps.
  • Say what a given grid spacing can and cannot resolve, using the L/4 rule of thumb.
  • Derive the cost scaling Δx−3 and explain where the third power comes from.
  • Define parameterisation and give three examples with the quantity each one predicts.
  • Distinguish a boundary condition from an external forcing, and say why the distinction limits what a run can answer.
  • Place EBM, EMIC and GCM on the complexity–duration trade and choose the right one for a stated question.
  • Write down and solve the 0-D energy balance model, both at equilibrium and as a time-dependent equation.
  • Explain what heat capacity does to a model run, and why coupled models need a long spin-up.
  • List the seven equations of an atmospheric GCM and say what each conserves.
  • Describe the four components and what the coupler exchanges between them.
  • Explain why sea-ice growth is self-limiting and what snow does to it.
  • Explain why a GCM grid box is not a place, and compare dynamical and statistical downscaling.
  • Distinguish verification, validation and calibration, and say why calibration is the dangerous one.
  • Compute a bias and an RMSE, and say what each one hides.
  • Explain why the multi-model mean beats nearly every member, and why more members eventually stop helping.
  • Apply the delta method and state the assumption it rests on.

Session A — Thursday 10 September, 08:15–12:00

Open

Framing: you cannot experiment on the planet

08:15 · 10 min

Points

  • Four lectures of what the system does. Today: how you find out, given that there is one Earth and no control.
  • Put Goosse’s definition on the board and leave it there: a mathematical representation of the climate system derived from physical, biological and chemical principles, and the way the resulting equations are solved.
  • Underline the second clause. Say out loud that nearly all model disagreement lives there, and that the rest of the day is an unpacking of it.
  • Outline the shape: what a model is → how complex to make it → what equations → what components → is it any good.
Warm-up
A weather forecast is useless beyond about ten days. So how can anyone claim to say anything about 2100? Almost every room contains someone who thinks this is a knockout objection, and it is the best possible way in. Get to: weather is an initial value problem and chaotic; climate is a boundary value problem and statistical. You cannot say what the temperature will be in Bø on 3 July 2087; you can say what the distribution of July temperatures will look like, for the same reason you can predict that summer is warmer than winter without forecasting any particular day. Come back to this in Block 2 when the EBM reaches equilibrium regardless of where it started.
Block 1

Grids, time steps and parameterisation

08:25 · 45 min

1 · Why numerically at all

  • State plainly: there is no analytical solution to fluid motion on a rotating sphere with moisture, radiation and mountains. Nobody is hiding a cleverer method.
  • So: continuous fields → values at points; derivatives → differences between neighbours; evolution → a march of finite steps.
  • Draw one cell and its six neighbours. Dynamics is between cells; physics is inside them. That split organises the whole lecture.
Show
Page 1 — the cell-and-column diagram near the top. Left half exact physics approximated numerically; right half approximate physics applied exactly. Say the sentence “model uncertainty lives on the right” and let it sit.

2 · The grid demonstration — the centrepiece of the block

  • Open the demo at 100 km first, so they see the default. Then drag left to 400 km before going right — the loss is more shocking than the gain.
  • Narrate the three things that break, in this order: the summit flattens (no orographic rain, no rain shadow), the island vanishes, the coastline shortens.
  • The point to land: the model is not given a blurry picture of the mountain. It is given a different, lower mountain, and it then does perfectly correct physics on the wrong mountain.
  • Then the resolved/parameterised list underneath. Drag slowly and let them watch badges flip. Stop at 25 km and note that fronts have just become resolvable — that is what a regional model buys.
  • Finish on the last two rows, which never flip. Some parameterisation is permanent, not a temporary embarrassment.
Ask
The model says the grid box containing Bø will warm by 3 degrees. What has it actually told you? That the average of a 100 × 100 km area, whose elevation the model believes to be the mean of everything in it, warms by 3 K. It has said nothing about the valley floor, and nothing about whether the snow line moves past a particular hillside. Park this — Monday’s first block is entirely about it.

3 · Why it is expensive — do this on the board

  • Write cost ∝ NxNyNz / Δt. Ask for the scaling with Δx and let someone say “squared”.
  • Then supply the third power: the CFL condition. Nothing may cross a grid box in one step, so Δt must shrink with Δx too. Halving the grid costs eight times, not four.
  • Computer power doubled every ~1.2 years. Three doublings ≈ 3.6 years buys one halving. Point at the IPCC timeline: sixteen years, factor of four. The history is exactly this arithmetic.
  • Then the Earth Simulator question on the page. The honest answer — that we should be at 1.5 km and are at 100 — is more instructive than the scaling law.

4 · Parameterisation, and the two kinds of input

  • Definition first, then the table. Stress the last column: those parameters are not measured constants, and several describe quantities that only exist once you agree to average over a grid box.
  • The 40 % question: two groups, same equations, same resolution, warming rates differing by 40 % — it is clouds, and it has been clouds in every generation from FAR to AR6.
  • Boundary conditions vs external forcings. Do this quickly but do not skip it: it is the cleanest single idea in the lecture and it recurs on Monday.
  • The line between them moves, and that movement is the history of model development. SST was a boundary condition in the 1970s. Vegetation was a map. Ice sheets still mostly are.
Ask (links to PC Lab 3)
Could a model with vegetation prescribed as a map produce the Sahara collapse you will simulate in Lab 3? No — prescribing vegetation removes the feedback that is the result. The model would run happily and tell you nothing. Good moment to say: a model cannot be wrong about something it was told, and it cannot be right about it either.
break · 10 min
Block 2

The hierarchy, and running a climate model live

09:20 · 55 min

1 · The trade, in one diagram

  • Complexity against affordable duration. Every step up costs roughly two orders of magnitude of simulated years.
  • The sentence to land: the three types are complementary, not competing. A glacial cycle and next decade’s regional rainfall are not answerable by the same model, and no computer fixes that.
  • Then the corrective: complexity is not accuracy. A simple model containing the process that matters beats a complex one whose extra processes you cannot check.

2 · The 0-D EBM — derive on the board, then run it

  • Recall Lecture 2 in thirty seconds only: S0(1−αp)/4 = σTe4 → 255 K. Do not re-derive it; they have a whole page on it.
  • Now add the two things that make it a model. Write C dT/dt = S0(1−αp)/4 − τaσTs4 and name the two new symbols out loud: C introduces time, τa introduces the greenhouse effect as a knob.
  • Set the left side to zero, recover Ts, note τa ≈ 0.61 gives 288 K. That single number is standing in for the entire radiative transfer scheme of a GCM.
Show
Page 2, the live model. Run it in this order and do not rush: (a) start at 265 K, mixed layer, no perturbation — watch it climb to 288 and stop. Point at the e-folding marker. (b) change the start temperature to 320 K — same destination. That is the boundary-value answer to the warm-up question, and this is the moment to say so. (c) switch to whole ocean — equilibrium unmoved, response time 2 yr → 150 yr. (d) apply Double CO2. (e) the volcano, twice: atmosphere-only then whole ocean.
Ask — the key exchange of the day
This model says doubling CO2 gives 1.1 K. Every GCM says 3. Which is wrong? Neither. This model has exactly one feedback — the Planck response — and αp is a number you typed in rather than something the model computes. No water vapour, no ice albedo, no lapse rate, no clouds. The gap between 1.1 and 3 is the entire content of next Tuesday’s lecture, and it is why the hierarchy has more rungs than this one. Write 1.1 and 3 on the board side by side and leave them up.
Ask
Heat capacity changed nothing about where the model ended up. Why should a modelling centre care? Because it changed how long it takes to get there, from 2 years to 150. A run that would spin up in a decade now needs centuries of computer time before the answer means anything — hence the thousand-year spin-ups, and hence model drift, which comes back on Monday as something you have to correct for.

3 · Up the ladder

  • 1-D EBM: latitude bands, and a transport term D that stands in for the whole general circulation. Two things arrive: the ice line, and transport as its competitor. Name both labs here — Lab 1 is a 0-D EBM with biology, Lab 2 is this equation made runnable.
  • EMIC: Goosse’s defining property is that it always keeps the geography. Give up the 3-D atmosphere, keep the ocean and the carbon cycle, buy ten thousand years and large ensembles.
  • GCM and ESM: the AOGCM/ESM distinction is exactly the boundary-condition-versus-prognostic line from Block 1 — concentration-driven versus emission-driven.
  • Finish on the development-history matrix. Each new row is a new way to be wrong and a new question you can ask. The carbon cycle example is the one to tell: coupling it revealed that sinks weaken as climate warms, which was invisible until the component existed.
break · 10 min
Block 3

The seven equations

10:25 · 40 min

Points

  • Frame before listing: these are not climate equations. They are conservation laws that would be as true in a bathtub. The climate is what emerges when you solve them on a rotating sphere heated unevenly.
  • Go through the seven as three conservation laws and their consequences: momentum (three equations, one per direction), mass (continuity, plus a second one for water), energy (first law), and a closure (equation of state).
  • Count the unknowns against the equations out loud. Seven equations, seven unknowns — u, v, w, T, q, p, ρ. It closes. That is the whole reason there are exactly seven.
  • Spend the time on the total derivative if you spend it anywhere. Following the parcel versus watching a fixed point is the single conceptual step that trips people, and it is the reason advection appears at all.
Show
Page 3 — open the equations in order from the selector at the top. Two of the eight repay a live demonstration: the total derivative, where you set a wind and a temperature gradient and watch the station thermometer move in the opposite direction to the parcel; and the standard approximations, whose scale-analysis bar chart shows gravity sitting eight decades above the vertical acceleration. Point at the rest. Each screen also carries three questions with worked solutions behind a button, so it doubles as the reading you set afterwards.
Ask
Which of the seven is the one a model has to approximate hardest? The balance for water vapour, because condensation happens at scales far below the grid and releases the latent heat that drives most of the circulation. This is the hinge between the exact half and the parameterised half, and it is why cloud schemes dominate model spread.
If you are running short
Show the list, name what each conserves, do the total derivative, and set the page as reading. The equations are examinable as a list with meanings, not as derivations.
Block 4

Components and coupling

11:05 · 50 min

1 · Four components and a coupler

  • Click through all four on the diagram. The column to keep coming back to is memory: days, millennia, weeks, and — for the land — all of them at once.
  • The point to land: the ocean has a thousand times the heat capacity of the atmosphere, so it sets the pace of the coupled system and is the reason committed warming exists.
  • The coupler is not a formality: different grids, different time steps, and heat and water must be conserved exactly or the model drifts. Mention flux adjustment and that it was abandoned in the 2000s — a concrete, checkable sign that models improved.

2 · Ocean — the three differences that matter

  • Salinity instead of humidity, and it does not condense. Non-linear equation of state, so cold fresh water can float on warm salty water — which is what lets the overturning have more than one stable state.
  • Heated and stirred from above, so stably stratified almost everywhere; deep water forms in a few small places.
  • Parameterisation is harder than in the atmosphere: the Rossby radius is tens of kilometres, so a 1° ocean resolves no eddies at all, and deep convection is represented by re-sorting the water column.

3 · Sea ice — run the growth demo

  • Thermodynamics and dynamics, and say why both are needed: thermodynamics alone gives smooth level ice; dynamics gives ridges and leads, and a lead in midwinter loses heat a hundred times faster than the ice beside it.
  • Run Arctic today → equilibrium about 1.6 m. Then No snow at all → about 2.9 m. Let them react before explaining.
  • The point to land: ice growth is self-limiting. The ice a model has already grown is the insulation standing between the cold air and the water it is trying to freeze. That is why sea ice has an equilibrium thickness at all.
  • Then drag the temperature to about −7 °C and watch the summer minimum go to zero. Note explicitly that there is no ice–albedo feedback in this demo — and ask what adding it would do.
Ask
Snow is white, so removing it should warm the surface and thin the ice. The demo says the opposite. Who is right? Both, in different seasons. In winter insulation wins outright — there is no sunlight to reflect. In summer albedo wins. So the answer depends on the seasonal timing of snowfall, which is a precipitation field, which is the least reliable thing a model produces at high latitudes. Two well-understood processes, and the competition between them is settled by a poorly-constrained third. That is a general shape worth naming.

4 · Land surface — from bucket to biosphere

  • The land component exists to return four numbers: reflected shortwave, emitted longwave, sensible heat, latent heat. Everything in it serves those four.
  • The bucket, in one line: dw/dt = P − ET − R. Say explicitly that they have already run it — it is the soil water balance from the Lecture 4 water page.
  • Then what a modern model adds, and why each addition exists: layers so the surface can dry while roots stay wet; a canopy so the atmosphere sees leaves; stomata, which is what couples water to carbon; snow; and vegetation dynamics, which is the step that turns a GCM into an ESM.
Ask
Bucket and full land model, same rain, same radiation, European summer. Where do they disagree most? In the timing of the switch from energy-limited to water-limited evaporation, and so in the peak temperature of a drought. A layered model with roots keeps drawing water for weeks longer. Getting this wrong is a known cause of models over-predicting mid-latitude summer extremes — and it is a direct link back to Lecture 3.

5 · Close the session

  • Recap in three sentences: what a model is, how complex to make it, what is inside it. Say that Monday answers the two questions left over — how do I get from a grid box to a place, and is any of this right.
  • Set page 2 as reading before the afternoon lab, since Lab 1 is a 0-D EBM with feedback.

Session B — Monday 14 September, 10:15–12:00

Block 5

From a grid box to a place

10:15 · 35 min

1 · Reconnect, then the cross-section

  • One-minute recall of Thursday’s parked question: the model warms the box containing Bø by 3 K. What did it tell you about the valley?
  • Open the downscaling demo at 200 km, correction None. Read the three numbers out: valley bias, summit bias, and then the domain mean.
  • The point to land, and it is the one to make sure lands: the domain mean bias is essentially zero while every point in the domain is several degrees wrong. Any evaluation that only looks at area averages will call this model excellent.

2 · The two ways out

  • Dynamical (RCM): nest a finer model inside, solve the physics on a better orography. Costs a hundred times more, covers one domain.
  • Statistical: Tloc = asd + bsd TGCM, fitted per station on a period where you have both. Cheap, works well for temperature, applies to every station you have records for.
  • Switch the demo to Lapse-rate correction, then Statistical downscaling. Make the point that the statistical mode leaves the field untouched and repairs only the two stations — that is not a defect of the demo, it is what the method is.
  • Then the limit: at 200 km the model gives nearly the same temperature to summit and valley, so two places the model cannot tell apart cannot be given different answers by any function of the model output alone.
  • Drag Γ and note how completely the lapse-rate correction depends on a number you assumed — and that on a clear winter night in a Norwegian valley the lapse rate has the wrong sign entirely.
Ask — the one that matters for their own work
A 12 km regional model is nested in a GCM whose storm track is 400 km too far south. What does the regional model do about it? Nothing. It takes the GCM at its boundaries and reproduces the wrong storm track at magnificent resolution. Downscaling adds detail, not skill. This is exactly why the evaluation in the next block is done on the global model first — and it is worth saying that a great many published impact studies quietly forget it.
Block 6

Evaluation, metrics and bias

10:50 · 55 min

1 · Three words, three different questions

  • Verification — does the code solve the equations it claims to? Mathematics. Settleable.
  • Validation — are the results close enough to reality for the purpose? Never complete. Say that phrase and let it sit: passing every test you thought of is not the same as being right.
  • Calibration — what values should the free parameters take? Not a test at all. It is an adjustment, and it can be used to hide a failed validation.
  • The rule that survives: tune against far fewer quantities than you intend to evaluate, and never tune against the thing you are trying to predict.
Ask
A model is tuned so its top-of-atmosphere radiation balance matches observations to 0.1 W m−2. Is its global mean temperature then evidence that the model is good? Only if temperature was not itself part of what was tuned — and it usually is, indirectly, through the cloud parameters used to close the radiation balance. The honest move is to state the tuning targets and then evaluate against something else: the seasonal cycle, a volcanic eruption, the last glacial maximum. Those were not tuned, so agreement means something.

2 · Metrics — bias and RMSE on the board

  • Write both. Then the punchline: bias can be zero when everything is wrong — too warm in the tropics, equally too cold at the poles. This is the normal situation, not a hypothetical.
  • Which is why RMSE is reported alongside, and why the standard IPCC figure is a map rather than a number.

3 · The ensemble demonstration — protect the time for this

  • Open with 12 models, defaults. Read the three numbers: multi-model mean 0.8 K, typical member 1.4 K, best single model 0.7 K.
  • Ask why the mean beats nearly everything before explaining. Let someone get to cancellation.
  • Then the crucial manipulation. Set shared structural error to zero and drag the ensemble size to 24 — the mean converges on truth. Then set it back to 2 K and do it again — it stops on the orange line and stays there.
  • The point to land: independent errors cancel as 1/√N. Shared errors do not cancel at all. Averaging fifty copies of the same mistake gives you the mistake.
  • Then the consequence they should carry away: ensemble spread is not an uncertainty estimate. It measures how much models disagree, not how wrong they all are — and CMIP models share components, schemes and lineage, so their errors are correlated by construction.
  • Note when a single member beats the mean, if it does. It happens, and it is a trap: you can only identify which one after comparing with observations, and being closest today guarantees nothing under a different climate.
Show
Page 5, then the two AR5 maps from the slides. The pair on the slide is exactly the “multi-model mean” and “typical member” numbers in the demo — the gap between the two maps is the amount of error that cancels, and the places where they are equal are where every model is wrong the same way.

4 · MIPs, and where models are weak

  • Why standard experiments exist at all: so that differences between results are differences between models. Name piControl, historical, abrupt-4×CO2, the scenarios.
  • Point out that the scenario row is the only one that cannot be validated, and that this is why all the others matter.
  • The skill bar chart: temperature good, precipitation poor, sea ice worst. The ordering is not accidental — temperature is constrained by an energy balance solved from first principles; precipitation is produced almost entirely by parameterised convection.
Ask — aim this one at the ecologists in the room
For a species range-shift study, which error matters most? Precipitation and its seasonality — water availability sets range limits over most of the world, and it is exactly the field models are worst at. The practical consequence: use anomalies, not absolute values. Add the simulated change to an observed climatology. That is the delta method, and it is why it is standard in impact work.

5 · Bias correction — end on the uncomfortable one

  • Run the demo in order: Raw → Constant offset → Offset + drift → + trend.
  • Draw attention to the raw case first: the model is 2.5 K too warm and warms far too slowly, and yet its 2100 value looks almost right. That is luck, not skill — two errors of opposite sign partly cancelling.
  • Then the shock: correcting the offset, which is unambiguously the right thing to do, makes the 2100 number look worse. Compensating errors. Tie it straight back to the calibration warning at the start of the block.
  • Write the delta method on the board and read it aloud as English: keep the model’s change, throw away its absolute value. Its only assumption is that the bias is constant in time.
  • Then break that assumption out loud: a model 2 K too warm because it loses snow a month early will have a smaller bias in a warmer world where the real snow has gone too. The reference period cannot tell you this. The assumption is untestable within the data used to make it — which is the recurring shape of every problem on this page.
  • The trend correction last, and be visibly uneasy about it: it adjusts the model’s response to forcing, which is the thing you built the model to predict, using thirty years of data applied over a hundred and fifty.
Ask
Why is it legitimate to subtract a drift measured in a control run, when subtracting trends generally is not? Because the control run has constant forcing by design, so a long-term trend in it cannot be physical — there is nothing for it to be a response to. In a forced run the trend is what you are measuring. That distinction is the whole reason piControl is a required CMIP experiment.
Close

Six points, and the bridge forward

11:45 · 15 min

The six from the slides, in the students’ own words if possible

  • EBMs, EMICs and GCMs exist and are complementary.
  • An EBM is the energy balance of the Earth, and fits on one line.
  • A GCM describes component interactions in three dimensions, limited by resolution and parameterisation.
  • EMICs sit between, and exist because some questions take ten thousand years.
  • Models must be evaluated against past and present, and validation is never complete.
  • Bias-correction techniques exist, all resting on assumptions that cannot be tested with the data used to build them.

Then the sentence to finish on

  • A climate model’s value is not that it is right — it is not — but that it is explicit. Every assumption is written down and can be changed, and the consequence of changing it can be measured. That is the difference between a model and an opinion.
  • Point at the two numbers still on the board from Thursday: 1.1 and 3. Tomorrow’s lecture is entirely about the gap between them.
  • PC Lab 2 this afternoon is the 1-D EBM from Block 2. Tell them to expect the ice line and to expect it to be irreversible.
