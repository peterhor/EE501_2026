Present-day insolation at the top of the atmosphere

1361 W/m² is what arrives on a surface held face-on to the Sun. Almost nowhere on Earth is a surface held face-on. Everything below is the geometry that turns that one number into the pattern of energy the planet actually receives.

QTOA = S₀ · (d̄/d)² · cos ζ   →   S₀ = 1361 W/m²
S₀ the solar constant at 1 AU · (d̄/d)² the distance factor over the year (±3.4 %) · ζ the solar zenith angle. Only cos ζ varies across the planet — and it does all the work.

1 · The cosine law — why angle is everything

A beam of fixed cross-section carries fixed power. Tilt the surface it lands on and the same power is smeared over a larger footprint, so the flux per square metre drops by cos ζ.

The counter-intuitive one. At the June solstice the North Pole receives the highest daily-mean insolation anywhere on Earth — about 542 W/m², more than the equator ever gets. The Sun sits low (ζ = 66.6°, a weak cos ζ) but it never sets, so 24 hours of weak beats 12 hours of strong. Latitude alone does not decide insolation; latitude × day length does.

3 · From 1361 to 340

Averaged over the whole globe and the whole day, the geometry costs exactly a factor of 4: the Earth intercepts sunlight on a disc of area πR² and spreads it over a sphere of area 4πR².

Q̄global = S₀ · πR² / 4πR² = S₀ / 4 = 340 W/m² this is the number every global energy budget starts from — before any albedo is subtracted

And the distance factor. Earth is at perihelion in early January and aphelion in early July, so S₀ at the top of the atmosphere swings between 1408 and 1317 W/m² — ±3.4 % about the mean. Southern-hemisphere summer therefore receives about 7 % more sunlight than northern-hemisphere summer. On present-day Earth this is a small effect next to the 23.44° tilt, but it is the same knob Milankovitch turns.

S₀ = 1361 W/m² · obliquity 23.44° · eccentricity 0.0167 · declination approximated as δ = −23.44° · cos(2π(N+10)/365.25), accurate to about half a degree · day length from the sunrise hour angle h₀ = arccos(−tan φ tan δ), neglecting refraction and the Sun's finite disc · daily mean Q̄ = (S₀/π)(h₀ sin φ sin δ + cos φ cos δ sin h₀), with the distance factor set to 1.
