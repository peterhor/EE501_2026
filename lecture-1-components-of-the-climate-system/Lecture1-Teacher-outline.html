Components of the climate system — lecture outline

A running order for the Monday 10:15–12:00 slot and the first half of the 12:15–16:00 slot, with the point to make at each step, the number to put on the board, which interactive page to open, and the question to throw back at the room. Built from Lecture 1 — Components of the Climate System (117 slides) and the seven teaching pages that go with it.

The one sentence the whole session is built on

The climate system is five components with five different clocks, and almost everything that is confusing about climate comes from the fact that they run at wildly different speeds.

Say it at the start and land it five times: the atmosphere responds in weeks, the mixed layer in years, the deep ocean in centuries, the ice sheets in millennia, the rock in a million years. Every component in this lecture gets the same four questions asked of it — what is in it, how much can it hold, how fast does it respond, what does it do to its neighbours — and if you keep asking those four in that order the lecture has a spine instead of being a tour.

The second thread, worth naming explicitly once. This is the only lecture in the course that is descriptive. Everything after it is an explanation of something described here. Say that out loud — students who think Lecture 1 is the boring inventory before the real physics will not connect Lecture 6's ice–albedo feedback to the albedo table they saw on day one. Every time you name a property, say which later lecture picks it up. The public pages already carry those cross-links; use them.

Where the time actually goes. This is a large lecture with 117 slides behind it and it will not fit unless you are ruthless. Blocks 1, 2 and 5 are the examinable core and must not be rushed — the definition of climate, the timescale argument, and the circulation. Block 7 (the cryosphere) is the one to compress if you are behind; it is largely inventory and the public page carries it well for self-study. Do not sacrifice the breakout discussion in Block 9 — it is the only point where the ecology half of the course meets the physics half in the same room.

Before you walk in, check three numbers. The slides quote CO2 and CH4 for the current year and the latest global temperature ranking, and all three go stale every January. Check gml.noaa.gov and the current Berkeley Earth or WMO annual statement. The public page The atmosphere carries the same values and needs updating in the same pass.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the session students should be able to:

  • State both definitions of climate and say what each one is for.
  • Explain what a 30-year normal estimates, and why a normal has an expiry date.
  • Explain why an anomaly is meaningless without its baseline.
  • Name the five components and give an order-of-magnitude response time for each.
  • Use τ = C/λ and explain why the ocean has more than one heat capacity.
  • State the composition of dry air and say which 0.1 % matters and why.
  • Derive hydrostatic balance and the exponential pressure profile.
  • Explain why humid air is lighter, and use the virtual temperature.
  • Compare an environmental lapse rate against Γd and Γm and classify the stability.
  • Explain why the troposphere cools with height and the stratosphere warms.
  • Compute f = 2Ωsinφ and explain the deflection it produces.
  • State geostrophic balance and read a wind speed off an isobar spacing.
  • Explain the three-cell structure and place the world's deserts on it.
  • Explain a monsoon from land–sea heat capacity.
  • State the Ekman result — 90° to the right — and derive upwelling from it.
  • Explain why salinity takes over from temperature as the density control in polar water.
  • Name the deep water masses and explain the ordering of the deep ocean.
  • Give the sea-level equivalent of the ice sheets and explain why floating ice contributes nothing.
  • Explain the latent-heat buffer and why ice sheets are slow.
  • List the six surface properties vegetation controls, and give the sign of the boreal albedo feedback.
  • Explain why climate zones form latitudinal bands and name three exceptions.

Block 1

What climate actually means

10:30 · 30 min

1 · The definition, taken apart

  • Read the traditional definition off the slide: a description, in terms of the mean and the variability, of relevant atmospheric variables. Then say which word students always drop: variability.
  • Weather is a state; climate is a distribution. Write both words on the board and leave them there for the hour.
  • Then the wider definition — the whole system — and say explicitly that the shift from one to the other is why this lecture exists.
Show
The weather-versus-climate video from the deck (youtu.be/lrPS2HiYVp8) if you want a break in voice, but it is cuttable — the demonstration below does the same work with more rigour.

2 · Where thirty years comes from — this is the piece worth protecting

  • Ask first: “Why thirty years? Why not five, or a hundred?” Take answers. Most rooms will say “to average out the weather” and stop there.
  • Then give the other half: a long window is out of date at the far end. Two errors, pulling opposite ways — sampling error σ/√N falling, staleness ½·trend·N rising.
  • Now run the demonstration and let the room watch the minimum move.
  • The uncomfortable result, and do not soften it: at a trend of 0.07 K/decade the optimum is about 25 years, so thirty is a good number for the climate of the 1930s. At today's 0.2 K/decade it collapses to about 12 years. By its own criterion the standard normal is roughly twice as long as it should be, and it survives because comparability between countries is worth more than the extra tenth of a degree.
  • Be honest about the status of the argument: the 30-year convention was fixed in the 1930s for standardisation, not by anyone minimising this expression. This is a reconstruction of why it is a reasonable number, not the historical reason.
Show
From weather to climate — “Average some weather”. Press the climate 30 years was chosen for, then the normal we use, the climate we have, and let the optimum jump. Ninety seconds; do not narrate the sliders.
Ask
Set the trend to zero. Why does the best window run off the end of the slider? Because with no trend nothing goes stale, so every extra year purely buys precision. A finite window only makes sense because the thing being estimated is itself moving. A climate normal is therefore not an estimate of the climate — it is an estimate of the climate of a period, and it expires.

3 · The baseline trap

  • Put the NASA-style figure from the slides up and point at the six different reference periods marked on it. Then say: the curve never moves; the axis does.
  • Numbers to have ready: +1.09 °C for 2011–2020 vs 1850–1900 and +0.85 °C for 1995–2014 (IPCC AR6 SPM A.1.2). Their difference, 0.24 °C, is what a change of baseline is worth.
  • Then the current year from Berkeley Earth — check this before the lecture.
  • Land the Norwegian example: Telemark +4.0 °C and +10 % precipitation, 1971–2000 to 2071–2100. Then the line that matters for this audience: the day length does not change. A Telemark with central European temperatures still has midwinter darkness, and no ecosystem on Earth is adapted to that combination. That is why the ecology half of this course is not a footnote.
Show
Same year, six different anomalies — click through the four baselines and read the final-year number changing. Fifteen seconds each.
Block 2

Five components, five clocks

11:00 · 25 min

1 · Name the five, then immediately weigh them

  • Atmosphere, hydrosphere, cryosphere, biosphere, land surface. Write them on the board in a pentagon and draw arrows between them as you go — you will refer back to it all afternoon.
  • Do the mass comparison out loud: the ocean outweighs the atmosphere by 270×, and water holds four times more heat per kilogram. The biosphere is seven orders of magnitude lighter than the ocean and runs the carbon cycle. Mass is not importance — but it does set the clock.
Ask
The classic: how deep a layer of ocean has the same heat capacity as the whole atmosphere? About 3 metres. Let them guess first — the guesses are usually in hundreds of metres. This single number does more work in this course than any other, and it is worth making them wrong before you make them right.

2 · C, λ and τ

  • C = ρ cp d — and the judgement is all in d: which depth actually participates. Seasonal ocean means the mixed layer; centennial ocean means the whole thing. The ocean has more than one heat capacity depending on how long you wait.
  • C dT/dt = ΔF − λT ⇒ τ = C/λ. Flag it: this is PC Lab 2, and it is the fast layer of the two-layer model in Lecture 6.
  • Run the demonstration and read four numbers off it: atmosphere ~3 months, mixed layer ~5 yr, whole ocean ~260 yr as a lower bound.
Show
Five clocks on one axis. Note the log time axis out loud — students misread it otherwise.
Ask
Set λ to 3.2 (Planck only). Every τ shortens. Why should a feedback change how long the system takes, rather than only how far it goes? τ = C/λ, and λ is the rate at which the system sheds the extra energy. A weak restoring feedback means the imbalance closes slowly. A more sensitive climate is also a slower one — genuinely counter-intuitive, examinable, and the reason high-ECS models do not necessarily warm faster in the next thirty years.
Ask
The whole-ocean row says centuries. The standard statement is millennia. Where does the calculation go wrong? It assumes the column is well mixed. It is not — the heat capacity is real but the access to it is the bottleneck. This is exactly why models use two layers with a transfer coefficient rather than one deep slab.

3 · The interaction matrix, and the question to leave hanging

  • Open the matrix and click two or three cells — not all twenty-five. Suggested three: Cry → Atm (albedo and insulation), Hyd → Atm (86 % of evaporation, 50× the atmosphere's carbon), Bio → Atm (a quarter of emissions taken up).
  • Point out that the matrix is not symmetric, and that most couplings route through the atmosphere — the ice sheet does not talk to the rainforest directly.
  • Then the slide question from Ruddiman: “Where would you put human activities in this system?” Take answers, do not settle it, and say you will come back to it at the end.
Show
The interaction matrix.

Block 3

The atmosphere: what it is made of, and why it does not fall down

11:35 · 25 min

1 · Composition, and the two questions from the slides

  • Ask both slide questions cold, before any table: “What are the main constituents of the atmosphere?” and “What is the CO2 concentration today?” The second reliably produces guesses spanning three orders of magnitude, which is the point.
  • Then the numbers: N2 78.08, O2 20.95, Ar 0.93 %. CO2 ~429 ppm, CH4 ~1937 ppb (2026, NOAA GML — update annually).
  • The point to land: the first three rows are 99.96 % of dry air and are climatically inert. Every radiative effect in this course is done by well under a tenth of one percent. The reason is molecular: only molecules with three or more atoms have the modes that absorb terrestrial infrared. Say it once here; Lecture 2 draws the bands.
  • Then the “snapshot in time” slide — composition is not a constant, and the part that changes is precisely the part that matters.
Show
The atmosphere — composition table. Highlighted rows are the radiatively active ones.

2 · The gas law, and the humid-air question

  • pV = nRT → p = ρRgT, Rg = 287.0 for dry air. Say once that Boyle, Charles, Gay-Lussac and Avogadro are all this one equation with something held fixed. Do not spend four slides on them.
  • Then the question that catches everyone. Ask it and wait: “Is humid air heavier or lighter than dry air at the same temperature and pressure?” Most of the room will say heavier.
  • Answer: lighter. Water is 18 g/mol, average dry air is 29. At fixed T and p the number of molecules per unit volume is fixed (Avogadro), so adding vapour replaces heavy molecules with light ones. Hence moist air is buoyant, hence humidity and low pressure go together.
  • Introduce Tv = T(1 + 0.608q) as the bookkeeping device, up to 4 K in the tropics, and move on.
  • Show the saturation curve. The one sentence: es roughly doubles every 10 K, which is the water-vapour feedback and Clausius–Clapeyron in Lectures 6 and 8.
Show
Saturation, dewpoint and the density of humid air. Drag the temperature slider once across the whole range and let the exponential do the talking; the density readout answers the question you just asked. The Khan Academy gas-law clip from the deck (youtu.be/qObcdZj8YTM) is a fallback if the room is struggling, but it costs five minutes you do not have.

3 · Hydrostatic balance — derive this on the board

  • Three forces on a slab: Fbottom = pbA, Ftop = −ptA, Fg = −ρAΔz g. Set the sum to zero.
  • Result: ∂p/∂z = −ρg — the pressure drop across any layer is the weight of the air in it. Say that sentence; it is the whole equation in English.
  • Say what each force prevents. No pressure gradient → the atmosphere collapses into a thin dense layer. No gravity → it escapes to space. What we have is the standoff, and it is exact to about one part in a million in a weather system — which is why models simply assume it.
  • Eliminate ρ with the gas law and integrate: p ≈ ps e−z/H, H = RgT/g = 7–8 km. One sentence for why it is exponential: each layer supports the weight of everything above it, and anything whose rate of decrease is proportional to what is left decays exponentially.
Ask
How high do you have to go to leave half the atmosphere below you? 5.5 km — lower than most of the Alps. Let them guess first; guesses cluster around 30–50 km. It is the single most effective way to make the thinness of the atmosphere real, and you will use it again in Lecture 2 when the emission level turns out to be at about the same height.

Block 4

Vertical structure and stability

12:15 · 35 min

1 · The profile, and the layers

  • Run the profile demonstration. Landmarks to point at: half the mass below 5.5 km, Everest 314 hPa in the standard atmosphere, cruise altitude 226 hPa.
  • Worth mentioning because it is a nice honesty note: climbers measure closer to 335 hPa on Everest, because the real subtropical troposphere is warmer and deeper than the mid-latitude standard. A 7 % difference, and a non-trivial amount of oxygen at 8848 m.
  • Four layers, and two heating mechanisms: troposphere heated from below so it convects and cools with height; stratosphere heated from within by ozone absorbing UV, so it warms with height and does not convect. Every turning point in the profile marks a change in where the energy comes from.
  • Tropopause height with latitude: 16–18 km equator, 8–9 pole, and the counter-intuitive bit — the tropical tropopause is colder (~190 K) despite a surface 50 K warmer, because the air had 8 km further to rise. That cold trap is what keeps the stratosphere dry.
Show
Pressure, density and temperature with height. Set Γ to 0 and back to show what an isothermal atmosphere would look like.
Ask
Why does a colder upper atmosphere mean less pressure up there? H = RgT/g. Cold air is dense, a dense layer is physically thinner for the same mass, so pressure surfaces are squeezed down. This is why the 500 hPa surface is at 5.0 km over the winter pole and 5.9 km over the tropics, and why upper-air maps are drawn as height of a pressure surface — those contours are a temperature map of the air below.

2 · Stability — the answer to the slide question about moist air

  • Γd = g/cp = 9.8 K km−1. A constant, everywhere on Earth. Emphasise: the parcel cools without losing heat to anything — it did work expanding.
  • Then the slide's question: “Why does moist air cool more slowly with height?” Condensation releases 2.5 MJ per kilogram back into the parcel. Hence Γm, about 4–5 K/km in warm moist air and tending to Γd when it is too cold to hold water.
  • Three regimes, and spend most of the time on the middle one: absolutely unstable (Γenv > 9.8, survives only in the lowest metres over hot ground); absolutely stable (Γenv < Γm); and conditionally unstable in between, which is most of the troposphere most of the time. That is why forecasting convection is hard and why humidity matters so much.
  • Run the four presets in order. They tell a story on their own.
Show
Launch a parcel. Watch the right-hand panel, not the left — the buoyancy is a couple of kelvin on a hundred-kelvin axis and is invisible in the temperature plot. Presets in order: average conditions → summer afternoon before a storm → winter inversion → hot and bone dry.
Ask
Press “a winter inversion”. Why is this a public health problem in Norwegian valleys? Nothing rises, so nothing mixes. Wood smoke, exhaust and road dust accumulate at the surface for as long as the inversion lasts — days, in a sheltered valley under clear winter skies. Good local hook, and it sets up Lecture 3: the reason inversions form is the surface energy balance on a clear calm night.
Ask
Press “hot and bone dry”. The parcel is buoyant but no cloud forms. What forms instead? Dry convection — thermals, dust devils, a deep turbulent boundary layer, cloudless sky. The condensation level is above the level where buoyancy runs out. This is the normal daytime state over a desert, and it is why deserts have enormous diurnal ranges: no latent heat released, no cloud to slow the nocturnal cooling.
Block 5

Circulation: Coriolis, geostrophy, three cells, monsoon

12:50 · 40 min

1 · Set up the problem before solving it

  • Tropics receive more than they emit; poles emit more than they receive. Something must carry heat poleward. On a non-rotating Earth: one cell per hemisphere, surface wind everywhere from pole to equator. Draw it. Then say: the Earth does not do this, and the reason is that it spins.
  • Convection at five scales — thermal, cumulonimbus, sea breeze, monsoon, Hadley cell — and the thing that separates them is whether rotation has time to act. Quarter of a pendulum day, a few hours in mid-latitudes.

2 · Coriolis

  • Merry-go-round first, equation second. f = 2Ωsinφ. Zero at the equator, maximal at the poles, opposite sign in the south.
  • Run the trajectory demonstration and drag the latitude down to 0. Then land the consequence: hurricanes never form within about 5° of the equator, and none has been observed to cross it, because with f = 0 there is nothing to turn the inflow and any low simply fills in.
  • The deck's Norwegian slide about surface speed — 40 000 km/day at the equator against much less at 60° — is the same statement without vectors. The demonstration computes the number for whatever latitude you set; use that rather than quoting the slide.
Show
Fire a parcel and watch it bend.
Ask
Does the Coriolis force determine which way water spins down a bathtub drain? No, and make them estimate rather than assert. U ~ 0.1 m/s over 0.5 m gives 5 s against an inertial period of ~17 hours: the deflection is of order a hundredth of a millimetre. Formally, Rossby number ~2000 for the bath and ~0.2 for a depression. This question is worth the two minutes — it is the first time in the course they scale an equation to decide whether a term matters, which is the single most transferable skill in the subject.

3 · Geostrophic balance

  • ug = |∇p| / (ρf). Air pushed from high to low, turned by Coriolis until the two oppose, at which point it flows along the isobars.
  • Two things to say aloud: ug ∝ 1/f, so the same gradient gives a stronger wind at low latitude; and wind speed can be read off a weather map quantitatively, which is why forecasters draw isobars at all.
  • Then turn the friction slider up and follow it through to the conclusion: the wind crosses the isobars inward, mass converges at the centre, it cannot go down, so it goes up, so it rains. Friction is why lows are wet.
Show
Balance the forces. Default 3 hPa/100 km at 45°N gives 23.7 m/s — put that number on the board.

4 · Three cells, and where the deserts are

  • Walk the schematic once: rising at 0 and 60, sinking at 30 and 90; trades, westerlies, polar easterlies; the two jets at the tropopause breaks.
  • The payoff, and make them produce it: ask why the Sahara is a desert. Answer: it sits under the descending branch. Air that rose at the equator, rained out there, and travelled north aloft is now sinking, compressing and warming, so relative humidity collapses. The dryness comes first; the heat and sunshine are consequences. Flag forward to Lecture 2: the Sahara is actually a net radiative deficit at the top of the atmosphere.
  • If time allows, the honest caveat about the Ferrel cell: it is indirect and exists only as a residual of the zonal average. Real mid-latitude heat transport is done by travelling depressions, not by an overturning cell. This is worth 60 seconds and it prevents a persistent misconception.
Show
Three cells, not one. Then show the observed sea-level pressure and wind fields (Figs. 1.6–1.7) from the deck — those are not reproduced on the public page, by design, so this is one of the places the slides carry something the site does not.

5 · The monsoon, in one demonstration

  • Say the connection explicitly: this is the heat-capacity argument from Block 2, used to make a wind reverse.
  • Land swings ±20 K and lags the sun by days; the ocean swings ±2.4 K and lags by ~80 days. The contrast reverses sign twice a year, and that sign change is the monsoon.
  • Summer: land hot, air rises, pressure falls, moist air drawn in from the ocean. Winter: the reverse, dry and offshore. The ITCZ is dragged along with the rising branch, which is why the rain arrives with the wind.
  • Close with the scale: Cherrapunji 11 600 mm/yr, and 2493 mm in 48 hours on 15–16 June 1995, against Bø at 790 mm/yr. Three years of Telemark's rain in two days. That comparison lands better than any map.
Show
Drive a continent and an ocean with the same sun. The monsoon clip from the deck (youtu.be/1nstixjMS7A) is a good closer for this block if you are on time, and the first thing to cut if you are not.

Block 6

The ocean: wind on top, density underneath

13:45 · 40 min

1 · Frame it as two drivers, and keep them separate

  • Write on the board: wind (friction) and density (T and S). Everything in this block hangs on one or the other. Students who conflate them cannot explain why the Gulf Stream and the overturning are different things.
  • The numbers: 71 % of the surface, 3700 m mean depth, 96.5 % water, ~34.7 psu. One sverdrup = 106 m3/s ≈ 100 Amazons. Gulf Stream ~150 Sv, AMOC ~17 Sv, ACC ~135 Sv.
  • Gyres, and western intensification — assert it, do not derive it. Say it is a consequence of f increasing with latitude and that it is why northwest Europe has the climate it has.

2 · Ekman — the best fifteen minutes in the block

  • Tell the Nansen story: sea ice on the Fram drifted 20–40° to the right of the wind, Nansen noticed, Ekman explained it in 1905. It makes the result memorable and it is true.
  • Surface current 45° to the right; depth-integrated transport 90° to the right, M = τ/(ρf).
  • The point worth pausing on: drag the eddy viscosity across its whole range. The spiral changes shape completely and the transport does not move at all. The one quantity that matters for the large-scale circulation is the one that does not depend on the turbulence parameter nobody can measure. That is rare and worth pointing at.
  • Then upwelling, in two forms. Coastal: wind along a coast with the land on its left pushes water offshore, and it is replaced from below. Equatorial: transport is to the right north of the equator and to the left south of it, so the surface diverges and deep water rises — no coast required.
  • Why anyone cares: upwelling zones are ~1 % of the ocean surface and support roughly half the world's fish catch. Also cold coastal deserts (Atacama, Namib) and a large natural CO2 source in the equatorial Pacific. Then one sentence on ENSO as the case where the wind, and therefore the upwelling, stops — and flag that Lecture 7 does it properly.
Show
Turn the wind on. Default 10 m/s at 45°N: stress 0.159 N/m², Ekman depth 44 m, surface current 15 cm/s, transport 1.51 m²/s — which over 100 km of coast is 0.15 Sv pulled up from below.
Ask
A northerly wind down the coast of California. Upwelling or downwelling? And on the coast of Portugal? Both upwelling — both are west-facing coasts, so a northerly pushes water offshore in each case. Which is why San Francisco's sea is 12 °C in July and why the Portuguese–Moroccan system is one of the four great eastern-boundary upwelling regions. The rule is not about which continent; it is about the wind relative to the coastline.

3 · Density, and the cabbeling demonstration

  • Cold is denser, salty is denser — but thermal expansion collapses near freezing, so in polar water salinity is in charge. Hence brine rejection under growing sea ice is one of the few processes that makes very dense water quickly.
  • Two independent controls on one quantity is what allows more than one stable state. Say this sentence deliberately; it is the seed of Lecture 7's abrupt change.
  • Run the demonstration. Press two parcels of equal density, mix them, and the mixture is 0.157 kg/m³ denser than either. Nothing added, no heat lost — that is cabbeling, and it exists purely because the equation of state is curved.
  • Answer the deck's question with the freezing line on the diagram: water can be below 0 °C because salt depresses the freezing point, to about −1.9 °C at S = 34.7.
  • Then the water masses: AABW under NADW, AAIW above both as a salinity minimum, Mediterranean water spreading at 1000 m. Be careful here and use the honesty note on the page: at surface reference AABW and NADW come out within 0.003 kg/m³ of each other. The ordering is real but needs pressure, which is why deep water is compared using σ4. If a student catches this, they have caught something worth catching — say so.
Show
Which parcel sinks — and a trap. Then the overturning schematic (Fig. 1.12) from the deck; it is not reproduced on the public page.
Ask
Cool Arctic surface water further and almost nothing happens to its density. Why, and what makes deep water form there instead? Thermal expansion collapses near freezing — visible on the diagram as the contours going nearly horizontal — and the water cannot get colder than its freezing point anyway. What works is salt: brine rejected by growing sea ice, plus evaporation in the Nordic Seas. This is the coupling that puts sea ice and the deep circulation in the same sentence.
Block 7

The cryosphere

14:25 · 25 min compress this one if behind

1 · Two cryospheres, and say so

  • Thin, seasonal, enormous in area — snow and sea ice, doing the radiative work. Thick, permanent, enormous in volume — the ice sheets, doing the sea level. Read the area and volume columns against each other and the structure falls out: sea ice and snow cover a comparable area to the ice sheets and contain a thousandth of the ice.
  • Seasonality: Arctic 15 → 4.5 million km², Antarctic 18 → 2–3, NH snow 47 → 2. A factor of three in area, every year, on schedule. No other component does anything like it.
Ask
Why is the Antarctic seasonal swing so much larger than the Arctic one? Geography. The Arctic Ocean is an enclosed basin, so winter ice has nowhere to grow beyond the coastlines and much survives as thick multi-year ice. Antarctic ice grows outward into an open Southern Ocean with no boundary, then melts almost completely in a warm stormy summer. Antarctic ice is thin, first-year and seasonal; Arctic ice is not.

2 · Sea level, and the two numbers that differ

  • Antarctica 58.3 m, Greenland 7.4, glaciers 0.32. Floating ice: essentially zero.
  • Do the Archimedes question properly — and then the refinement, because it separates the people who understand it from the people who remember the slogan: melting all floating ice raises sea level by of order 4 cm, because sea ice is fresher than the water it displaces.
  • Then the naive-versus-published gap: the calculation gives 65 m for Antarctica, the assessed figure is 58.3. The missing seven metres is ice grounded below sea level, already displacing water. Flag forward: that same fact is the marine ice-sheet instability in Lecture 8.
Show
Melt some ice. Toggle the floating-ice button on and off and let the total barely move.

3 · The latent-heat buffer, and permafrost

  • Lf = 3.34×105 J/kg against 2100 J/kg/K for warming ice. As long as ice and water coexist the temperature does not move.
  • The comparison to make out loud: Greenland is 9×1023 J; the entire planetary imbalance delivers 1.4×1022 J per year. Even with every joule pointed at Greenland it would take several decades; at the ~4 % that actually goes into ice, 1.5 kyr. Ice sheets are slow because the energy is enormous, not because the physics is slow.
  • Immediately give the counterweight or you have taught complacency: ice does not have to melt in place, it can flow. The energy argument bounds the thermodynamic route only, and the dynamic route is where the deep uncertainty lives.
  • Permafrost, in two sentences: ~15 million km², and 1400–1600 Pg C in the top three metres — roughly twice the atmosphere. It is a feedback, not an emission, which is why it is subtracted from the carbon budget rather than added to emissions.
Show
Put the planetary energy imbalance against the ice.
Block 8

The land surface — the boundary conditions the atmosphere actually sees

14:50 · 22 min

0 · Settle the name first, in thirty seconds

  • The deck contradicts itself and the students will notice. Slide 16 lists four components and omits the land surface; slide 117 lists five and calls the fifth one pedosphere. Goosse calls it the land surface, and never uses the word pedosphere at all. Say which one you are using and why, once, and move on.
  • The distinction is worth thirty seconds because it is substantive, not cosmetic: pedosphere is strictly the soil mantle; lithosphere or geosphere is the solid Earth including the rock; land surface is the functional set of boundary conditions — albedo, roughness, water storage, topography — which is what a climate model actually needs. The deck's own “pedosphere” slides show a topography map and an albedo map, neither of which is soil.
  • If you are updating the deck, this is the fix: make slide 16 agree with slide 117 and relabel the fifth component land surface.

1 · Six levers, and where each one returns

  • Albedo, roughness, evapotranspiration, water storage, soil carbon, topography. Read the list and name the later lecture for each one — that is the whole purpose of the slide.
  • Note what is missing from the list: temperature. The surface does not impose a temperature; it has properties, and the temperature is what comes out of the energy balance. That distinction is Lecture 3 in one sentence, and it is why a land-cover change is a climate change rather than a response to one.
  • Albedo table: fresh snow 0.75–0.90, coniferous forest 0.05–0.15, ocean 0.05–0.10, planetary mean 0.30. Point out that a conifer forest is almost as dark as open ocean and stays that way all winter.

2 · The albedo experiment that makes the point

  • Run the albedo mixer with the snow on. All tundra gives ~0.75; all forest gives ~0.20. The trees stick up through the snow and hide it.
  • Then the question that connects physics to policy, and let them argue: planting boreal forest warms by albedo and cools by carbon — which wins?
  • Answer: in the boreal zone the albedo effect frequently wins and afforestation can be net warming; in the tropics the reverse is emphatically true, with evapotranspiration cooling on top. Where you plant determines the sign. Carbon-only accounting is the wrong accounting.
  • One more, if there is time: drag the insolation to 40 W/m². The albedo difference is unchanged and the watts nearly vanish — so ice–albedo is a summer feedback, and winter Arctic amplification must be something else (loss of the insulating lid). Two mechanisms, two seasons, one phenomenon.
Show
Build a landscape and read its albedo. The albedo clip from the deck (youtu.be/d1U7X1k3d0Y) covers the same ground more slowly.

3 · Where the rain goes — Budyko in five minutes

  • Frame it as the same logic twice: evaporation cannot exceed the water that arrives, and it cannot exceed the energy available to vaporise it. The lower ceiling wins. That ratio is the dryness index Φ = Rn/(λP).
  • Run the presets in this order: Sahara (Φ ≈ 52, everything evaporates, no runoff) then Amazon (Φ ≈ 0.64, half the rain runs off). Ask why one makes the largest river on Earth. The answer students miss: the fraction is ordinary, the quantity is not.
  • Then Telemark: Φ = 0.57, 382 mm evaporated, 408 mm runoff on 790 mm of rain. A runoff coefficient of 0.52, which is why Norway has the hydropower it does. Good local hook.
  • The sentence that makes it a components lecture: the same curve that predicts river flow predicts how the surface splits its energy between latent and sensible heat, because every millimetre evaporated is 2.45 MJ that did not warm the air. Water and energy at the land surface are the same problem written twice.
Show
Split the precipitation. Budyko's curve has no fitted parameters — worth saying aloud; students assume anything that fits the world must have been tuned to it.
Ask
Deforest a tropical catchment. Which way does runoff move, and is that good? Up, at first — shallower roots and a smoother, brighter surface evaporate less, so more rain leaves sideways. Whether that is good depends on timescale: locally it means more water and more flooding and erosion; regionally and over years it can mean less rainfall, because much of Amazon precipitation is recycled from the forest itself. That is a positive feedback and it is PC Lab 3.
Block 9

The biosphere — where the two halves of the course meet

15:12 · 25 min do not cut this

1 · Open on the mass paradox

  • Put three numbers on the board: ocean 1.4×1021 kg, atmosphere 5.1×1018, biosphere ~2×1015 kg C. Then: marine biota are three petagrams and run a global cycle.
  • The point: a component's importance is set by its flux and by what it controls, not by what it weighs. Land vegetation exchanges about a seventh of the atmosphere's entire carbon content with it every year — which is why the Keeling curve has teeth.
  • This is the block where the ecologists in the room have something the physicists do not. Say so. The first eight lectures of this course were ecological case studies; this is the page that connects them to the physics.

2 · What limits growth, and where

  • Same logic as Budyko, one block earlier: two ceilings, and the lower one binds. Say the connection out loud — plants evaporate water in order to acquire carbon, so the constraint on one really is the constraint on the other.
  • Run the Miami model. The pairing to use: Sahara (49, water-limited) against Arctic tundra (524, temperature-limited). Both unproductive, opposite sensitivity to warming. That is why high latitudes are greening and drylands are not, and it is the single most useful thing a two-parameter model will ever tell you.
  • Then break your own model, deliberately. Press Savannah and raise the temperature: the prediction barely moves, because the water ceiling in the Miami model does not fall as it gets hotter. In reality warming raises evaporative demand and the same 700 mm supports less. Knowing where a model is wrong is worth more than knowing what it outputs — and it justifies the water-balance approach they just saw in Block 8.
Show
What grows here, and what is stopping it. The filled marker is the binding ceiling — point at it.

3 · Breakout discussion — run it properly, five minutes in pairs

  • Put the three slide questions up together and give them five minutes in pairs before taking answers.
  • (a) Are vegetation/climate zones more dependent on latitude or longitude? (b) Are there exceptions? (c) Which two climate parameters define the zones?
  • Then run the Köppen classifier live on whichever place the room names. Presets cover Bø, Bergen, Cherrapunji, Cairo, Manaus and Longyearbyen; Bø comes out Dfb.
  • The intended payoff: the classifier is given nothing but twelve temperatures and twelve precipitation totals and it reproduces the world's biomes. That is the evidence that temperature and precipitation are the controls. Say it explicitly — students see the tool and miss the argument.
  • Exceptions to collect: western Europe versus eastern North America (ocean current and westerlies); Atacama and Namib (coastal upwelling — point back to Block 6); mountains (2 km of altitude doing what 4000 km of latitude does); continental interiors (distance from the sea).
  • If the room is quick, the follow-up on the page is worth asking: the classifier and the productivity model take the same two inputs — what does each add? Categorical and seasonal versus continuous and annual; neither is complete.
Show
Classify a climate. Show the Köppen–Geiger world map (Peel et al. 2007) from the deck afterwards, not before — it is not on the public page, by design.

4 · Carbon and feedbacks, and both signs

  • Two numbers to hold together: living land vegetation ~450 Pg C against an atmosphere of ~890, and soil beneath it holding three to five times more than the plants growing on it. Most of the land's carbon is dead, in the ground, and slow. That is why land-use change is such a large budget term: the trees go quickly and the soil keeps going for decades.
  • Run the feedback list: snow masking (+), CO2 fertilisation (−), soil respiration (+), vegetation–precipitation (+), evaporative cooling (−), fire (both).
  • Both signs are present, and that is the honest summary. The biosphere is not a thermostat and not an amplifier. Where a positive feedback dominates strongly enough the response becomes a threshold — which is PC Labs 1 and 3, both of which they run this week.
Ask
Which of those six are actually observed, and which are mostly model results? Observed: CO2 fertilisation (the land sink has grown roughly in step with emissions; satellites show widespread greening), treeline advance, and fire. Partly observed: soil respiration — well measured at plot scale, genuinely uncertain in its global integral. Mostly model: large-scale vegetation–precipitation coupling, because you cannot run the experiment. Sorting claims into those three boxes is the same habit the provenance appendix trains for figures.

Things that reliably go wrong

  • “Climate is the average of weather.” It is the distribution. Dropping variability is what makes a single hot summer look like evidence, and it is the misconception the whole first block exists to remove.
  • Quoting an anomaly without its baseline. Correct it every single time it happens this week, including in the labs.
  • “Humid air is heavy.” Universal, and worth the two minutes in Block 3.
  • Confusing the adiabatic lapse rate with the environmental one. The parcel and its surroundings are two different things with two different temperature profiles. Say “parcel” and “environment” as separate words, every time.
  • “The Coriolis force pushes things.” It does no work and it is not a force in an inertial frame. It turns, it does not accelerate the speed.
  • “The Sahara is a desert because it is hot and sunny.” Backwards. Subsidence first, dryness second, cloudlessness third, heat fourth.
  • “Ekman transport is in the direction of the wind.” 90° to the right. The surface is 45°. Two different numbers for two different things, and students merge them.
  • “Melting sea ice raises sea level.” Archimedes. But do not let the refinement (~4 cm, because sea ice is fresher) turn into “so it does after all”.
  • Thinking the ice sheets are slow because the physics is slow. They are slow because the energy is enormous — and the dynamic route around that is exactly what makes projections uncertain.
  • Treating the biosphere as a passenger. It is a boundary condition. This is the misconception the whole final block exists to remove, and it is the one that matters most for this audience.
